GLP-1 side effects: timing, common symptoms, and red flags

Current Wegovy and Zepbound labels list gastrointestinal reactions among their most common adverse effects. In Wegovy injection trials, these reactions were most frequent during dosage escalation; Zepbound reports that most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during escalation and decreased over time. This guide separates those label-supported patterns from warning signs that need medical attention. [1] [2]

Mostly gastrointestinal: Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, and abdominal symptoms appear in both labels.
Escalation matters: Both labels connect dosage escalation with gastrointestinal tolerability.
Patterns are not promises: A trend in trials cannot predict what one person will experience.

Educational only. This page explains general patterns. It does not diagnose symptoms, recommend dose changes, or replace advice from your prescriber.

What the labels say about mechanism and GI effects

Wegovy and Zepbound both delay gastric emptying. Their labels also state that semaglutide and tirzepatide decrease calorie intake, likely through appetite effects, and both list gastrointestinal adverse reactions. The labels do not establish one explanation for every symptom. [1] [2]

Both products use stepwise escalation schedules intended to reduce gastrointestinal adverse reactions. The schedule and any dose change remain medication-specific decisions for the prescriber and current label. [1] [2]

The evidence boundary: delayed gastric emptying and appetite effects are label-supported mechanisms, but a symptom log cannot diagnose why a symptom occurred.

The common side effects

The current Wegovy and Zepbound labels list the effects below among adverse reactions observed in their trials. Rates differ by product, dose, trial, and population, so this is not a personal risk estimate or a head-to-head comparison. [1] [2]

Label-listed effect Source context Useful context to record
Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea Common gastrointestinal reactions in both labels. Onset relative to the injection, duration, and whether fluids stay down.
Constipation Listed in both products' adverse-reaction tables. Start date, severity, and any change from the usual pattern.
Abdominal pain, dyspepsia, or reflux Listed gastrointestinal reactions; severe or persistent pain is a different safety question. Location, severity, duration, and associated vomiting or other symptoms.
Fatigue or headache Reported in Wegovy trials; fatigue is also listed for Zepbound. Timing, hydration, food intake, sleep, and other context to discuss.
Injection-site reaction Listed for Zepbound; both labels instruct rotating injection sites. Site, appearance, duration, and whether the reaction is spreading or severe.

What the labels say about timing

Neither cited label provides a universal week-by-week side-effect timeline. The strongest source-supported pattern is tied to escalation, and the two labels describe it differently:

  • Wegovy injection: its trial section says gastrointestinal reactions were most frequently reported during dosage escalation. [1]
  • Zepbound: its label says most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during dosage escalation and decreased over time. [2]
  • For an individual: neither statement guarantees when a symptom will start, how long it will last, or whether it is caused by the medicine.
Why timing is worth recording: a dated log can show whether a symptom began before or after an injection or escalation. It gives a prescriber chronology; it does not establish causation.

Red flags: when to seek medical care

These are not "track and wait" symptoms. The current labels tell patients to contact a healthcare professional promptly for specific serious reactions. Emergency symptoms still require emergency care. This list summarizes label language and is not exhaustive. [1] [2]
  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain, sometimes radiating to the back and with or without nausea or vomiting, is listed as a possible sign of pancreatitis.
  • Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea that does not go away can lead to dehydration and kidney injury; the medication guides tell patients to contact their healthcare provider.
  • Vomiting or being unable to eat or drink, especially while eating very little: Japan’s regulator reported ambulance cases of ketosis and ketoacidosis in people using these drugs to slim down (see below).[3]
  • Severe stomach problems: both medication guides say to tell a healthcare professional about stomach problems that are severe or do not go away. Both labels also report postmarketing ileus, intestinal obstruction, and severe constipation including fecal impaction.
  • Signs of a serious allergic reaction, including swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat or trouble breathing or swallowing, require urgent care.
  • Symptoms of low blood sugar matter especially when these medicines are used with insulin or an insulin secretagogue such as a sulfonylurea, because the labels describe increased risk with those combinations.
  • A neck lump, persistent hoarseness, trouble swallowing, or shortness of breath appears in the labels' counseling language about thyroid tumors. Both products are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2.
  • Before surgery or a procedure using anesthesia or deep sedation, tell the care team you take the medicine. Both labels describe rare postmarketing pulmonary aspiration reports and say evidence is insufficient to define a universal mitigation plan.

Vomiting and not eating: Japan’s October 2026 ketosis alert

The short answer: Japan’s drug regulator, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency (PMDA), warned clinicians on October 30, 2026 that it has several reports of people who used GLP-1 or GIP/GLP-1 drugs to slim down while eating very little, vomited repeatedly, and were taken to hospital by ambulance with ketosis or ketoacidosis. Its instruction to clinicians is to tell patients to stop the drug at once and seek medical care if they vomit or cannot take in food or water.[3]

PMDA’s alert (No. 18, October 2026) says some medical institutions in Japan prescribe these drugs “for aesthetic and slimming purposes” rather than for type 2 diabetes or obesity, and that safety and efficacy for that off-label use “have not been established.” It explains ketosis as a build-up of ketone bodies from extreme dietary restriction that can progress to ketoacidosis, which “may result in disturbed consciousness and life-threatening conditions, requiring urgent treatment.”[3] What the alert reports:[3]

  • Report counts for slimming use (Japan’s adverse-event database, April 2004 to May 2026): 28 gastrointestinal, 17 ketosis or ketoacidosis, 14 low blood sugar and 11 pancreatitis events. Of those 70, Mounjaro (tirzepatide) accounted for 56, including all 17 ketosis events; semaglutide sold as Ozempic or Rybelsus for 12; and liraglutide (Victoza) for 2. Zepbound and Wegovy had 0 in every row. PMDA notes the counts “do not represent the incidence of adverse reactions.”[3]
  • Three ambulance cases, all women in their 20s using the drug for weight control, all recovered. One, with a history of anorexia nervosa, got the drug through an online healthcare service at a BMI of about 18 and was hospitalized with starvation ketosis about two months later at a BMI of about 16. One developed fever, frequent vomiting and could not eat after her second dose and was hospitalized on day 10 with starvation ketoacidosis. One was already cutting carbohydrates, got the drug online without nutrition counselling, and was hospitalized about a month later with starvation ketoacidosis.[3]

Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), whose warning page the alert links, says it notified medical institutions and manufacturers about proper use on June 16, 2026 and asked for that notice to be re-circulated on October 14, 2026 after a ketosis warning from the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity. The page also says person-to-person resale of these drugs online is illegal in Japan, and that harm from beauty or slimming use is very likely not to qualify for Japan’s drug side-effect relief payments.[4]

Limits. These are spontaneous reports from one country. The alert does not say how many people used each product for slimming, so the counts cannot show that one drug is riskier than another. We read PMDA’s English version, which says the Japanese original prevails if they differ, and the MHLW page in Japanese.[3][4]

Our analysis: the US Wegovy and Zepbound medication guides already warn about nausea, vomiting or diarrhea that does not go away and the dehydration it can cause. What these Japanese reports add is that combination in people who were also eating very little, two of whom got the drug online. If you can’t keep food or fluids down, contact a healthcare professional promptly. Side effects can be reported to FDA’s MedWatch program.[1] [2] [3]

What to discuss with your prescriber

The labels support a short, non-prescriptive checklist. Your prescriber and the current product label determine medication-specific decisions.

  • Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms: both medication guides tell patients to report symptoms that do not go away.
  • Fluid loss: both guides advise drinking fluids to reduce dehydration risk from nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea.
  • The prescribed escalation plan: follow the medication-specific label and prescriber; do not change the dose or timing from this page.
  • Planned anesthesia or deep sedation: make sure the procedural team knows which medicine you use.

The source for each point is the current Wegovy or Zepbound prescribing information and medication guide. [1] [2]

Side effects vary by medication

Wegovy and Zepbound have overlapping but not identical adverse-reaction tables, warnings, and schedules. For medication-specific context, use the current label and start with the relevant pillar page: [1] [2]

FAQ

Do GLP-1 side effects go away?
The labels do not promise one timeline. In Wegovy injection trials, gastrointestinal reactions were most frequent during dosage escalation. Zepbound says most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during escalation and decreased over time. Persistent or severe symptoms belong with a healthcare professional, not a generic timeline.
Are side effects worse after a dose increase?
Both labels connect escalation with gastrointestinal tolerability. In Wegovy injection trials, gastrointestinal reactions were most frequent during escalation; Zepbound reports that most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during escalation. That trial pattern cannot predict one person's response.
Does this page tell me what dose to take?
No. This page is educational and focused on understanding and tracking side effects. Dosing, titration, and switching decisions belong with your prescriber.
When should I contact a healthcare professional?
Promptly report severe or persistent abdominal pain, gastrointestinal symptoms that do not go away, or symptoms of a serious allergic reaction. The current label for the medicine is the source of truth for its warning signs.
Can GLP-1 drugs cause ketosis or ketoacidosis?
Japan’s drug regulator, PMDA, said in an October 2026 alert that it has several reports of people using GLP-1 or GIP/GLP-1 drugs for slimming while eating very little who vomited repeatedly and were taken to hospital with ketosis or ketoacidosis. Its table lists 17 such reports from 2004 to May 2026; these are report counts, not rates. PMDA tells clinicians to have patients stop the drug and seek care if they vomit or cannot eat or drink. Talk to your prescriber about any symptoms like these.

When to contact a healthcare professional

The medication guides tell patients to report or get medical help for:

  • Severe abdominal pain that will not go away, with or without nausea or vomiting, and which may radiate to the back
  • Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea that does not go away
  • Stomach problems that are severe or do not go away
  • Symptoms of a serious allergic reaction

See sources [1] and [2].

Minimum useful log

  • Dose timestamp and current dose step
  • Symptom onset relative to that dose
  • A 0–10 severity note
  • Context notes such as sleep, travel, or a large meal
