Why GLP-1 medications cause side effects

Wegovy and Zepbound both delay gastric emptying. Their labels also describe effects on appetite or calorie intake, and both list gastrointestinal adverse reactions prominently. Those facts support a broad mechanism-level connection, but they do not explain every symptom for every person. [1] [2]

Both products use stepwise escalation schedules intended to reduce gastrointestinal adverse reactions. The schedule and any dose change remain medication-specific decisions for the prescriber and current label. [1] [2]

The evidence boundary: delayed gastric emptying and appetite effects are label-supported mechanisms, but a symptom log cannot diagnose why a symptom occurred.

The common side effects

The current Wegovy and Zepbound labels list the effects below among adverse reactions observed in their trials. Rates differ by product, dose, trial, and population, so this is not a personal risk estimate or a head-to-head comparison. [1] [2]

Label-listed effect Source context Useful context to record
Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea Common gastrointestinal reactions in both labels. Onset relative to the injection, duration, and whether fluids stay down.
Constipation Listed in both products' adverse-reaction tables. Start date, severity, and any change from the usual pattern.
Abdominal pain, dyspepsia, or reflux Listed gastrointestinal reactions; severe or persistent pain is a different safety question. Location, severity, duration, and associated vomiting or other symptoms.
Fatigue or headache Reported in Wegovy trials; fatigue is also listed for Zepbound. Timing, hydration, food intake, sleep, and other possible confounders.
Injection-site reaction Listed for Zepbound; both labels instruct rotating injection sites. Site, appearance, duration, and whether the reaction is spreading or severe.

What the labels say about timing

There is no universal week-by-week side-effect timeline. The strongest source-supported pattern is tied to escalation, and the two labels describe it differently:

  • Wegovy: its label says gastrointestinal reactions were most frequently reported during dosage escalation. [1]
  • Zepbound: its label says most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during dosage escalation and decreased over time. [2]
  • For an individual: neither statement guarantees when a symptom will start, how long it will last, or whether it is caused by the medicine.
Why timing is worth recording: a dated log can show whether a symptom began before or after an injection or escalation. It gives a prescriber chronology; it does not establish causation.

Red flags: when to seek medical care

These are not "track and wait" symptoms. The current labels tell patients to contact a healthcare professional promptly for specific serious reactions. Emergency symptoms still require emergency care. This list summarizes label language and is not exhaustive. [1] [2]
  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain, sometimes radiating to the back and with or without nausea or vomiting, is listed as a possible sign of pancreatitis.
  • Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea that does not go away can lead to dehydration and kidney injury; the medication guides tell patients to contact their healthcare provider.
  • Severe stomach problems warrant prompt contact. Both labels also report postmarketing ileus, intestinal obstruction, and severe constipation including fecal impaction.
  • Signs of a serious allergic reaction, including swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat or trouble breathing or swallowing, require urgent care.
  • Symptoms of low blood sugar matter especially when these medicines are used with insulin or an insulin secretagogue such as a sulfonylurea, because the labels describe increased risk with those combinations.
  • A neck lump, persistent hoarseness, trouble swallowing, or shortness of breath appears in the labels' counseling language about thyroid tumors. Both products are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN 2.
  • Before surgery or a procedure using anesthesia or deep sedation, tell the care team you take the medicine. Both labels describe rare postmarketing pulmonary aspiration reports and say evidence is insufficient to define a universal mitigation plan.

What to discuss with your prescriber

The labels support a short, non-prescriptive checklist. Your prescriber and the current product label determine medication-specific decisions.

  • Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms: both medication guides tell patients to report symptoms that do not go away.
  • Fluid loss: both guides advise drinking fluids to reduce dehydration risk from nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea.
  • The prescribed escalation plan: follow the medication-specific label and prescriber; do not change the dose or timing from this page.
  • Planned anesthesia or deep sedation: make sure the procedural team knows which medicine you use.

The source for each point is the current Wegovy or Zepbound prescribing information and medication guide. [1] [2]

Side effects vary by medication

Wegovy and Zepbound have overlapping but not identical adverse-reaction tables, warnings, and schedules. For medication-specific context, use the current label and start with the relevant pillar page: [1] [2]

FAQ

Do GLP-1 side effects go away?
The labels do not promise one timeline. Wegovy says gastrointestinal reactions were most frequent during dosage escalation. Zepbound says most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during escalation and decreased over time. Persistent or severe symptoms belong with a healthcare professional, not a generic timeline.
Are side effects worse after a dose increase?
Both labels connect escalation with gastrointestinal tolerability. Wegovy reports gastrointestinal reactions most frequently during escalation; Zepbound reports that most nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea events occurred during escalation. That trial pattern cannot predict one person's response.
Does this page tell me what dose to take?
No. This page is educational and focused on understanding and tracking side effects. Dosing, titration, and switching decisions belong with your prescriber.
When should I contact a healthcare professional?
Promptly report severe or persistent abdominal pain, gastrointestinal symptoms that do not go away, or symptoms of a serious allergic reaction. The current label for the medicine is the source of truth for its warning signs.

When to seek care

The labels identify these as reasons for prompt medical attention:

  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain, especially radiating to the back
  • Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea that does not go away
  • Severe stomach problems or severe constipation
  • Signs of a serious allergic reaction

See sources [1] and [2].

Minimum useful log

  • Dose timestamp and current dose step
  • Symptom onset relative to that dose
  • A 0–10 severity note
  • Confounders like poor sleep, travel, or a large meal
