What to log every injection

Most people do not want a journal. They want a repeatable way to answer four questions: what did I take, when did I take it, what changed after, and was this a normal week or a disrupted one.

High-signal rule: the best log is usually shorter than people think. Timestamp, medication, site, dose as prescribed, and one factual note often beats a long diary you will never reread.
  • Injection anchor: date, time, medication, and site.
  • Dose as prescribed: record the dose your prescriber set, so the entry is identifiable later without turning the log into dosing advice.
  • Symptom timing: nausea, constipation, fatigue, appetite change, sleep change, or any effect you want to compare against the clock.
  • Routine context: travel, unusual meals, poor sleep, illness, training, alcohol, or major schedule drift.
  • Missed-dose context: whether the week was late, skipped, restarted, or otherwise off the normal routine.
Boundary: this is tracking guidance only. It does not tell you what dose to take, when to increase, or how to switch medications. Those decisions belong with your prescriber.

Timing, missed, and late doses

Most GLP-1 medications in this family are taken on a fixed weekly cadence, though the specifics depend on your medication and prescriber. The job of a log is not to decide what to do about a missed or late dose — that is what the medication label and your prescriber are for — but to capture what actually happened so a disrupted week is not misread as a normal one later.

Practical filter: if you find yourself writing the same context note every week, that is a signal worth showing your prescriber. Consistent patterns are exactly what a good log is meant to surface.

FAQ

Do I need to log every symptom?
No. Log the symptoms and context that help you interpret timing later. A shorter, consistent log is usually more useful than an exhaustive one you abandon.

What if I track non-GLP-1 peptides too?
The same timestamp-and-context logic works across compounds. The peptide hub covers the other classes if your routine is broader than one medication.

Does this page give dosing advice?
No. It is educational and focused on tracking. Dosing, titration, and switching decisions belong with your prescriber and the medication label.

Quick logging rule

Anchor every entry to the injection timestamp first. Everything else — symptoms, sites, context — hangs off that one reliable reference point.
