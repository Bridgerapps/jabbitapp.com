Home / Research / GLP-1 drugs and AFib teardown

Study teardown · Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) and other GLP-1 drugs · Atrial fibrillation · Meta-analyses, trials, health records, a US guideline, FDA labels and trial registries · Sources checked October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

Do Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound lower the risk of atrial fibrillation (AFib)? Pooled trials suggest fewer AF reports on GLP-1 drugs, especially semaglutide, but certainty is low. A network meta-analysis of 94 trials published October 26, 2026 ranked injected semaglutide best and tirzepatide near the bottom, though tirzepatide-only data are too thin to call. None of the trials pooled was powered to detect AF, and the first placebo-controlled trials in people with AF list completion dates from December 2026.

Atrial fibrillation (AF, or AFib) is an irregular heart rhythm that becomes more common with age and weight, and raises the risk of stroke and death.[15] On October 26, 2026, the journal Heart Rhythm published a network meta-analysis of 94 randomized trials with 155,989 people. It found 18% fewer AF reports on injected semaglutide than on placebo, and fewer than on tirzepatide.[1] Earlier meta-analyses point the same way, most clearly in people with obesity.[6][2][3] But AF was mostly logged as a side effect, none of the 249 trials in one large analysis was adequately powered to detect it, and an umbrella review rates the certainty as low.[3][5] A tirzepatide-only analysis found no clear AF signal either way.[4]

Why this matters now

The new analysis ranked 12 GLP-1 drugs and combination drugs on AF. Injected semaglutide came out on top, while tirzepatide and dulaglutide scored far lower; the authors call for “further studies focusing on arrhythmic outcomes” to confirm the findings.[1] Our analysis: a ranking like this is easy to over-read. The semaglutide-vs-tirzepatide result is largely an indirect comparison across trials that were not designed to measure AF, and a separate 2026 meta-analysis found no difference between single-, dual- and triple-acting drugs.[1][2] We set out what each kind of evidence shows.

The evidence, rung by rung

What the pooled trials show

  • Semaglutide. Fewer AF reports than placebo in the new network analysis (relative risk 0.82) and in a 2024 meta-analysis of 10 trials, mostly in diabetes (0.58).[1][6]
  • GLP-1 drugs in obesity. 18% fewer AF reports across 24 trials with 40,694 people, with no difference by drug type and no link to how much weight people lost.[2] A 249-trial European Heart Journal analysis found a similar result for obesity treatment (0.79).[3]
  • The whole class in diabetes. Eight large heart-outcome trials in type 2 diabetes, with 60,081 people, found no significant change in AF (0.96).[7] A network analysis of diabetes drugs found lower AF risk with SGLT-2 inhibitors than with GLP-1 drugs.[9]
  • Tirzepatide. In 10 trials with 6,515 people, the odds ratio for AF was 2.20, but the range ran from 0.81 to 6.75, so the result is not significant. Any arrhythmia was more common (odds ratio 1.84), with low event rates; the authors urge caution.[4]

Heart rate: what the labels say

GLP-1 drugs speed up the resting heart a little. Wegovy’s label lists heart-rate increase as a warning: 1 to 4 extra beats per minute on average, with more people than on placebo having rises of 20 beats or more at some visit (26% vs 16%).[16] It tells patients to report palpitations or a racing heartbeat at rest, and to stop Wegovy if a resting heart-rate increase is sustained.[16] Zepbound’s label reports a mean rise of 1 to 3 beats per minute among its side effects.[17] Our analysis: a faster heart rate is not AF, and the trials above suggest fewer AF reports, not more. But palpitations on these drugs are worth raising with a prescriber rather than assuming they are harmless. For more on heart-rate changes, see our GLP-1 heart rate variability guide.

If you already have AFib

  • Weight loss is in the guideline. The 2023 US guideline gives its strongest recommendation class to weight loss, ideally 10% or more, for people with AF and a BMI above 27; it does not name any GLP-1 drug.[15] The LEGACY study found people who kept off 10% or more were six times as likely to stay free of arrhythmia, though it was observational.[14]
  • Heart failure plus AF. In Novo Nordisk-funded STEP-HFpEF trials, 518 of 1,145 people had AF. Semaglutide improved heart-failure symptom scores more in people with AF than without, and serious cardiac disorders were less common than on placebo regardless of AF history.[10]
  • After ablation. In 6 mostly observational studies, AF came back less often in people on GLP-1 drugs (28.6% vs 32.9% at 12 months), with high variation between studies.[12] A 2026 review warns this evidence is open to “healthy-user bias”, meaning people who start these drugs may differ in other ways.[13]

Healthy aging: why AF matters for longevity

The US guideline puts lifetime AF risk at about 30% to 40% in White individuals and says AF carries a 1.5- to 2-fold higher risk of death.[15] Fat around the heart, especially next to the left atrium, may be one link between obesity and AF, a 2026 review suggests.[13] In US health records of adults with obesity and kidney disease, new AF over one year was 1.63% on GLP-1 drugs vs 2.62% in matched people not on them.[11] Our analysis: if the trial signal holds up, fewer AF cases would be a meaningful healthy-aging benefit. But the records study shows an association, not cause and effect, and the trial pools have not been confirmed by a trial designed to measure AF.[11][5]

The case for caution

An umbrella review of 10 meta-analyses rated none of them moderate or high quality, and found their agreement “partly reflects a shared set of primary trials rather than independent replication.”[5] The European Heart Journal analysis noted that 207 of its 249 trials found AF only through adverse-event reports.[3] Heart-outcome trials in diabetes found no clear class effect.[7][8] Our analysis: the semaglutide signal is consistent but not proven, and the tirzepatide ranking rests on few events.[1][4]

Trials coming

  • TIRO-AF (Cleveland Clinic): tirzepatide vs placebo in 100 people with AF and obesity, measuring AF burden; primary completion listed as December 31, 2026.[18]
  • DUTCH weight-control study (Rijnstate Hospital, Netherlands): semaglutide vs placebo in 280 people, double-blind; primary completion April 2027.[19]
  • University of Chicago: semaglutide vs placebo in 200 people, measuring time to AF and AF burden; December 2027.[20]
  • A San Francisco VA semaglutide trial is listed as suspended, citing a “company decision.”[21] Several trials of GLP-1 drugs around ablation are open-label or single-blind and run to 2027-2029.[22]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. Most AF counts come from side-effect reports in weight and diabetes trials.[3]
  2. The meta-analyses overlap, so several papers agreeing is not several independent confirmations.[5]
  3. The semaglutide-vs-tirzepatide gap comes from one network analysis; a pooled obesity analysis found no difference by drug type, and tirzepatide trials have few AF events.[1][2][4]
  4. Results differ by population: clearer in obesity trials, neutral in diabetes heart-outcome trials.[2][7]
  5. The new paper’s disclosures were not in the PubMed record and its full text is paywalled; we relied on the abstract.[1]

Frequently asked questions

Does Ozempic or Wegovy lower the risk of AFib?
Pooled trials report fewer AF cases on semaglutide than on placebo, about 18% fewer in the newest analysis. But AF was mostly logged as a side effect and certainty is rated low.[1][5]
Can Zepbound or Mounjaro cause AFib?
Tirzepatide trials show no clear link: the AF estimate was not significant and had a wide range. Overall arrhythmias were somewhat more common, with few events.[4]
Is semaglutide better than tirzepatide for AFib?
One October 2026 network analysis ranked semaglutide higher. That is largely an indirect comparison, and another meta-analysis found no difference by drug type.[1][2]
Do GLP-1 drugs raise heart rate?
Yes, slightly. Labels report 1 to 4 extra beats per minute for Wegovy and 1 to 3 for Zepbound on average. Wegovy’s label asks patients to report a racing heartbeat at rest.[16][17]
Should people with AFib take a GLP-1 drug?
The US guideline recommends weight loss for people with AF and overweight, without naming a drug. Placebo-controlled AF trials list completion dates from late 2026; decisions belong with a cardiologist.[15][18][19]
Do GLP-1 drugs stop AFib coming back after ablation?
Mostly observational studies link them to fewer recurrences, about 29% vs 33% at one year. Randomized trials are still running.[12][22]

Bottom line

Trial pools consistently suggest fewer AF reports on GLP-1 drugs in people with obesity, most clearly for semaglutide, and the October 26 network analysis adds to that picture.[1][2][3] The certainty is low, tirzepatide data are too thin to rank, and the first placebo-controlled trials in people with AF list completion dates from December 2026.[5][4][18] Our analysis: this is a promising signal for healthy aging, not yet a reason to choose one drug over another.
