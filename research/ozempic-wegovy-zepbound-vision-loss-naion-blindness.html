Home / Research / GLP-1 drugs and vision loss

Evidence review · Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus (semaglutide), Zepbound and Mounjaro (tirzepatide), Trulicity, Victoza and Saxenda · NAION, sudden vision loss · Cohort studies, trials, regulators, 9 US drug labels, 835 FDA adverse event reports, federal court data, ClinicalTrials.gov · Sources checked October 20, 2026

Published October 20, 2026 · Updated October 20, 2026

Can Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro or Zepbound cause sudden vision loss? Possibly, but rarely: the biggest studies find about 3 to 10 extra cases of NAION per 10,000 users, Europe, the UK and Australia now warn about it, and US labels still don’t.

NAION is a stroke of the optic nerve. It usually dims or blurs part of the vision in one eye, without pain, and often permanently.[6][20] The largest recent studies of people with type 2 diabetes found it more often on GLP-1 drugs than on other diabetes pills: 8.5 vs 5.5 per 10,000 over 18 months in one, 39 vs 29 per 10,000 over three years in another.[6][8] Novo Nordisk’s own placebo-controlled trials found no increase, and part of the gap may come from who gets prescribed these drugs.[14][7] Europe and the UK list NAION as a very rare side effect of semaglutide, and Australia added warnings to five GLP-1 drugs in July.[19][20][21] We read all 9 current US labels: none mentions NAION.[23] FDA’s adverse event database holds 835 NAION reports naming a GLP-1 drug, 821 of them received since 2024, and federal vision-loss lawsuits grew from 29 to 247 this year.[25][27]

Why this matters now

On October 10, NPR told the story of Todd Engel, a Maryland man who lost vision in one eye and then the other while taking Ozempic for diabetes, and reported that FDA is still studying the risk.[1] Four days earlier, a study presented at the American Academy of Ophthalmology’s annual meeting found that among people who lost vision in both eyes, GLP-1 users lost the second eye sooner.[2] The Rutgers study recirculated on October 15, and the federal vision-loss litigation reached 247 cases on October 1.[3][27] English posts on X about GLP-1 drugs and vision loss peaked at 33 on October 15 (UTC).[5] NPR, citing Gallup, reports that more than one in 10 Americans are taking a GLP-1 drug for weight loss.[1] Readers want to know if their eyes are at risk and what to watch for.

The short answer

  • The risk looks real but small. Most large studies find more NAION on GLP-1 drugs, roughly 3 to 10 extra cases per 10,000 people over 1½ to 5 years.[6][7][8]
  • It isn’t proven to be caused by the drugs. Randomized trials haven’t shown a clear increase, the gap shrank in Sweden when researchers compared people with similar diabetes, and the US studies lacked full data on weight and diabetes severity.[14][15][7][6]
  • Who is most at risk: men, people over 50, and people with heart disease or other eye conditions. The extra risk was minimal in women and people under 50.[6]
  • Regulators disagree. Europe, the UK and Australia warn about it. The FDA hasn’t changed any label, and none of 9 US labels we checked mentions NAION.[19][20][21][23]
  • Sudden vision loss is an emergency. Get a same-day eye exam and mention the GLP-1. Don’t stop the drug just because of this risk without talking to your prescriber.[20][21][17]

What NAION is

Non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy happens when blood flow to the front of the optic nerve drops. It typically causes sudden, painless vision loss in one eye, often described as blurring or cloudiness.[20] The vision loss is usually permanent. About a third of patients improve somewhat, usually within six months, but some deficit often remains.[6] There is no treatment shown to improve vision afterward.[21]

It is rare to begin with. In people 40 and older, the UK regulator puts the background rate at about 8 to 11 cases per 100,000 people a year. In type 2 diabetes, estimates range from 11 to 82 per 100,000. Diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure and high cholesterol all raise the risk.[20] About 15% of people who have NAION in one eye get it in the other within five years.[2] That overlap is the core problem for research. The people who take GLP-1 drugs are, on average, already at higher risk.

Most big records studies point the same way. In US insurance claims, Rutgers researchers found 3.0 extra cases per 10,000 GLP-1 users over 18 months compared with SGLT2 pills, and 3.6 compared with DPP-4 pills. That is one extra case for every 3,333 and 2,778 people treated.[6] A VA study of 588,168 veterans found 9.98 extra cases per 10,000 over three years. GLP-1 users weren’t more likely to get other eye diseases, such as diabetic retinopathy or optic neuritis, a check the authors used to test whether the link is specific to NAION.[8] A meta-analysis in Neurology put semaglutide’s relative risk at 2.52.[16]

But confounding is a real possibility. In Sweden, the 5-year risk ratio fell from 1.69 to 1.23, no longer significant, when researchers limited the comparison to people already on metformin. They did that to compare people with similar diabetes severity.[7] Rutgers saw a smaller gap versus SGLT2 pills in people on metformin alone (2.0 vs 5.7 extra cases per 10,000 in people on two or more diabetes drugs), and noted it lacked data on body weight and how long people had diabetes.[6] A large multinational records study found no significant link at all.[13]

Randomized trials haven’t settled it. Novo Nordisk pooled its placebo-controlled semaglutide and liraglutide trials, 96,829 people in all, and found 3 confirmed cases on the drugs and 5 on placebo. Three of the authors work for Novo.[14] An independent meta-analysis counted 5 vs 1 ischemic optic neuropathy events in diabetes trials and 4 vs 1 in weight-loss trials. Neither difference was statistically significant, because the events were so rare.[15] Europe’s safety committee, which had access to trial data, said the trials “also point to a slightly higher risk.”[19]

Weight-loss users and tirzepatide are the gaps. Nearly all the large studies involve people with type 2 diabetes. In observational studies of semaglutide for weight loss, the hazard ratio was 1.57 and not significant.[15] The 2024 Mass Eye and Ear study that started the concern found much larger hazard ratios, but it drew on patients already referred to neuro-ophthalmologists.[11] For tirzepatide, the one large analysis pooled it with semaglutide: 0.04% vs 0.02% over two years.[12] One editorial in Ophthalmology last month summed it up in its title: “An Important Rare Issue, Lacking Adequate Data.”[18]

The second eye. The new AAO meeting study looked at about 1,440 GLP-1 users and 20,700 non-users who lost vision in both eyes. On GLP-1 drugs, 56.8% of second-eye events came within six months, versus 40.6% without them. GLP-1 users also had higher blood sugar, and the study used pooled records, so it can’t show cause. It was a meeting presentation, not a peer-reviewed paper.[2]

The US labels are silent. We read the current prescribing information for Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus, Zepbound, Mounjaro, Trulicity, Saxenda, Victoza and Foundayo. None mentions NAION, ischemic optic neuropathy or the optic nerve. Seven tell patients to report “changes in vision,” which the prescriber sections tie to a warning about diabetic retinopathy, a different eye disease.[23] The FDA’s Sentinel study of GLP-1 drugs and NAION in type 2 diabetes was posted in June 2025 and is still listed as in progress. The FDA told NPR it will “determine the need for regulatory action” once its assessment is complete.[22][1]

The Viagra precedent. A US label already handles evidence of a similar size with a warning. Viagra’s FDA-approved label tells patients to stop and get care for sudden vision loss. It cites observational risk estimates of 2.15 and 2.27, and it says neither the reports nor the studies prove cause.[24] Europe’s estimate for semaglutide is “approximately two-fold,” but no US semaglutide label carries a similar warning.[19][23]

What FDA’s adverse event reports show

We pulled every report in the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System coded as optic ischaemic neuropathy, the medical term that covers NAION, through June 30, 2026.[25]

  • Almost all of the reports are recent. Of 669 reports naming semaglutide as a suspect drug, 4 arrived before 2024. Then came 57 in 2024, 337 in 2025 and 271 in the first half of 2026.[25] The first study linking semaglutide to NAION came out in mid-2024.[11][1] Sildenafil shows the same pattern: 344 of its 555 NAION reports arrived in 2005–2007.[25]
  • Who reports has changed. In 2024, doctors filed 40 of the 65 GLP-1 NAION reports. In early 2026 they filed 68 of 330, while consumers filed 201 and lawyers 24.[25] Reports filed after publicity and lawsuits don’t prove anything either way.
  • The patients fit NAION’s usual profile. The median reported age was 60, and 455 of the 528 reports with an age were 50 or older. 425 were men and 308 women. 157 reports were coded as disabling.[25]
  • Tirzepatide has fewer reports. FDA has 149 reports naming it as a suspect drug, against 669 for semaglutide, even though FAERS holds about twice as many reports of any kind for tirzepatide.[25]

Semaglutide’s share of NAION reports is the highest of the 13 drugs we checked, about double that of sildenafil, whose label already warns about NAION. These counts mix suspect and background drugs, which is why metformin shows up. Treat them as a measure of how much attention the issue has drawn, not of risk.[25][24] The UK shows the other extreme. Its Yellow Card scheme had received just 3 reports suggestive of NAION on semaglutide by August 2025, against roughly 10.2 million packs dispensed.[20]

The lawsuits

Federal NAION cases against GLP-1 makers were consolidated in Philadelphia in December 2025 under Judge Karen Marston. They numbered 29 on January 5 and 247 on October 1.[27] The biggest jump came between July 1 and August 3, from 146 to 200 cases, around the July 14 publication of the Rutgers and Swedish studies.[27][6][7] A separate GLP-1 stomach-injury litigation has 4,025 pending cases.[27] Novo Nordisk calls the suits without merit, and Eli Lilly says it will “vigorously defend against these claims.”[1][4] One plaintiffs’ firm alone says it represents at least 150 people with NAION after GLP-1 use.[4]

What is being studied

Of 77 studies of NAION or ischemic optic neuropathy registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, one looks at NAION in GLP-1 users. It is a 28-person retrospective chart review in Istanbul, not yet recruiting.[26] No randomized trial registers NAION as an outcome, and we found no registered NAION study run by Novo Nordisk or Eli Lilly. Novo’s 1,500-person FOCUS eye trial of semaglutide tracks diabetic retinopathy and visual acuity but registers no optic nerve outcome, and its primary results are due in late 2027.[26] The study most likely to move US regulators is the FDA’s own Sentinel analysis, which has no published date.[22]

What to do with this

  • Know the sign. Sudden blurring, dimming, a dark patch or a missing part of vision in one eye, usually painless. Both patients in the recent news coverage noticed it on waking.[20][6][1][4]
  • Treat it as an emergency. UK guidance says to go urgently to eye casualty or A&E. Tell the eye doctor you take a GLP-1 drug, including compounded or private prescriptions, which may not be in your records.[20]
  • Don’t stop just because of the headlines. The US ophthalmology societies don’t recommend stopping, and Optometry Australia says people without symptoms shouldn’t be told to stop solely because they take a GLP-1. Decisions belong in a shared discussion with your clinician.[1][21][17]
  • If NAION is confirmed, Europe, the UK and Australia say semaglutide should be stopped.[19][20][21]
  • Have the risk talk if you are in a higher-risk group. That means men over 50, people with heart disease or eye conditions, and anyone who has already had NAION in one eye.[6][2]

Strengths and limits

Strengths. The largest studies used active comparators, adjusted for dozens of factors and include hundreds of thousands of people from the US, Sweden, Denmark and Norway.[6][7][8][10] Our label, adverse event, registry and court counts are direct reads of primary records.[23][25][26][27]

Limits. Every large study is observational and mostly in type 2 diabetes. None can rule out confounding by diabetes severity or body weight.[7][6] Diagnostic codes for NAION are imperfect; Rutgers had to use a broader code.[6] Trials are too small to detect an event this rare.[15][14] Adverse event reports are unverified, influenced by publicity, and can’t give rates.[25] Data on tirzepatide and on people taking GLP-1 drugs for weight loss alone are thin.[12][15]

Bottom line

If GLP-1 drugs cause NAION, they do so rarely: a few extra cases per 10,000 people over a couple of years, concentrated in older men and people with vascular disease.[6][8] Eye specialists quoted in the coverage say the benefits likely outweigh that risk for most people, and the ophthalmology societies call the overall risk low. But the risk isn’t zero, it is permanent when it hits, and three major regulators now warn about it while US labels don’t.[4][17][19][20][21][23] Knowing the warning sign and acting the same day is the one thing every user can do.

Frequently asked questions

Can Ozempic or Wegovy make you go blind?
Rarely, it may cause NAION, a stroke of the optic nerve that causes sudden, usually painless vision loss in one eye. It usually blurs or dims part of the vision rather than causing total blindness, and the loss is often permanent. Europe’s and the UK’s drug regulators list it as a very rare side effect of semaglutide, the drug in Ozempic and Wegovy, affecting up to 1 in 10,000 users. Studies disagree on whether the drug causes it or whether people who get these drugs are simply at higher risk.
How common is NAION on a GLP-1 drug?
Rare. In the largest recent studies of people with type 2 diabetes, it occurred in about 8.5 per 10,000 GLP-1 users over 18 months versus 5.5 on other diabetes pills, and 39 versus 29 per 10,000 over three years in older, mostly male veterans. That works out to roughly 3 to 10 extra cases per 10,000 people. Novo Nordisk’s pooled placebo-controlled trials found no increase (3 vs 5 cases).
What are the warning signs, and what should I do?
Sudden loss of vision in one eye, sometimes noticed on waking: blurring, dimming, a dark patch, or a missing wedge of vision, usually without pain. UK guidance says to go urgently to eye casualty or A&E. Treat it as an emergency, get a same-day eye exam, and tell the doctor you take a GLP-1 drug, including compounded or privately prescribed versions.
Should I stop my GLP-1 because of the NAION risk?
Not because of the risk alone. The US ophthalmology societies don’t recommend stopping, Optometry Australia says people without symptoms shouldn’t be told to stop just because they take a GLP-1, and the societies say decisions to start, continue or stop belong in a shared discussion with your clinician. If NAION is confirmed, European, UK and Australian guidance says to stop semaglutide. People who already had NAION in one eye, men over 50 and people with heart disease or other eye conditions have the most to discuss.
Do Zepbound and Mounjaro (tirzepatide) carry the same risk?
Less is known. Most studies looked at semaglutide. One US study that pooled semaglutide and tirzepatide found NAION in 0.04% of users vs 0.02% of comparison patients over two years. Australia added the warning to Mounjaro in July 2026, while Europe’s 2025 and the UK’s 2026 warnings cover semaglutide. FDA has received 149 NAION reports naming tirzepatide as a suspect drug, versus 669 for semaglutide.
Why don’t US labels warn about NAION?
The FDA hasn’t changed them. Its own Sentinel study has been in progress since June 2025, and it told NPR it will determine the need for regulatory action once that is done. None of the 9 US GLP-1 labels we checked mentions NAION. A US label already warns on similar-sized evidence: Viagra’s label cites a roughly two-fold risk estimate from observational studies, while saying the data don’t prove cause.
