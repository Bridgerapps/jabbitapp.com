Study teardown · Dihexa (dihexa acetate, PNB-0408, sold online as a “nootropic peptide”) · FDA documents, the original rat papers and their journal notices, SEC filings on its prodrug fosgonimeton, registry and FAERS counts · Sources checked October 24, 2026

Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

Dihexa (the “10 million times stronger than BDNF” nootropic peptide): does it work, and is it safe? It has never been tested in a human trial, FDA says it has no human exposure data, three related papers from its home lab were retracted in 2025, and the drug designed to turn into it failed its Alzheimer’s trials.

Dihexa is a small, modified fragment of angiotensin IV made at Washington State University (WSU) and described in 2013 as an oral, brain-penetrant compound that reversed memory deficits in rats.[5] The famous claim that it is “10 million times” stronger than the brain growth factor BDNF comes from a 2012 WSU news release about a lab dish assay, not from a human study.[4] As of October 24, 2026, no trial of dihexa itself appears on ClinicalTrials.gov, FDA says it has found no human exposure data, and no product is approved.[16][1][17] The closest human evidence comes from fosgonimeton, a drug its developer says turns into dihexa in the blood: it missed its main goals in two Alzheimer’s trials, though it was generally well tolerated.[12][13][14]

Why this matters now

FDA says its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee will discuss dihexa acetate, with LL-37, GHK-Cu, melanotan II and PEG-MGF, at a meeting before the end of February 2027, as candidates for the list of substances pharmacies may compound.[2] No date or briefing materials have been posted. FDA’s separate peptide compounding policy cleared White House review on October 27, 2026, and the version HHS made public on October 28 lists six peptides; dihexa is not among them.[3] Dihexa is also widely discussed online as a “nootropic peptide.” This page sets out what the record shows before that meeting. We covered the wider policy in our FDA peptide compounding explainer.

Where “10 million times stronger than BDNF” comes from

The figure traces to WSU’s October 2012 news release. It says that “in bench assays using living nerve cells to monitor new neuronal connections,” the researchers found dihexa “seven orders of magnitude more powerful than BDNF,” and translates that as needing “10 million times as much BDNF to get as much new synapse formation.”[4] That is a comparison of concentrations in a lab dish, not of effects in people. The same release said the rat results needed “additional studies with larger test groups” and that FDA safety testing had to come before any human trial.[4] We could not find a human trial that followed.[16][18]

The animal evidence

The 2013 discovery paper reported that dihexa, given by mouth, reversed memory deficits in rats treated with scopolamine and in aged rats, and increased new synapses in brain cells.[5] Its conflict of interest statement says two authors “are founders and shareholders of M3 Biotechnology, LLC, which is developing pharmaceuticals based on this technology.”[5]

Some later work came from outside WSU. A 2021 study from China reported better maze performance and less brain inflammation in Alzheimer’s model mice given dihexa, and its authors declared no conflict of interest.[20] A 2018 systematic review of animal studies found that 8 of 9 studies of angiotensin IV and its analogs, dihexa among them, improved performance in models of memory deficit.[23] Not every result was positive: a 2024 rat study of a Huntington’s-like model found dihexa “did not protect rats” from the induced deficits.[21] Researchers have also tested it in a rat nerve-injury model.[22] PubMed lists 19 records for “dihexa,” 14 of them actually about the compound, and none is a human trial.[18] A 2026 review that lists dihexa among popular peptides notes “a current lack of clinical trials.”[24]

Papers behind dihexa that were retracted or flagged

In January 2025 the Journal of Pharmacology and Experimental Therapeutics retracted the 2014 paper that tied dihexa’s effects to the HGF/c-Met receptor. Its notice says: “Following an investigation by Washington State University, Figures 1B, 2A/C, and data in the subsequent erratum submission for the article have been found to contain falsified and/or fabricated data,” and names two authors as solely responsible.[8] The journal retracted two earlier papers from the same lab on HGF/Met-targeting angiotensin IV analogs at the same time, citing the same WSU investigation.[9][10]

The 2013 paper that first described dihexa has not been retracted. Since 2021 it has carried an expression of concern in which the editors cite “possible image manipulation” and say they “will await the results of an inquiry.”[6] An expression of concern is not a finding of misconduct, and as of October 24, 2026 PubMed lists no retraction or correction for that paper.[11]

Separately, Athira said in an October 2021 SEC filing that a special committee of its board found that its then-chief executive “altered images in her 2011 doctoral dissertation and at least four research papers” co-authored as a WSU graduate student and published from 2011 to 2014, and that “WSU’s dihexa patent incorporates images from papers” she co-authored, “certain of which were altered.” The filing reports her resignation effective October 18, 2021.[12] Athira’s filing also says its own drug, fosgonimeton, belongs to a different patent family from the dihexa patent.[12] A retraction removes a paper from the record; it does not by itself show that every result in it, or in related work by other groups, is wrong.

The only human data: fosgonimeton, which turns into dihexa

Athira says that fosgonimeton (ATH-1017) “metabolizes rapidly in plasma into … dihexa.”[12] So its trials are the nearest thing to human data on dihexa exposure, with caveats: fosgonimeton is a different molecule, it was given by injection under the skin to screened patients, and the trials were not designed to measure dihexa’s own effects.[14] This is our analysis, not a claim Athira makes.

  • ACT-AD (phase 2, 77 patients): the primary endpoint, a brainwave measure, “was not statistically significant for the full study population.” Athira pointed to a pre-specified subgroup that did not take standard Alzheimer’s drugs.[13]
  • LIFT-AD (phase 2/3, 312 patients in the primary analysis): missed its primary endpoint (p=0.70) and its key memory and daily-function endpoints (p=0.35 and p=0.61). Athira reported a reduction in the blood marker pTau217 and better numbers in some subgroups, and its chief medical officer said a placebo group that barely declined over a short study may have made a benefit hard to detect.[14]
  • What came next: the open-label extension and the SHAPE dementia trial were terminated, and the company’s 2025 annual report says it “decided to pause further development of fosgonimeton.”[16][15]

Safety: what is and isn’t known

  • Dihexa itself: FDA says it “has not identified any human exposure data on drug products containing dihexa acetate administered via any route of administration” and “lacks important information regarding any safety issues,” including “whether it would cause harm if administered to humans.”[1]
  • The prodrug in trials: Athira reported fosgonimeton was generally well tolerated over 26 weeks, with more adverse events than placebo, mainly injection site reactions, a similar rate of serious adverse events and no deaths; 22% of LIFT-AD participants stopped early.[14][13] Those results come from screened patients under medical supervision and do not tell us about unregulated products.
  • Adverse event reports: FAERS has no reports coded to dihexa.[17] With no approved product, that reflects how little it is tracked rather than evidence of safety.
  • A theoretical concern (our analysis): dihexa’s developers described it as activating the HGF/MET pathway.[5][19] MET is a well-known cancer gene, and drugs that block it are used against some cancers.[25] Nobody has shown that dihexa causes cancer; the point is only that long-term effects of switching this pathway on in people have not been studied.

Legal status

No dihexa product is approved by FDA.[17] FDA lists dihexa acetate among bulk substances whose nominations for compounding were withdrawn, with the safety summary quoted above, and its advisory committee is due to discuss it by February 2027.[1][2] The interim peptide policy HHS made public on October 28 does not list dihexa.[3] The WSU patent that covers dihexa and related molecules remains active, according to Google Patents.[19]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the cited sources.

  1. The best-known claim about dihexa, “10 million times stronger than BDNF,” comes from a university news release about a cell-culture assay, not from a peer-reviewed human result.[4]
  2. The company that licensed dihexa said in an SEC filing that it stopped developing it because it lacked the characteristics of a successful drug.[12]
  3. The paper linking dihexa to HGF/c-Met is retracted, and the discovery paper carries an unresolved expression of concern, which is not a finding of misconduct.[8][6]
  4. Independent animal studies point in both directions, and none is in humans.[20][21][23]
  5. The one drug tested in people that turns into dihexa failed its main Alzheimer’s goals in two trials, and its development is paused.[13][14][15]
  6. FDA’s committee will be weighing a compound for which FDA itself says it has no human exposure data.[1][2]

Frequently asked questions

Is dihexa safe?
Nobody knows. FDA says it has no human exposure data on dihexa and lacks information on whether it would cause harm in people. No trial of dihexa itself has been registered.[1][16]
Does dihexa work for memory or Alzheimer’s?
It improved memory in some rat and mouse studies but not in others, and it has never been tested for that in people. Fosgonimeton, a drug that turns into dihexa, missed its main goals in two Alzheimer’s trials.[5][20][21][13][14]
Is dihexa really 10 million times stronger than BDNF?
That figure comes from a 2012 WSU news release about a lab dish assay of new nerve connections. It is not a measure of effects in people.[4]
Is dihexa FDA approved or legal to compound?
No dihexa product is FDA approved. FDA lists dihexa acetate among withdrawn compounding nominations, and its advisory committee is due to discuss it before the end of February 2027.[17][1][2]
Were dihexa studies retracted?
The 2014 paper linking dihexa to the HGF/c-Met receptor and two related papers were retracted in 2025 after a WSU investigation. The 2013 discovery paper has an expression of concern, which is not a finding of misconduct, and has not been retracted.[8][9][10][6]
What is the difference between dihexa and fosgonimeton?
Fosgonimeton (ATH-1017) is a different molecule that Athira says breaks down into dihexa in the blood. It was injected in clinical trials; dihexa itself was dropped by Athira before human testing.[12]
Could dihexa raise cancer risk?
There is no evidence that it does, but it is described as activating HGF/MET, a pathway that drives some cancers. Long-term human effects have not been studied.[19][25]

Bottom line

Dihexa has interesting rat data and a striking marketing line, but no human trial, no FDA safety data, a retracted mechanism paper, and a discovery paper under an expression of concern.[5][4][16][1][8][6] The only drug tested in people that becomes dihexa was generally well tolerated but did not beat placebo on its main Alzheimer’s goals.[14] Anyone considering it would be relying on animal data alone.
