Published October 25, 2026 · Updated October 25, 2026

Rapamycin for longevity: does the anti-aging drug work, and is it safe? It reliably extends lifespan in mice, but no human trial has tested whether it extends life; the human aging trials are small or short with mixed results, and as sirolimus it is an FDA-approved transplant immunosuppressant with a boxed warning for infection and cancer risk.

Rapamycin, also called sirolimus, is a prescription drug that calms the immune system. In the US it is approved to prevent rejection of kidney transplants and to treat a rare lung disease, LAM, and its label carries a boxed warning about infections and cancer.[1][2] It is now prescribed off-label and discussed widely as a longevity drug.[23] Does it work for that? In mice, yes: a national testing program found it extended lifespan at three sites, even when started late in life.[5] In people, no trial has tested whether it extends life; the randomized aging trials in our chart enrolled 25 to 1,024 people, the three largest tested related drugs, and the results are mixed.[26][10][12][13] Is it safe? In small, short aging trials in healthy people, serious side effects were uncommon, but the label lists real risks, and one 2026 trial reported more adverse events on the drug than on placebo.[19][12][13][1]

Why this matters now

In March 2026, UT Health San Antonio announced an NIA-funded rapamycin study whose largest part is a placebo-controlled trial in about 84 older adults, and its release says some people are “experimenting with the drug on their own, often without medical supervision.”[23] An April 2026 randomized trial found rapamycin did not help older adults gain more from exercise and may have blunted the gains.[13] A University of Wisconsin everolimus aging study listed as completed in June 2026 has not posted results, a September 2026 paper on rapamycin in frail older adults with heart failure is out but has no public abstract, and the FDA approved a new topical sirolimus cream in July 2026.[24][22][2]

What rapamycin is

Rapamycin was first described in 1975 as an antifungal compound.[4] Today, as sirolimus, it is a pill or oral solution approved for kidney-transplant patients aged 13 and older and for lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM), plus an IV form and two skin products; Drugs@FDA lists 16 sirolimus applications in all, including 11 generics.[1][2][3] It blocks mTOR, a protein that helps cells decide when to grow; blocking this pathway extends lifespan in yeast, worms and flies, which is why aging researchers tested it in mice.[5] Everolimus (RAD001) and RTB101 are related mTOR-blocking drugs, and several of the biggest “rapamycin for aging” trials actually tested them.[8][9][10]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • Mice. The NIA program found rapamycin extended median and maximum lifespan in both sexes at three independent test sites, even when feeding started at 600 days of age, which the authors’ title calls “late in life.”[5] A later study found larger gains at a higher dose and more benefit in females.[6] Another found a short course in middle age helped, but at one dose female mice got more aggressive blood cancers without living longer.[7] The 2009 authors wrote that rapamycin may work “by postponing death from cancer, by retarding mechanisms of ageing, or both.”[5]
  • Dogs. A 10-week trial in 24 healthy middle-aged dogs reported no clinical side effects and some changes in heart measures.[20] The TRIAD trial is designed to test lifespan and healthspan in pet dogs; we found no published results yet.[21]
  • People: lifespan. We found no human trial that measured whether rapamycin extends life. A PubMed search returned 18 randomized trials mentioning rapamycin or sirolimus with aging terms; seven tested a rapamycin-type drug in people for aging-related outcomes, and none measured lifespan.[26]
  • People: aging measures. In PEARL (129 enrolled per the registry), the main measure, visceral fat, did not change; the authors, seven of whom disclose they are employees and shareholders of AgelessRx, reported gains in lean mass and pain in women.[12][24] A 25-person pilot found no changes in physical, cognitive or self-rated health measures, but some red-blood-cell values fell.[11] A 13-person heart-rehab pilot noted “no improvement in frailty.”[16] A six-man open-label study reported better heart-filling and blood-vessel measures.[15] The 2024 review of 19 studies found improvements in immune, heart and skin measures but none in hormone, muscle or nerve systems.[19]
  • Exercise. In RAPA-EX-01, 40 sedentary adults aged 65 to 85 did home exercise with sirolimus or placebo; both groups improved, and sensitivity analyses favored placebo.[13]
  • Brain. In a 10-person open-label trial in early Alzheimer’s or mild cognitive impairment, rapamycin was not detectable in spinal fluid, cognition did not change, and some Alzheimer’s and inflammation markers rose.[17] A 40-person phase 2 trial, REACH, is listed as active but not recruiting and has not posted results.[24]
  • Skin. A topical cream lowered a cell-aging marker in skin samples, but 19 of 36 participants dropped out.[14]

The key trials, in one chart

Our analysis: the biggest human test of the mTOR idea, a 1,024-person phase 3 trial of a related drug, did not reduce respiratory illness, though its authors, who include resTORbio employees, reported that more antiviral immune genes were switched on with the drug.[10] Of the trials that used rapamycin itself, the largest had 129 people and missed its main goal; the clearest positive results came from a skin cream with heavy dropout and a six-person open-label study.[12][24][14][15]

The case for rapamycin, in fairness

The mouse data are strong: the NIA program tested rapamycin at three independent sites in genetically mixed mice, and it worked in both sexes and even when started late.[5][6] Phase 2 trials of related drugs reported better vaccine responses and fewer reported infections in older people.[8][9] A systematic review found no serious side effects attributed to the drugs in healthy people in the trials it covered.[19] A pharmacology study found weekly dosing kept blood levels below immunosuppressive levels between doses in 13 patients.[18] Our analysis: these are reasons to run bigger trials, which is what the UT Health San Antonio and dog studies are doing, not proof that it slows human aging.[23][21] The university release puts it this way: “there’s a difference between something that is biologically plausible and something that has been rigorously tested in people.”[23]

Safety

  • Infections and cancer. The label’s boxed warning says immunosuppression can increase “susceptibility to infection and the possible development of lymphoma and other malignancies,” including serious and fatal infections; it also notes a higher skin cancer risk on immunosuppressants.[1] The review found more infections in people with age-related diseases taking these drugs.[19]
  • Lipids and blood sugar. The label lists raised cholesterol and triglycerides needing treatment, and postmarketing reports of high blood sugar and diabetes.[1] The review found higher cholesterol and triglycerides in people with age-related diseases; in the 10-person brain trial, HbA1c and blood pressure rose.[19][17]
  • Mouth sores, healing and lungs. The label lists mouth sores (stomatitis), slow wound healing and lung inflammation, some fatal.[1] Mouth sores and rash showed up in the 25-person pilot.[11]
  • Blood counts. Red-blood-cell values fell in the 25-person and 10-person trials.[11][17]
  • More adverse events in one trial. RAPA-EX-01 counted 99 adverse events on sirolimus vs 63 on placebo, including one serious pneumonia possibly related to the drug.[13]
  • Other label cautions. Avoid live vaccines; grapefruit juice must not be taken with it; it can cause fetal harm; it may lower sperm counts.[1]

Purity

Sirolimus is sold as FDA-approved brand and generic products.[2] PEARL used compounded rapamycin, and its sponsor registered a separate study comparing compounded and generic versions; that study has not posted results.[12][24] Our analysis: compounded products are not FDA-approved, so their strength and absorption cannot be assumed to match approved tablets. We do not name or rate sellers, pharmacies or telehealth services.

Legal status

In the US, sirolimus is a prescription drug approved for kidney-transplant rejection prevention, and LAM, with an IV form (FYARRO) and topical products approved for other conditions; aging and longevity are not approved uses.[1][2][3] Doctors can prescribe approved drugs off-label, which is how it is used for longevity now; the UT Health San Antonio release describes a shift away from “off-label and speculative use.”[23]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. “Extends lifespan” is a mouse result. No human trial has measured lifespan.[5][26]
  2. The biggest human trials tested different drugs. RTB101 and everolimus are related, but results do not automatically transfer.[8][9][10]
  3. Look at who ran the trial. Company employees co-wrote the RTB101 and PEARL papers, as they disclosed. That does not make the results wrong, but independent replication matters.[10][12][11][13]
  4. Surrogate measures are not outcomes. Vaccine responses, skin markers and heart-filling scores can move without changing how long or how well people live.[8][14][15]
  5. The one randomized trial pairing rapamycin with exercise pointed the wrong way.[13]

Frequently asked questions

Does rapamycin extend lifespan in humans?
Nobody knows. It extends lifespan in mice, but no human trial has measured lifespan. Human trials have been small or short and used aging-related measures.[5][26]
Is rapamycin safe for anti-aging?
Small, short trials in healthy people found few serious side effects, but one 2026 trial counted more adverse events on the drug. The FDA label warns of infections, cancer risk, high lipids and blood sugar, mouth sores and slow healing.[19][13][1]
Is rapamycin FDA approved?
Yes, as sirolimus, for preventing kidney-transplant rejection and for LAM, plus IV and topical forms for other conditions. It is not approved for aging.[1][2][3]
What did the PEARL trial find?
Its main measure, visceral fat, did not change after 48 weeks. The authors reported gains in lean mass and pain in women and similar side effects across groups.[12]
Does rapamycin help muscle or exercise in older adults?
In RAPA-EX-01, it did not add to exercise gains and may have blunted them. The 2024 review found no significant muscle effects.[13][19]
Does rapamycin weaken the immune system?
It is an immunosuppressant at transplant doses, with a boxed warning. Trials of related drugs in older people reported better vaccine responses, but phase 3 did not reduce respiratory illness.[1][8][10]
Is rapamycin being tested in larger trials?
Yes. UT Health San Antonio has a placebo-controlled trial of about 84 older adults, the TRIAD dog trial is under way, and a completed Wisconsin everolimus study has not posted results.[23][21][24]

Bottom line

Rapamycin is one of the best-supported lifespan drugs in mice.[5][6] In people, it has not been tested for lifespan, and the small aging trials give a mixed picture: some immune, skin and heart signals, missed main goals, and one trial suggesting it may blunt exercise gains.[26][10][12][13][19] As sirolimus, it is a powerful immunosuppressant with a boxed warning, and larger placebo-controlled trials are under way.[1][23]
