Home / Research / Thymosin alpha-1 teardown

Study teardown · thymosin alpha-1 (thymalfasin, sold abroad as Zadaxin) · 60 years of papers, FDA’s 2024 review, SEC filings and 60 registered trials · Sources checked October 24, 2026

Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

Thymosin alpha-1 (TA1): does it actually boost immunity, and is it FDA approved? It isn’t approved in the U.S., FDA’s advisers voted 17 to 4 against letting pharmacies compound it, and its largest trial, in 1,106 people with sepsis, found no survival benefit.

Thymosin alpha-1, often shortened to TA1 or TA-1, is a 28-amino-acid peptide first purified from calf thymus in 1977.[2] A version called thymalfasin is sold as Zadaxin in China and, by the maker’s count, more than 30 countries.[9] It is not approved in the United States.[6] In December 2024, FDA’s pharmacy compounding advisers voted 17 to 4 against adding it to the list of substances pharmacies may compound from.[5] The trials behind its reputation are mostly small, and the largest one, TESTS in sepsis, found no difference in deaths.[15] The main safety concern FDA raised for compounded versions is the immune system reacting to the peptide itself.[6][8]

Why this matters now

TA1 is promoted online as a peptide for staying well through cold and flu season, often alongside the claim that it is approved in dozens of countries. FDA’s 2024 review found compounded TA1 marketed online as injections and nasal sprays, and one website calling a compounded product “FDA-approved,” although FDA has approved no TA1 product.[6] Two new things change the picture. TESTS, the biggest TA1 trial ever run, was published in January 2025, after FDA’s review was written.[15][7] And on October 14, 2026, FDA sent an “interim access policy” for compounding certain peptides to the White House for review. It cleared that review on October 27, and the version HHS made public on October 28 lists six peptides; thymosin alpha-1 is not among them.[26] We cover that policy here.

“Fraction 5” was a partly purified calf-thymus extract containing many peptides. Thymosin alpha-1 is a different peptide from thymosin beta-4, which was sequenced separately in 1981 and is sold online as TB-500, and it is not the same product as thymalin, a Russian thymus extract.[2][27][8]

What looks promising

  • A real immune effect in some patients. In sepsis, TA1 raised a marker of immune-cell readiness (monocyte HLA-DR) more than control in ETASS.[14] In a small COVID-19 trial, CD4 T-cell counts rose faster in one subgroup.[19] These are lab markers, not proof that people do better.
  • Some positive trials. A Taiwan hepatitis B trial found more patients clearing the virus 18 months after treatment.[12] The melanoma trial found more tumor responses in two TA1 groups, and the authors called for more trials.[13]
  • Long clinical use abroad. Zadaxin is approved in China for hepatitis B and as an immune system enhancer, and it sold about $150 million a year there by 2016, according to SciClone’s filings.[9]
  • Tolerability in trials. ETASS recorded no serious drug-related side effects, no safety outcome differed between groups in TESTS, and the melanoma trial found no added toxicity.[14][15][13]

Side effects and safety so far

  • Common. Injection-site irritation, redness and discomfort are the most commonly reported side effects, according to FDA’s review.[6]
  • Reported in studies. FDA’s review lists liver enzyme (ALT) flares in people with hepatitis B, thyroid (TSH) abnormalities in people with hepatitis C, nipple pain, and fatal immune hemolytic anemia and graft failure in stem cell transplant recipients.[6] FDA also flagged a possible risk of graft-versus-host disease in people deliberately immunosuppressed after transplant.[6]
  • Foreign labels. Labels for TA1 products sold abroad carry warnings or contraindications for children, pregnancy and breastfeeding, autoimmune disease and immunosuppressed patients, according to FDA.[6]
  • Compounded and “research” products. FDA says TA1 “may pose a significant risk for immunogenicity,” meaning the immune system may react to the peptide, possibly made worse by clumping (aggregation) and impurities. It also judged TA1 not well characterized for compounding.[6][8] Those concerns apply to versions made outside an approved process. A trial result says nothing about what is in a vial bought online.
  • Prevention in healthy people. In the only prevention data we found, a retrospective review of 435 hospital staff in Wuhan, “thymosin drugs” had “an insignificant effect,” and side effects increased, especially when combined with interferon.[20]

The sepsis test: the biggest trial found no benefit

Sepsis is where TA1 got its most rigorous test. Both major trials were led by Sun Yat-sen University in China.[14][15]

  • ETASS (2013) randomized 361 people at six hospitals. Deaths at 28 days were 26.0% with TA1 and 35.0% without it, a result the authors called marginal (P = 0.062).[14]
  • TESTS (2025) randomized 1,106 people at 22 centres, with a placebo and double blinding. Deaths were 23.4% with TA1 and 24.1% with placebo (hazard ratio 0.99).[15] The authors concluded there was “no clear evidence” that TA1 lowers 28-day deaths.
  • Subgroups point both ways. In TESTS, people under 60 did worse on TA1 (HR 1.67), while people with diabetes did better (HR 0.58).[15] Subgroup findings like these need confirmation before anyone acts on them.
  • Pooled data. A 2025 meta-analysis of 11 trials found fewer deaths overall, but not when it kept only the high-quality or multicentre trials. Its own analysis said the total evidence is still too small.[16]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the cited sources.

  1. The gap shrank as the trials got bigger. ETASS showed a 9-point gap in deaths. TESTS, about three times larger and placebo controlled, showed 0.7 points.[14][15] This pattern, where small early trials look better than large later ones, is common in medicine.
  2. FDA voted without the best trial. FDA’s briefing and the December 2024 vote came before TESTS was published in January 2025.[5][7][15] TESTS doesn’t help the case for TA1 in sepsis. FDA’s review weighed effectiveness across all the evaluated uses, not sepsis alone.[6]
  3. The maker was involved in TESTS, and it was still null. SciClone is listed as a collaborator on the TESTS registry record, and the paper reports SciClone support and payments to several authors.[15] A null result from a trial the maker supported is worth noting, because it is the opposite of what a sponsor would hope for.
  4. “Approved in 30+ countries” comes from the maker. The figure is SciClone’s own statement.[9] FDA said it could not independently verify those approvals in every country, and that TA1 is not approved in the U.S., Japan or Europe except Italy.[6] Foreign approval for hepatitis B or as a hospital immune booster is also not evidence that it prevents colds in healthy people.
  5. One market carried the drug. About 95% to 97% of SciClone’s revenue came from China in 2014 to 2016.[9] Of the 60 registered TA1 trials we found, 37 have a site in China and 7 have one in the U.S.[25]
  6. Few results, many trials. Only 2 of those 60 records have results posted on the registry. Of the 15 that are active or planned, 9 are in cancer.[25]

Does it prevent colds, flu or COVID?

We found no randomized trial showing that TA1 prevents colds or flu in healthy adults. In the registry, only 2 of 60 TA1 trials accept healthy volunteers. One, a 2020 Chinese study of nasal interferon drops in medical staff, lists TA1 as an intervention but has an unknown status and no posted results.[24][25] The other, a U.S. phase 1 trial in older adults getting COVID boosters, is mainly measuring side effects and is due to finish in December 2026.[22]

The vaccine idea is older. A 2007 review reported better vaccine responses in older adults given TA1 with flu shots.[21] FDA found the vaccine evidence insufficient, noting the studies were exploratory and didn’t compare against today’s high-dose or adjuvanted flu vaccines for older adults.[6] For COVID-19 treatment in hospital, an early retrospective study from Wuhan reported fewer deaths, but it was not randomized.[17] A 2023 meta-analysis of 8 studies reported fewer deaths (risk ratio 0.59), but the studies disagreed sharply with each other, and its authors said randomized trials are still needed.[18] FDA noted that three of four meta-analyses it reviewed found no drop in deaths, and the one U.S. randomized pilot found no significant difference.[6][19]

Is it legal for a U.S. pharmacy to make it?

TA1 isn’t FDA approved, isn’t on the 503A bulks list, and FDA’s advisers voted against adding it.[5][6] FDA lists it among substances that were “nominated but withdrawn” from its category 2 safety list by the people who nominated it, and it keeps a safety warning on that listing.[8] In our reading, that leaves no clear federal path for a pharmacy to compound it from bulk powder today. This is our analysis, not legal advice. The interim policy HHS made public on October 28 does not list TA1.[26]

Frequently asked questions

Is thymosin alpha-1 FDA approved?
No. FDA has approved no drug containing thymosin alpha-1, and FDA notes that a website advertising a compounded version as “FDA-approved” was wrong.[6] It is sold as Zadaxin in China and other countries, and FDA says it is not approved in Japan or Europe, except Italy.[6][9]
Can pharmacies compound thymosin alpha-1?
In December 2024, FDA’s compounding advisers voted 17 to 4 against adding it to the 503A bulks list. FDA now lists it as “nominated but withdrawn” from its category 2 list, with a warning about immune reactions.[5][8] FDA’s interim policy on compounding certain peptides, made public on October 28, does not list it.[26]
Does thymosin alpha-1 prevent colds or flu?
There’s no good evidence it does. We found no randomized trial in healthy adults showing it prevents colds or flu. A retrospective study of 435 hospital staff found that thymosin drugs had “an insignificant effect” against COVID-19, with more side effects.[20][25]
Is thymosin alpha-1 the same as TB-500 or thymalin?
No. Thymosin alpha-1 and thymosin beta-4 are different thymus peptides, sequenced in 1977 and 1981. FDA describes TB-500 as a thymosin beta-4 fragment.[2][27][8] Thymalin is a Russian thymus extract. See our TB-500 teardown and thymalin teardown.
What are the side effects of thymosin alpha-1?
Injection-site irritation is the most common. FDA’s review also lists liver enzyme flares, thyroid test changes, and rare serious immune problems in stem cell transplant patients. Foreign labels warn about use in pregnancy, autoimmune disease and immunosuppression.[6]
Does thymosin alpha-1 help in sepsis?
The largest trial says no. TESTS randomized 1,106 people with sepsis, and deaths at 28 days were 23.4% on TA1 versus 24.1% on placebo.[15]

Bottom line

Thymosin alpha-1 has a real scientific history and decades of use as a prescription drug abroad.[2][9] But its evidence comes mostly from small trials, and the largest, best-designed trial found no survival benefit in sepsis.[15][16] FDA found no good evidence for any of the 13 uses it reviewed, its advisers voted against compounding it, and FDA warns about immune reactions to the peptide.[5][6][8] We found no trial showing it keeps healthy people from getting sick.[25] Claims that it does go further than the evidence.
