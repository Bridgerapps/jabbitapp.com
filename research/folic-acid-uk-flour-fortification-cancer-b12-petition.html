Home / Research / Folic acid in UK flour

Evidence review · Folic acid, folate, vitamin B9 · UK mandatory flour fortification from December 13, 2026 · Parliament petition 769589 · cancer, vitamin B12, unmetabolised folic acid · UK regulations, government impact assessment, SACN and FSS reports, randomized trials, ClinicalTrials.gov · Sources checked October 20, 2026

Published October 20, 2026 · Updated October 20, 2026

Will folic acid in UK flour cause cancer or hide B12 deficiency? Probably not at this dose: flour will add about 100 micrograms a day, a tenth of the upper limit, and the worrying studies used pills at 8 to 10 times that.

From December 13, UK millers must add 250 micrograms of folic acid to every 100 grams of non-wholemeal wheat flour, to prevent spina bifida and other neural tube defects (NTDs).[7][8] A petition to stop it has 117,907 signatures, enough for Parliament to consider a debate.[3] We traced the viral claims to their studies. The cancer and B12 worries come from pill trials at 800 to 1,000 micrograms a day.[29][27][37] By our math from the UK diet survey, flour will add about 100 micrograms a day on average.[14] That is a quarter of the standard pregnancy supplement and a tenth of the upper limit. Pooled trials of about 50,000 people found no overall rise in cancer, and B12 deficiency didn’t rise after the US fortified.[30][35] Some real unknowns remain, and the UK skipped one safeguard its advisers asked for.[13][11]

Why this matters now

The petition, started by Dr Clare Craig, passed 100,000 signatures on October 8 and stood at 117,907 at 10:21 AM ET on October 20. No debate date has been set, and the government already answered it in June.[3][4] The change takes effect in under eight weeks. Over the past week X has run a string of trending stories about it, from the petition to a viral photo of Brussels sprouts whose label lists “folic acid.”[1] English posts on X mentioning folic acid peaked at 945 on October 15 and totalled 4,626 in a week.[2] Two of the most-shared posts make specific claims. Dr Craig says folic acid “must be avoided by millions,” and former MP Andrew Bridgen says it brings “a 20% increased cancer risk” at an average dose of “250 micrograms per day.”[5][6] Those claims can be checked against the papers.

The short answer

  • The dose is small. 250 micrograms is the amount per 100 grams of flour, not per day. Average intake works out to about 100 micrograms a day.[7][14]
  • The benefit is real but modest. Folic acid before pregnancy prevents most NTDs in trials.[22][24] The UK expects fortification to cut them by about 19%, mostly as fewer terminations of affected pregnancies.[11]
  • The cancer signals come from pills at higher doses. They appeared at 800 to 1,000 micrograms a day and weren’t confirmed when 13 trials were pooled.[29][27][30]
  • B12 masking is a high-dose problem. The 1 milligram upper limit exists for it. Flour stays far below that, and the US saw no rise in masked B12 deficiency.[15][13][35]
  • Some questions are open. No one knows if unmetabolised folic acid in the blood matters. The UK also didn’t adopt its advisers’ condition of capping voluntary fortification of other foods.[13][11]

What the law actually does

The England regulations were made on November 13, 2024 and laid before Parliament the next day, under the Food Safety Act 1990. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have matching rules.[7][8] From December 13, 2026, non-wholemeal common wheat flour must contain 0.250 mg (250 µg) of folic acid per 100 g, “regardless of the natural level of folate in the flour.”[7][9] The rules apply to flour milled in or imported into the UK, which already must carry added calcium, iron, niacin and thiamin. They also change the required amounts of calcium carbonate, iron and niacin.[9][7]

Wholemeal flour is exempt, and so are small mills producing under 500 tonnes a year. Gluten-free, spelt and soya flours aren’t covered, and neither are imported foods made with flour.[7][9][4] Added folic acid must be named in the ingredients list, including when fortified flour is an ingredient.[4] Ministers made the change as secondary legislation, laid before Parliament rather than voted through as a bill. Scotland’s version states it was “subject to negative procedure,” which means it stands unless members vote to annul it.[7][8]

About the sprouts. UK nutrition labels must use the name “Folic acid” for vitamin B9, with a reference value of 200 µg.[17] A sprouts pack listing 135 µg “folic acid” at 68% of that value is describing the vegetable’s own folate (135 ÷ 200 = 68%), not an additive.[1][17] The ingredients list is where added folic acid has to appear.[4]

How much folic acid you’d actually get

We started from the UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey figures in Food Standards Scotland’s 2017 modelling. Average non-wholemeal flour intake is 59 g a day, from 48 g for women aged 35 to 49 to 82 g for teenage boys.[14] At 2.5 µg per gram, 59 g holds 148 µg. The modelling assumes 25% is lost in processing and that 11.3% of flour and flour foods are imported and unfortified. On those assumptions people actually eat about 98 µg a day. That matches the model’s own figure of 97.5 µg.[14]

To reach the 1,000 µg upper level from flour alone, an adult would need to eat about 600 g of fortified flour a day, roughly 10 times the average.[14] The real risk of going over is from stacking sources. People already average 79 µg a day of folic acid from fortified cereals, spreads and supplements, and the model puts the total at 176 µg after fortification.[14] About 0.42% of people are over the limit today. All 38 such people in the survey would drop below it without their main source, such as a supplement. The government estimates the share over the limit will rise to about 0.64%.[14][11]

So the claim that the average dose will be “250 micrograms per day” mixes up the amount per 100 g of flour with a daily dose. The real figure is closer to 100.[6][7][14]

The case for it

The trial evidence is strong. In the 1991 MRC Vitamin Study, women who had already had an affected pregnancy took 4 mg of folic acid or not. There were 6 NTDs in the folic acid groups and 21 in the others, a 72% reduction.[22][37] A Hungarian trial of a multivitamin with 0.8 mg prevented first occurrences: 0 vs 6 cases.[23] A Cochrane review of 5 trials found folic acid cut NTD risk by about 69%, rated high-quality evidence.[24]

Fortification worked elsewhere. After Canada fortified white flour in 1998, NTDs fell 46%, from 1.58 to 0.86 per 1,000 births. The biggest drops came in provinces with the highest rates.[25] In the US, anencephaly and spina bifida fell 28% overall, 35% where programs counted prenatal diagnoses. That is about 1,326 fewer affected births a year.[26]

Why the UK says it needs it. About half of UK pregnancies are unplanned, and only about one in five women take folic acid before pregnancy.[12][4] The national diet survey found 89% of women aged 16 to 49 had blood folate levels that may raise NTD risk, and their folate intakes fell from 2008 to 2019.[12]

What “200 cases” means. The government says fortification will prevent about 200 NTD-affected pregnancies a year, about 20% of UK cases.[10][4] Its impact assessment breaks the England figures down. About 112 fewer terminations are expected each year, out of 591, along with 31 fewer babies born with an NTD and 6 fewer miscarriages.[11] Termination is the most common outcome of an affected pregnancy, so most of the “cases” prevented would be terminations that are no longer needed.[11] The 19% is an average of two models, 15.4% and 22.4%. That is well below what Canada and the US saw, even though the UK level is higher.[11][25][26]

“A 20% increased cancer risk.” Mr Bridgen’s post cites no study.[6] The closest match we found is two Norwegian trials in 6,837 heart patients. They took 800 µg of folic acid plus vitamin B12 for a median of 39 months. Over about six years, 10.0% got cancer vs 8.4% without folic acid (hazard ratio 1.21), driven mainly by lung cancer. Norway didn’t fortify food.[29] Those investigators then joined a pooled analysis of 13 trials covering 49,621 people over an average of 5.2 years, including the US polyp trial below. Folic acid quadrupled blood levels but didn’t significantly change cancer rates: 1,904 vs 1,809 cancers (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.99–1.13). It had no significant effect on any single site, including bowel, prostate, lung and breast.[30] The authors note fortification doses are “on average, an order of magnitude smaller.”[30]

Polyps and prostate cancer. The strongest single warning comes from a US trial of 1 mg a day in 1,021 people with previous bowel polyps. Folic acid didn’t prevent polyps. At the second check, 11.6% had an advanced lesion vs 6.9% on placebo, a borderline result (P = .05).[27] Among the trial’s 643 men, 9.7% on folic acid were diagnosed with prostate cancer within 10 years vs 3.3% on placebo.[28] The UK’s Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition (SACN) calls the prostate evidence “inconclusive.” It concluded that, overall, the data “do not suggest a detrimental effect,” while noting the trials were generally underpowered.[13]

The trend-line argument. A 2007 paper noticed that US and Canadian bowel cancer rates stopped falling around 1996–98, when fortification began, and ran 4 to 6 cases per 100,000 above trend. They found changes in colonoscopy use didn’t seem to explain it. Still, they called it a hypothesis and wrote that the observations alone “do not prove causality.”[31] A Chilean study found colon cancer hospital discharges were 2.6 to 2.9 times higher in 2001–04 than in 1992–96, after fortification at 220 µg per 100 g. Hospital discharges aren’t new diagnoses, though, and the study couldn’t separate fortification from a decade of other changes.[32] SACN’s 2017 review cites an Irish assessment that US rates of all cancers, bowel and prostate cancer had not significantly increased since fortification.[13]

Masking B12 deficiency. This concern is the reason for the upper limit. High-dose folic acid can fix the anaemia of B12 deficiency while nerve damage continues unnoticed. The UK’s 1 mg a day upper level was set by its Expert Group on Vitamins and Minerals in 2003 and reaffirmed by its toxicology committee in 2018.[15][11] The US also uses 1 mg. Its limit rests on 23 studies of patients with pernicious anaemia, mostly from the 1950s, and 11 of them were single-patient case reports. Nicholas Wald and colleagues argue the limit should be scrapped.[37] SACN says the masking evidence comes mainly from doses over 1 mg a day.[13] After the US fortified, the share of adults over 50 with B12 deficiency but no anaemia, the masked kind, stayed flat: 4.0% before and 3.9% after.[35] The open question is narrower. In US seniors with low B12, high blood folate went with more anaemia (OR 3.1) and cognitive impairment (OR 2.6). That is an observational link that can’t show cause.[33]

Unmetabolised folic acid (UMFA). It is real and common. It is detectable in more than 95% of Americans.[36] In UK diet survey samples taken before mandatory fortification, it was below detection in only about a third of adults (32.6% aged 19 to 64), so roughly two-thirds already had it. SACN says UMFA makes up 1–3% of total blood folate.[13] SACN found no clear dose-response between folic acid intake and UMFA, and “insufficient data” to say whether it causes harm.[13] The best-known worry comes from a US study of seniors with low B12, where detectable UMFA went with lower cognitive test scores.[34]

“Must be avoided by millions.” The petition cites an NHS page listing people with cancer, a stent or low B12.[3][5] That page is about tablets. It says folic acid tablets “may not be suitable” for people with cancer, a coronary stent or B12 deficiency anaemia. It also says it “does not cover folic acid that has been added to food,” which is “much lower” than in supplements.[16]

Epilepsy and cancer drugs, MTHFR and miscarriage. The NHS lists folic acid tablets as possibly interacting with phenytoin, phenobarbital and primidone.[16] In the 2021 consultation, 38 respondents raised drug interactions, including lamotrigine and methotrexate. The government said the folic acid levels in fortified foods are “not enough to reach the tolerable upper level alone.” It recommends that people on medication speak to their GP or a healthcare professional if they’re concerned.[12] On MTHFR gene variants, the government accepted that carriers process folate less well. It said extra folic acid still raises their blood folate.[12] On pregnancy loss, the Cochrane review found no clear effect on miscarriage from folic acid supplements (RR 1.10, 95% CI 0.94–1.28, 5 trials).[24]

Consent. This part is a values question, not a lab result. The petition calls folic acid “a medicine not a food” and calls fortification mass medication without consent.[3] The petition also says wholemeal and gluten-free products are increasingly adding folic acid voluntarily, which makes it harder to avoid.[3] The government calls it “an evidenced public health measure, not a medical intervention,” and points to unfortified wholemeal and non-wheat flours as the opt-out.[4] An earlier petition asking for at least one unfortified white flour drew 24,140 signatures.[3]

The safeguard the UK didn’t adopt

SACN backed mandatory fortification in 2006, 2009 and again in 2017. Each time it said it “should only be introduced alongside restrictions on voluntary dietary fortification,” so that no more people end up over the upper limit.[13] The 2021 consultation response left limits on voluntary fortification to “a later date.”[12] The 2024 regulations change flour only, and the government’s impact assessment works on the basis of no restrictions. Its own table shows the difference. With caps on fortified cereals, spreads and supplements, the share of people over the limit would be about 0.07%. Without them, it is 0.64%.[7][11] In both cases that is a small minority, mostly people taking supplements.[14] But it means the policy that starts in December is not quite the one SACN set out.

The UK level is higher than the US or Canadian level but within the global range of about 50 to 330 µg per 100 g.[20] The US also fortifies many other enriched grain products, such as bread, so its total exposure isn’t comparable gram for gram.[18][26] The Food Fortification Initiative counts 85 countries that require wheat flour fortification. In 2002 only 38 countries required fortification of any grain.[20] How many require folic acid depends on the source. FFI’s fact sheet says 81 of 86, using 2017 legislation data, and a 2025 preprint citing the Global Fortification Data Exchange counts 70.[20][21]

What is being studied

Not much on the open questions. ClinicalTrials.gov lists 10 studies that mention UMFA. All measure it, or folate, in blood. One has posted results, and none registers a cancer, cognitive or dementia outcome.[38] Of 88 registered studies pairing folic acid with fortification, mostly iron and multi-nutrient programs abroad, 3 test fortified flour or bread. All 3 measure blood levels.[38] PubMed has 124 papers on UMFA, only 17 of them tagged as randomized trials.[39] The government plans to watch NTD rates, blood folate, B12 status and cancer registries. It concedes that cancer trends will be hard to pin on any one cause.[11]

What to do with this

  • If you could become pregnant, UK advice hasn’t changed: 400 µg of folic acid a day before conception and for the first 12 weeks. Fortified flour adds roughly a quarter of that.[12][4][14]
  • If you want to avoid it, the government points to wholemeal flour, gluten-free products and non-wheat flours. Read the ingredients list, where added folic acid must be named.[4]
  • If you take epilepsy, cancer or other long-term drugs, the official advice is to speak to your GP or a healthcare professional about interactions.[12][4]
  • If you take folic acid supplements on top, remember that stacking sources, not bread, is what pushes people over the 1 mg upper level.[14][15]
  • Fortification doesn’t replace a B12 diagnosis. Low B12 can damage nerves whatever your folate level, and it needs testing and treatment.[11][13]

Strengths and limits

Strengths. The benefit side rests on randomized trials and large national registries.[22][24][25][26] The safety side includes a pooled analysis of about 50,000 people.[30] Our intake math uses the government’s own survey data and assumptions, and it reproduces the model’s published figure.[14]

Limits. The cancer trials tested pills at higher doses for about five years, not lifetime exposure from food.[30] The B12 and UMFA evidence is mostly observational.[33][34][35] The UK forecast is a model with a wide range.[11] The diet survey intake data predate 2017, and people who eat far more bread than average will get proportionally more.[14] We didn’t check the trend data behind every country’s program.[20]

Bottom line

At the UK’s dose, flour will add about 100 micrograms of folic acid a day. That is enough to cut neural tube defects modestly, by about a fifth in the government’s forecast, and far below the levels where cancer or B12 signals appeared.[14][11][29][27] The “20% cancer risk” and “avoid it” claims rely on pill trials and a supplements page.[6][5][16] What’s honestly unknown is the long-term meaning of unmetabolised folic acid, and whether skipping SACN’s cap on other fortified foods will matter.[13][11] Whether the trade is worth it for everyone else is a values call that Parliament may now debate.[3]

Frequently asked questions

When does folic acid go into UK flour, and which flour?
From December 13, 2026, millers in all four UK nations must add 250 micrograms of folic acid to every 100 grams of non-wholemeal wheat flour, the white and brown flour already fortified with calcium, iron, niacin and thiamin. Wholemeal flour, non-wheat flours and flour from small mills producing under 500 tonnes a year are exempt. Imported flour must be fortified, but imported foods made with flour are not covered. Folic acid must appear in the ingredients list.
How much folic acid will I actually get from bread and flour?
About 100 micrograms a day on average. UK diet survey data put average non-wholemeal flour intake at 59 grams a day, and official modelling assumes about a quarter is lost in processing and 11% of flour foods are imported unfortified. That gives roughly 80 to 140 micrograms a day across age groups, a quarter of the 400 microgram pregnancy supplement and about a tenth of the 1,000 microgram adult upper level.
Does folic acid in flour cause cancer?
No large study shows that it does at flour doses. The concern comes from pill trials at 800 to 1,000 micrograms a day: a Norwegian trial in heart patients found 10.0% vs 8.4% got cancer, and a US polyp trial found more advanced polyps and prostate cancer. When 13 trials with about 50,000 people were pooled, folic acid didn’t significantly raise cancer rates. The UK’s nutrition advisers say the data don’t suggest harm but note the trials had limited power.
Can folic acid in flour hide vitamin B12 deficiency?
At high doses, folic acid can correct the anaemia of B12 deficiency while nerve damage continues, which is why the UK and US set a 1 milligram a day upper level. Flour adds about a tenth of that. In the US, the share of older adults with B12 deficiency but no anaemia, the masked kind, didn’t rise after fortification. People with low B12 still need it diagnosed and treated.
What is unmetabolised folic acid (UMFA), and is it harmful?
It is folic acid that circulates in the blood before the body converts it. It is detectable in more than 95% of Americans and, before mandatory fortification, in about two-thirds of UK adults tested. UK nutrition advisers found no clear link between intake and UMFA levels and not enough data to say whether it causes harm. One US study linked it to lower test scores in seniors with low B12, which can’t show cause.
Can I avoid fortified flour, and should women still take a folic acid supplement?
To avoid it, the government points to wholemeal flour, gluten-free products and non-wheat flours such as spelt or soya, and to the ingredients list, where folic acid must be named. People on medication who are worried are advised to speak to their GP or a healthcare professional. UK advice for anyone who could become pregnant is unchanged: 400 micrograms of folic acid a day before conception and for the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, or a higher dose if a doctor advises it because of a higher risk of an affected pregnancy.
