Home / Research / Senolytics teardown

Study teardown · Senolytics: dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) and fisetin · The mouse lifespan studies, every human trial with published or posted results that we found, two 2026 reviews, the dasatinib label, FDA’s supplement rules, registry records and PubMed counts · Sources checked October 26, 2026

Published October 26, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026

Senolytics (fisetin, dasatinib plus quercetin): do the “zombie cell” drugs slow aging, and are they safe? They extended life in old mice, but no human trial has tested lifespan. The human trials are small, and most missed their main goals or had no comparison group; one placebo-controlled trial, in fatty liver disease, published October 2026, met its main goal. Dasatinib is a prescription cancer drug with serious label warnings.

Senolytics are drugs meant to clear senescent cells, the damaged “zombie” cells that stop dividing but linger and release inflammatory signals as we age.[3][4] The best-known are dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q), a prescription leukemia drug paired with a plant flavonoid, and fisetin, another flavonoid sold as a supplement.[4][6][1] Do they work? In old mice, D+Q increased post-treatment survival and fisetin extended median and maximum lifespan, but no human trial has tested lifespan.[5][6][22] The seven human trials in our chart enrolled 5 to 75 people; three had no comparison group, a bone trial and a fisetin knee trial found no main benefit, and one placebo-controlled trial in 31 people with fatty liver disease, published in October 2026, met its main goal.[8][9][10][11][13][19][15] Are they safe? Side effects were more common on D+Q than placebo, and dasatinib’s label lists serious risks, including low blood counts, bleeding and fluid around the lungs.[15][10][1]

Why this matters now

On October 1, 2026, Nature Metabolism published an Amsterdam UMC-led, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial of D+Q in 31 people with fibrotic MASH (fatty liver disease with scarring). On repeat liver biopsies, 47% improved by at least one fibrosis stage on D+Q, versus 7% on placebo; adverse events were also more common on D+Q (82% vs 43%), though the authors describe them as self-limiting.[15] Our analysis: it is the first placebo-controlled senolytic trial we found that met its main clinical goal. It is also small, and the authors call it “proof-of-principle.”[15] Days later, a systematic review in the British Journal of Clinical Pharmacology concluded that senolytics “should therefore be considered investigational rather than established therapies.”[16] Results are still pending from Mayo Clinic’s fisetin AFFIRM trials, registered in 2018, and from a completed 120-person Danish osteoporosis study.[20]

What senolytics are

As cells age or get damaged, some enter senescence: they stop dividing but do not die, and they release inflammatory signals. In mice, clearing them delayed or eased many age-related problems, which is the idea behind senolytics.[3] Dasatinib is a kinase inhibitor approved for certain leukemias; quercetin and fisetin are plant flavonoids.[1][4][6] The combination was chosen because, in lab tests, dasatinib cleared one type of senescent cell and quercetin another.[4] The drugs are given intermittently, in short bursts rather than daily, because the aim is to clear cells rather than block a process continuously.[5][10] Patents on senolytic drugs are held by Mayo Clinic, according to disclosures in several of the trial papers.[5][9][13]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • Mice. In naturally aged mice, intermittent D+Q eased physical decline and increased post-treatment survival by 36%; fisetin given late in life extended median and maximum lifespan.[5][6] A 2025 study by a separate team, reporting no conflicts, found fisetin improved frailty and grip strength in old mice, with no effect in young mice.[7]
  • People: lifespan. We found no human trial that measured whether senolytics extend life.[22]
  • Do they clear senescent cells in people? In 9 people with diabetic kidney disease, D+Q reduced senescent cells in fat within 11 days, and skin markers and inflammatory blood factors fell; there was no comparison group.[9]
  • Lung fibrosis. In a 14-person open-label pilot, walking and chair-stand tests improved, while lung function and frailty were unchanged.[8] A 12-person placebo-controlled pilot found the drugs feasible, but the measures “do not appear to differ meaningfully between groups.”[10]
  • Alzheimer’s disease. In a 5-person open-label pilot, dasatinib reached spinal fluid in four people but quercetin was not detected; thinking and brain-imaging measures did not change, and some inflammation markers in spinal fluid went up.[11][12] A 48-person phase 2 trial is under way.[20]
  • Bone. In 60 postmenopausal women, D+Q did not change the main bone-breakdown marker; a bone-formation marker rose briefly; in an exploratory subgroup with the most senescent cells, wrist bone density rose 2.7%.[13] The trial’s registry also lists a fisetin group, with no statistical comparison to control posted.[14]
  • Fatty liver (MASH). In 31 people, D+Q improved liver scarring on biopsy more often than placebo, 47% vs 7%.[15]
  • Fisetin. In 75 people with knee arthritis, the posted results show no significant difference from placebo on any outcome.[19] A 2026 review counted 34 registered fisetin trials, only 4 completed with results, and called the evidence “limited and heterogeneous.”[17] Small trials in obese men measured inflammation markers, not aging outcomes.[18]

The key trials, in one chart

Our analysis: after a decade, the human evidence is seven small trials, three without any comparison group. Of the four trials with a comparison group, one met its main goal, the October 2026 liver trial.[10][13][19][15] Both 2026 reviews describe the evidence as limited, and one says senolytics are investigational.[16][17] The liver result is a disease-treatment signal, not evidence that senolytics slow aging.

The case for senolytics, in fairness

The mouse data are unusually strong for a longevity idea: two different senolytics extended survival or lifespan in old animals, and in 2025 a separate team, reporting no conflicts, also found fisetin eased frailty in old mice.[5][6][7] The first human pilot showed the drugs can reduce senescent cells in people.[9] The October 2026 liver trial was double-blind and placebo-controlled, with a biopsy-based main goal, and it was positive.[15] The bone trial hints that people with more senescent cells may respond better, which the authors say should be tested.[13] Our analysis: these are reasons to run larger trials, which researchers are doing, not proof that senolytics slow human aging.[20]

Safety

  • Dasatinib is a cancer drug. Its label lists low blood counts and bleeding, fluid retention including fluid around the lungs, pulmonary arterial hypertension, QT prolongation, severe skin reactions, liver monitoring and harm to a fetus, and says to avoid strong CYP3A4 inhibitors and grapefruit juice.[1]
  • Side effects in trials. In the liver trial, 82% had adverse events on D+Q vs 43% on placebo, all described as self-limiting; in the 12-person lung trial, non-serious events were more common on D+Q (65 vs 22), and sleep problems and anxiety occurred in 4 of 6 on D+Q vs 0 of 6 on placebo.[15][10] One serious adverse event was reported in the 14-person open-label pilot; the bone trial reported none.[8][13]
  • Fisetin. In the knee trial, adverse events were about as common on fisetin as placebo, with 2 serious events in each group.[19]
  • What is unknown. The trials were short and small, and the 2026 systematic review says long-term safety is not established.[16]

Purity

Quercetin and fisetin are sold as dietary supplements, which the FDA does not approve before they are marketed.[2] Our analysis: supplement products are not the same as the study drugs used in trials, and results from trials do not automatically apply to them. Dasatinib is a prescription medicine.[1] We do not name or rate brands or sellers.

Legal status

In the US, no senolytic is approved to treat aging or any age-related disease. Dasatinib is approved for certain leukemias, and its use for other purposes would be off-label.[1] Quercetin and fisetin are sold as supplements, which FDA does not approve before sale.[2]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. “Extends lifespan” is a mouse result. No human trial has measured lifespan.[5][6][22]
  2. Check for a comparison group. Three of the seven trials in our chart had none, so their improvements could reflect chance or expectation.[8][9][11]
  3. Look for the main goal. The bone trial missed its main goal, and its positive finding came from an exploratory subgroup.[13]
  4. Most registered trials have not reported. Of 55 ClinicalTrials.gov records we found testing fisetin or D+Q, 8 have posted results, and some test the drugs in cancer or other diseases rather than aging.[21]
  5. One positive trial is a start. The liver trial was in 31 people with a specific disease, and the authors call for further trials.[15]
  6. Read the disclosures. Several trial papers report authors with financial interests in senolytic patents held by Mayo Clinic; that does not make the results wrong, but independent replication matters.[10][11][13][15]

Frequently asked questions

What are senolytics?
Drugs meant to clear senescent cells, damaged cells that stop dividing and release inflammatory signals. The best-known are dasatinib plus quercetin and fisetin.[3][4][6]
Do senolytics extend lifespan?
In old mice, yes: D+Q increased post-treatment survival and fisetin extended lifespan. No human trial has measured lifespan.[5][6][22]
Is dasatinib plus quercetin safe?
Dasatinib is a prescription leukemia drug whose label warns of low blood counts, bleeding, fluid around the lungs and other risks. In trials, side effects were more common on D+Q than placebo.[1][15][10]
Does fisetin work in people?
Not shown yet. A 75-person knee-arthritis trial found no difference from placebo, and Mayo Clinic’s AFFIRM fisetin trials have posted no results.[19][20][17]
Are senolytics FDA approved?
Not for aging. Dasatinib is approved for certain leukemias; quercetin and fisetin are supplements, which FDA does not approve before sale.[1][2]
What did the October 2026 fatty-liver trial find?
In 31 people with fibrotic MASH, 47% on D+Q improved their liver scarring vs 7% on placebo, with more side effects on D+Q.[15]
Who holds the senolytic patents?
Several trial papers disclose that patents on senolytic drugs are held by Mayo Clinic, and some authors disclose a financial interest in them.[5][13][15]

Bottom line

Senolytics have some of the strongest mouse lifespan data in the longevity field.[5][6] In people, the trials are small; most missed their main goals or had no comparison group, and the October 2026 liver trial is the first clear placebo-controlled win we found.[13][19][10][15] Dasatinib carries real drug risks, and reviewers call senolytics investigational.[1][16]
