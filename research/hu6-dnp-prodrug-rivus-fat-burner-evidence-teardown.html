Evidence teardown · Fat burners · Experimental drugs · Updated October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

What is HU6, and is it a “safe DNP”? HU6 is an experimental Rivus Pharmaceuticals pill that the liver turns into DNP, the 1930s diet drug that still kills people. In supervised trials of up to six months it cut liver fat by roughly 27% to 36% but body weight by under 3 kg, and no treatment-related serious side effects were reported. It is not approved, and no study shows that safety record would hold outside a trial.

HU6 is having a moment online as the “safe” version of DNP (2,4-dinitrophenol), a chemical that melts fat by making cells burn energy as heat, and that has killed people since the 1930s. The reader’s question: is HU6 actually a safe DNP, and does it work? Our plain answer: HU6 is a real drug in real, placebo-controlled trials, and in those trials it lowered liver fat a lot and body weight a little, without the disasters DNP is known for. But every one of those trials was run by its maker, under medical supervision, and HU6 is not approved or legally sold. The safety record belongs to the trial setting, not to anything bought online.

Why this matters now

On October 21, 2026, a “Ranking fat burners” tier list on X put HU6 in its second-highest tier, next to Ozempic, and put DNP in a bottom “death tier.” It passed 1.29 million impressions.[1] In a reply, the same account summed up the appeal: “HU6 is basically like DNP but you can’t kill yourself with it.”[2] Meanwhile, HU6’s maker began a new Phase 2 trial in April 2026, and FDA warned in June 2026 that a fat burner sold online contained hidden DNP.[8][19] So what do the trials show, and what don’t they?

What HU6 is

  • A DNP prodrug. The 2023 trial paper calls HU6 “a controlled metabolic accelerator that is metabolised in the liver to the mitochondrial uncoupler 2,4-dinitrophenol.”[3] In other words, the active chemical is DNP; the design aims to control how much reaches the body and how fast.
  • A different idea from Ozempic. Drugs like semaglutide mostly cut how much people eat. HuMAIN’s designers wrote that uncouplers aim at “increased catabolism rather than reduced caloric intake.”[5] Rivus says its drugs raise metabolism in a way that is “consistent and imperceptible to the patient.”[6]
  • Still experimental. ClinicalTrials.gov lists 11 HU6 studies, all sponsored by Rivus: 6 Phase 1 and 5 Phase 2. None is Phase 3.[9] Rivus calls itself “a clinical-stage biopharmaceutical company.”[8] HU6 has no FDA approval.

What the trials show

  • Liver fat, 61 days (peer-reviewed). In 80 adults with fatty liver and a high BMI, liver fat fell 26.8% to 35.6% across three HU6 dose groups and rose 5.4% on placebo.[3]
  • Liver fat, 6 months (company-reported). In M-ACCEL, 228 adults with MASH, an inflamed form of fatty liver disease that can lead to scarring, liver fat fell 27.0% to 31.2% on HU6 vs 6.7% on placebo. Half or more of HU6 patients (50% to 58%) cut liver fat by at least 30%, vs 22% on placebo.[7] We found no peer-reviewed M-ACCEL paper in PubMed, and no results are posted on ClinicalTrials.gov.[10][9]
  • Weight: small. In HuMAIN, 66 people with obesity and heart failure lost 2.86 kg more on HU6 than on placebo over 19 weeks, almost all of it fat (2.96 kg less fat mass), “with no significant loss of muscle mass.”[4] In M-ACCEL, Rivus reports a 2.4% (2.6 kg) weight drop at the highest dose.[7]
  • No better exercise capacity. HuMAIN’s heart failure measures, including peak oxygen use and the 6-minute walk, did not change significantly.[4]

Jabbit’s analysis: on weight, HU6 is not in Ozempic’s league so far. In STEP 1, semaglutide users lost 14.9% of body weight over 68 weeks vs 2.4% on placebo.[11] HU6’s trials were shorter and in different patients, so this is not a head-to-head comparison, but the weight results reported so far are a few kilograms. HU6’s case rests on liver fat and on fat loss that spares muscle.

What the trials say about safety

  • Phase 2a: no serious adverse events and no deaths. The most common side effects on HU6 were flushing (32%), diarrhoea (25%) and palpitations (12%), vs 10%, 0% and 5% on placebo.[3] Flushing and a racing heart fit how an uncoupler works: it turns energy into heat.
  • HuMAIN: serious adverse events in 4 people on HU6 and 1 on placebo, including 1 death, all judged unrelated to treatment.[4]
  • M-ACCEL (company-reported): “no treatment-related serious adverse events,” gastrointestinal problems in 14% on HU6 vs 16% on placebo, and fewer than 5% of patients discontinuing.[6][7]
  • Scale: Rivus says more than 500 patients have been treated with HU6.[8] That is too few to rule out rare harms.

Why DNP is in the “death tier”

  • A razor-thin margin. A history of the 1930s craze calls DNP’s therapeutic index “razor thin” and says “thousands of people suffered irreversible harm.”[12] It was withdrawn because of “high toxicity, a narrow therapeutic window, and a lack of an antidote.”[18]
  • How it kills. Poisoning brings very high body temperature, a fast heart, sweating and fast breathing, “eventually leading to death.”[14] In US and UK cases, a fast heart, high fever, acidosis and confusion each predicted death.[16]
  • Who it kills. Of 204 US and UK cases, 86% were under 40 and 71% were men. About 1 in 9 reported US cases and 1 in 6 UK cases died.[16] In an earlier UK poisons-service series, the median age was 23.5 and 5 of 30 reported cases died.[17] Worldwide, 11.9% of reported cases died.[15]
  • It is still sold. FDA says DNP “is illegally marketed as a weight-loss product and is not approved by the FDA for any use,” and in June 2026 found it hidden in a fat burner sold online.[19]

Is HU6 a “safe DNP”? What the claim gets right and wrong

Jabbit’s analysis:

  • Right: HU6 is designed to deliver DNP in a controlled way, and the trials so far have not reported the heatstroke-type poisonings seen with DNP.[3][4][7]
  • Unproven: “you can’t kill yourself with it”[2] is not something any study has tested. The trials enrolled screened adults, used set amounts of a known product, and monitored them. They tell us nothing about overdose, heat, heavy exercise, or mixing HU6 with other drugs.
  • Wrong in practice: HU6 is investigational and, as far as we found, is legally available only inside Rivus’s trials.[9][8] Anything sold online under that name cannot be checked against the trial drug, and FDA has found undeclared DNP in an online fat burner this year.[19] A product labeled HU6 could contain anything, including DNP itself.

The case for HU6

It is fair to say HU6 is one of the more interesting obesity-adjacent ideas in development. It works by raising energy use rather than cutting appetite, the 2023 trial was randomized, placebo-controlled and peer-reviewed, liver fat fell substantially, and the fat-only weight loss with no significant muscle loss in HuMAIN stands out at a time when muscle loss on GLP-1 drugs is a common worry.[3][4][5] A review of DNP itself notes that its efficacy “surpasses that of many contemporary anti-obesity drugs,” which is why researchers want a safer way to use it “under controlled conditions.”[18]

What a careful reader should notice

  1. All HU6 trials are sponsored by Rivus, and the 2023 paper’s first author disclosed owning shares in Rivus. That does not make the results wrong, but it is worth knowing.[9][3]
  2. The six-month M-ACCEL results come from company releases and a conference talk, not a peer-reviewed paper we could find.[7][10]
  3. Liver fat is not body weight. The large percentages are liver fat; the weight changes were a few kilograms.[3][4][7]
  4. No HU6 trial has reached Phase 3, and the next data are expected mid-2027.[9][8]
  5. Tier lists rank by vibes. A ranking that puts an unapproved, Phase 2 drug next to Ozempic is not based on comparable evidence.[1][11]

FAQ

What is HU6?
HU6 is an experimental pill from Rivus Pharmaceuticals that the liver converts into DNP (2,4-dinitrophenol), a chemical that makes cells burn energy as heat. Rivus calls it a controlled metabolic accelerator. It has been tested in Phase 1 and Phase 2 trials, mainly for fatty liver disease, and is not approved.
Is HU6 the same as DNP?
Not exactly. HU6 is a prodrug: the body turns it into DNP. It is designed to release DNP in a controlled way, and its trials have not reported the poisonings seen with DNP. But the active chemical is the same, and the trials only tested supervised use in screened adults.
How much weight do people lose on HU6?
Not much so far. In the HuMAIN trial, people lost 2.86 kg more than on placebo over 19 weeks, almost all of it fat. Rivus reported a 2.4% weight drop at the highest dose over six months in M-ACCEL. Semaglutide’s STEP 1 trial reported 14.9% vs 2.4% over 68 weeks.
Is HU6 safe?
In trials, no serious side effects were linked to HU6. Flushing, diarrhoea and palpitations were more common than on placebo. About 500 people have been treated, too few to rule out rare harms, and nothing is known about use outside supervised trials.
Is HU6 FDA approved or available to buy?
No. HU6 is investigational and, as far as we found, legally available only in Rivus’s clinical trials. Products sold online under the name cannot be verified, and FDA has found hidden DNP in a fat burner sold online in 2026.
Why is DNP so dangerous?
DNP has a very narrow margin between an effective and a deadly amount and no antidote. Poisoning causes very high body temperature, a racing heart and fast breathing. About 12% of DNP cases reported to poisons centres in 38 countries ended in death.

Bottom line

HU6 is a serious attempt to tame DNP, and in its maker’s trials it cut liver fat substantially, trimmed a few kilograms of fat, and did not produce DNP-style poisonings. That is promising for fatty liver disease, but it is not evidence that HU6 is a strong weight-loss drug or that it is safe outside a supervised trial. Until AMPLIFY and later trials report, the honest description is: interesting, unapproved, and not something to buy.
