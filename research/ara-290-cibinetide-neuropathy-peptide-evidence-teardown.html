Home / Research / ARA-290 teardown

Study teardown · ARA-290 (cibinetide) · The animal studies, every human trial we found, the trial registry records and posted results, FDA and EU orphan designations, FDA’s compounding list, the WADA 2027 list and two 2026 reviews · Sources checked October 26, 2026

Published October 26, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026

ARA-290 (cibinetide): does the “nerve repair” peptide work, and is it safe? It eased nerve pain in rats, but in people it has been tested only in six small, short trials. Early trials in sarcoidosis and diabetes reported symptom gains; the largest met its main goal in one of three dose groups, and pain improved on placebo too. It is not approved anywhere, and we found no long-term safety data.

ARA-290, also called cibinetide, is an 11-amino-acid peptide built from part of the hormone erythropoietin (EPO). It was designed to keep EPO’s tissue-protecting effects without raising red blood cells.[2] In rats, it gave long-lasting relief of nerve pain.[3][4] Does it work in people? Six human trials, with 9 to 64 people each and 218 in all, mostly treated people for four weeks. Early trials in sarcoidosis and type 2 diabetes reported better symptom scores. In the largest, a 64-person phase 2b, the main nerve-fiber goal was met in one of three dose groups, and pain improved in every group, including placebo.[5][6][7][9] A mood study and an eye study found no benefit.[8][11] Is it safe? The short trials found no clear safety signal, but in two of them serious adverse events occurred only in people on the drug.[7][10] It is not approved anywhere, no trial is active, and FDA lists it among substances nominated for compounding “without adequate support.”[20][13][24]

Why this matters now

ARA-290 is now being grouped with BPC-157, TB-500 and GHK-Cu as a “regenerative peptide.” In August 2026, a review by authors including Brigham and Women’s Hospital affiliations of these peptides for chronic pain concluded that human evidence is limited and that most remain unapproved by FDA.[17] On October 15, 2026, a review of jaw nerve injury described three uncontrolled cases treated with a mix that included ARA-290; its authors say “causality cannot be established” and call peptide treatments “investigational.”[18] FDA’s compounding list, updated in May 2026, places cibinetide in its “without adequate support” category, and WADA’s 2027 list, in force January 1, 2027, bans the drug class it belongs to.[24][25][19] Our analysis: the human trials behind the interest are a decade old, and none has been followed by a larger trial.[13][14]

What ARA-290 is

EPO is the kidney hormone that tells bone marrow to make red blood cells, and it also protects some tissues from injury. Making more red cells is a problem for that second use, because it can thicken the blood. In 2004 and 2008, researchers reported modified EPO and then a short EPO fragment that kept tissue protection in animals but did not raise red cells.[1][2] The fragment, ARA 290, is thought to act on the “innate repair receptor,” a pairing of the EPO receptor with the β-common receptor (CD131). In mice lacking the β-common receptor, it had no pain-relieving effect.[3][16] Its developer, Araim Pharmaceuticals, holds the FDA and EU orphan designations, and several authors of the trial papers disclose that they were Araim officers or employees with stock or stock options.[20][23][5][6][7]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • Animals. In rats with nerve injury, ARA 290 relieved pain for up to 15 weeks, and a second rat study reported up to 20 weeks.[3][4]
  • Sarcoidosis pilot (2012). In 22 people with sarcoidosis and small fiber neuropathy, one symptom checklist improved more on ARA 290 than placebo over 4 weeks. Pain and fatigue scores “improved significantly but equivalently in both patient groups,” and depression scores did not change.[5]
  • Sarcoidosis trial (2013). In 38 people, 28 days of daily injections improved symptoms, raised corneal nerve fiber density and improved the 6-minute walk test.[6]
  • Type 2 diabetes (2015). In 49 people with painful neuropathy, a pain questionnaire improved on ARA 290 vs placebo. Blood sugar (HbA1c) and lipids also improved, and corneal nerves grew in people who started with the fewest.[7]
  • Mood (2015). In 36 healthy adults, ARA 290 had no effect on mood, and the authors say the results do not clearly support an antidepressant-like profile.[8]
  • Sarcoidosis phase 2b (2017). This 64-person trial is the largest. Its main goal, a gain in corneal nerve fiber area, was statistically significant only in the middle of three dose groups. “Pain improved significantly in all groups,” including placebo.[9] The posted registry averages show pain and symptom changes on placebo in the same range as on the drug, with no statistical tests posted; walking distance rose more in the drug groups than on placebo.[10]
  • Diabetic eye swelling (2020). In 9 people with no comparison group, vision, retinal thickness and tear production did not improve over 12 weeks; a questionnaire score did. The trial ended early because the study drug expired.[11][12]

The key trials, in one chart

Our analysis: the human evidence is six small trials, almost all four weeks long, and most were run with the developer. The early trials reported gains on questionnaires and corneal nerve scans. When a larger trial came, the nerve-fiber result held in one dose group only, and pain improved about as much on placebo. No larger or longer trial has followed, and the last trial we found ended in 2017 and was published in 2020.[9][10][13][11]

The case for ARA-290, in fairness

The idea is reasonable: EPO protects tissue in animals, and ARA 290 was designed to keep that effect without EPO’s effect on red blood cells.[1][2] The rat pain results were long-lasting, and the effect disappeared in mice lacking the receptor it is thought to act on, which supports the proposed mechanism.[3] Most of the human trials were randomized and placebo-controlled.[5][6][7][9] A 2014 review by the Leiden group, with Araim-affiliated co-authors, said treatment “was consistently associated with a significant improvement of neuropathic pain symptoms” in the two early sarcoidosis trials.[15] Corneal nerve gains appeared in more than one trial, and in the phase 2b they tracked with skin nerve and walking-test changes.[6][9] Regulators in the US and EU granted orphan designations for sarcoidosis uses, which recognize a rare disease with few options; they are not approvals.[20][23] Our analysis: this is a reason for a larger, longer trial, not evidence that ARA-290 repairs nerves in people.

Safety

  • Short trials. Most trials treated people for four weeks; the longest lasted 12 weeks. We found no long-term safety data.[6][7][9][11]
  • Serious events. In the diabetes trial, all four serious adverse events occurred in the ARA 290 group, and two were judged possibly related, including a worsening of borderline kidney function that led the safety committee to stop the drug in that person; a death from a heart attack after severe cellulitis was judged unrelated. The paper’s abstract says “No potential safety issues were identified.”[7] In the phase 2b, the registry lists serious adverse events in 3 of 46 people on ARA 290, including one report of suicidal ideation, and in 0 of 16 on placebo; it does not say whether the drug caused them.[10]
  • Immune reactions. The eye trial reported no serious adverse events and no antibodies against the drug.[11]
  • What is unknown. A 2016 review co-written with the developer called for “long-term treatment and follow-up” trials, and a 2026 review lists unresolved questions about safety and long-term outcomes.[16][18]

Purity

No ARA-290 product is approved, so anything sold under that name is not a regulated medicine made to approved standards.[20][23] Our analysis: the trials used a developer-supplied study drug, and their results do not automatically apply to other products. We do not name or rate sellers.

Legal status

ARA-290 is not approved by FDA or in the EU. FDA’s orphan record says “Not FDA Approved for Orphan Indication,” and Araim said in 2014 that FDA granted Fast Track designation, a program for faster review, not an approval.[20][21][23] FDA lists cibinetide in 503A category 3, substances nominated for compounding “without adequate support.”[24] For athletes, WADA’s 2027 list bans “innate repair receptor agonists” at all times. It does not name ARA-290, but an anti-doping lab study calls ARA-290 a prohibited peptide.[25][19]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. Check the placebo group. In two sarcoidosis trials, pain improved on placebo too, so “pain got better” is not evidence on its own.[5][9]
  2. Count the dose groups. The largest trial met its main goal in one of three, with no clear pattern of more drug giving more effect.[9]
  3. Nerve scans are stand-in measures. Corneal nerve area is a surrogate endpoint, not a measure of how people feel or function.[9]
  4. Read the abstract and the safety section. The diabetes trial’s abstract reports no potential safety issues, while its full text describes four serious events on the drug.[7]
  5. Read the disclosures. Most trial papers list developer officers or employees with stock or options, or developer funding. That does not make the results wrong, but independent replication matters.[5][6][7][11][16]
  6. Designations are not approvals. Orphan and Fast Track designations come early in development.[20][21][23]

Frequently asked questions

What is ARA-290?
An experimental 11-amino-acid peptide, also called cibinetide, built from part of the hormone EPO to protect tissue without raising red blood cells.[2]
Does ARA-290 work for nerve pain?
Not proven. Small early trials reported symptom gains, but in the largest trial pain improved in all groups including placebo, and the main nerve-fiber goal was met in one of three dose groups.[5][6][9]
Is ARA-290 safe?
Unknown beyond short use. Trials lasted 4 to 12 weeks; serious adverse events occurred only on the drug in two trials, some judged possibly related.[7][10][11]
Is ARA-290 FDA approved?
No. It holds an FDA orphan designation for neuropathic pain in sarcoidosis, and FDA lists it as nominated for compounding “without adequate support.”[20][24]
Is ARA-290 banned in sport?
WADA’s 2027 list bans innate repair receptor agonists at all times without naming ARA-290; an anti-doping lab study calls it a prohibited peptide.[25][19]
Is ARA-290 the same as EPO?
No. It is a short fragment of EPO designed not to raise red blood cells.[2]
Are there new ARA-290 trials?
We found none recruiting or active. The last published trial ended in 2017.[13][12]

Bottom line

ARA-290 has a clear scientific rationale and long-lasting pain relief in rats.[2][3] In people, it has six small, short trials, mostly run with its developer, with promising early signals that did not clearly hold up in the largest trial.[5][6][9][10] It is not approved, no trial is active, and its long-term safety is unknown.[20][13][16]
