Home / Research / Retatrutide: biologic or drug?

Policy explainer · retatrutide, tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound), semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) · FDA’s October 2 decision, the Seventh Circuit appeal, patents, generics and compounding · Sources checked October 19, 2026

Published October 19, 2026 · Updated October 19, 2026

Why is Lilly suing FDA to call retatrutide a biologic? The label mostly decides how long it stays copy-free after approval, and whether anyone can ever legally compound it.

Eli Lilly wants FDA to treat retatrutide, its triple-hormone weight-loss shot, as a biologic, the category for proteins. FDA said no again on October 2.[1] Here is what that means for you. As a drug, cheaper copies could apply four years after approval, and compounding could reopen in a shortage. As a biologic, no copy could be approved for 12 years, and compounding would be shut for good. Lilly’s main patent probably runs to about January 2039 either way, and the label doesn’t change the brand’s own timing much.[11][10][19][14][4]

Why this matters now

On October 2, FDA’s drug center ruled again, on remand from a federal court, that retatrutide is not a biologic.[1][2] In September, the appeals court asked both sides whether it even has jurisdiction to hear Lilly’s appeal. Their answers came in on October 8. The government attached FDA’s new decision and asked the court to dismiss the appeal.[3][5] Lilly said it will file for approval “no later than the first quarter of 2027” and needs to know which pathway to use.[4] Trade press picked up the filing on October 14.[32] Retatrutide drew 178 to 270 posts a day on X last week.[34] The court hasn’t ruled.

The short answer

  • The brand’s timing barely changes. Lilly plans to file by early 2027, and the same FDA center reviews it on either path.[4][1]
  • The copy clock does. As a drug, a generic maker could apply four years after approval by challenging Lilly’s patents. As a biologic, no copy could be approved for 12 years.[11][10]
  • The patent may matter more than the label. The patent that covers retatrutide’s exact structure runs to about January 2039, before any extension.[14]
  • Compounding is illegal now, either way. As a drug, it could reopen only in narrow cases such as a shortage. As a biologic, it couldn’t reopen at all.[17][18][19]
  • Medicare price cuts would come four years later as a biologic, at about 13 years after approval instead of 9.[16] For scale, Medicare’s negotiated price for Ozempic and Wegovy, starting in 2027, is 71% below the 2024 list price. That covers Medicare, not your cash price.[24]

The fight: 39 or 41?

FDA’s rule says a protein is a chain of alpha amino acids, the standard building blocks, with a defined sequence “greater than 40 amino acids in size.” Proteins are biologics.[9] Retatrutide’s backbone is a chain of 39 alpha amino acids. A side chain hangs off one of them: a linker called ADO, then a gamma-linked glutamate, then a long fatty acid that helps the drug last a week. FDA says ADO is an amino acid but not an alpha amino acid. Even if the glutamate counted, the total would reach only 40, which is not greater than 40.[1] Lilly counts both side-chain pieces and gets 41.[7]

Lilly sued in September 2024.[1] In September 2025 a federal judge in Indianapolis sided with FDA on the count, writing that retatrutide “contains, at most, forty alpha amino acids.” She ruled for Lilly on a second question, whether retatrutide is “analogous” to a protein. She found FDA’s bright-line approach to that question “flouts the statutory text” and sent it back to FDA, while noting FDA could lawfully reach the same answer.[2] It did. FDA’s October 2 decision says a product analogous to a protein must share a protein’s two key traits: more than 40 alpha amino acids, and a specific defined sequence. Retatrutide fails the first.[1]

Both sides, fairly

Lilly’s case. Lilly says the rule counts the total number of amino acids, and ADO and the glutamate are amino acids, so retatrutide has 41.[7] It also argues that the law gives FDA 60 days to decide these requests, that FDA took nearly ten months after the remand, and that Lilly’s own proposed designation should therefore take effect. And it says only a ruling now gives it certainty before it files.[7][4] CEO Dave Ricks told analysts in August that “we believe retatrutide is a biologic application,” on both the count and the analogy argument.[29]

FDA’s case. FDA says alpha amino acids are what make up a protein’s chain. It says side chains don’t count, the same way it counted insulin degludec as 50 amino acids, not more.[1] The government’s brief points out that Lilly once described a protein to FDA as a “linear polymer of α-amino acids.”[6] FDA also notes Lilly’s own 2023 Mounjaro label called tirzepatide, which has a similar side chain, “a 39-amino-acid … peptide.” Lilly’s later labels dropped that wording.[1] FDA rejects the 60-day argument. It says it answered Lilly’s request on time, in March 2024, and that the statute sets no deadline for a remand.[1][6]

Who else weighed in. The only friend-of-the-court brief we found came from the Outsourcing Facilities Association, a compounders’ trade group. It argued that Lilly “seeks a windfall” and that a biologic label would make retatrutide “immune to competition from compounders.”[8] That group has its own stake. Compounders sold copies of semaglutide and tirzepatide during the shortages.[18] Its chairman, Lee Rosebush, also chairs the peptide lobbying group in our White House peptide meeting report.

The generic route is no longer hypothetical for this kind of molecule. Tirzepatide, also a 39-amino-acid Lilly drug, was approved in May 2022. In July 2026, just past the four-year mark, Lilly said several generic companies had filed applications to copy Mounjaro or Zepbound before some patents expire. Lilly said it plans to sue.[16][25] That same month, FDA published draft guidance for generic copies of Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro and Zepbound. It covers synthetic peptides submitted as standard generics.[22] Generic liraglutide, the older daily GLP-1, has had eight approvals since December 2024.[25]

The biologic route isn’t a wall forever, either. In October 2025 FDA moved to cut the extra studies biosimilars need. It said it “now generally does not recommend” the switching studies once required for pharmacists to swap one in.[23] But no biosimilar could be approved before year 12.[10]

The patent may set the date either way

Headlines frame the fight as 5 years of protection versus 12. The patent record makes it more complicated. U.S. Patent 11,542,313, granted to Lilly in January 2023, claims retatrutide’s exact structure. With 37 days of adjustment, it runs to about January 20, 2039.[14] Lilly can seek up to 5 more years for time lost in testing and review. By law, the patent can’t run past 14 years after approval.[13]

Here is a rough illustration, not a forecast. Suppose FDA approved retatrutide in 2028. As a drug, its 5-year exclusivity would end in 2033, years before the 2039 patent date, so the patent would be the real barrier. As a biologic, the 12-year period would end in 2040, close to when the patent ends anyway. So if the patent holds, the label adds little time. The label matters most if generic makers break the patent. As a drug, a copy could then win approval years before 2039. As a biologic, nothing could be approved before 2040, no matter what a patent court decided.[11][10][14] For comparison, Lilly lists tirzepatide’s compound patent to 2036 and its data protection to 2027. Generic makers filed anyway.[15][16]

Compounding: illegal now, and the label decides later

FDA says plainly that “retatrutide and cagrilintide cannot be used in compounding under federal law.”[17] Anything sold online as retatrutide today is unapproved. Our retatrutide review covers a lab test of gray-market vials.

If it is approved as a drug, compounding could come back only in narrow cases. That happened with semaglutide and tirzepatide, which compounders could copy while FDA listed them in shortage. The shortages ended in December 2024 and February 2025, and FDA then set end dates for those copies.[18] If it is a biologic, that door never opens. The compounding sections of the drug law don’t cover biologics.[19] When insulin and similar products became biologics in March 2020, FDA told compounders that several, including hCG, could no longer be compounded under those sections.[20][21]

Did Lilly lobby on it?

Not in any public lobbying report. We pulled all 173 federal lobbying reports filed for Lilly from early 2024 through September 2026. None mention retatrutide, the protein definition or amino acids. Lilly’s own reports use broad labels like “FDA policy issues.” Some outside firms list compounding bills.[31] Broad labels mean the topic could still have come up. Lilly has made its case in court and on earnings calls.[7][29]

What happens next

The Seventh Circuit could dismiss the appeal for lack of jurisdiction, as the government asks. Then Lilly would probably have to file a new lawsuit to challenge the October 2 decision.[5][4] Or the court could keep the case and rule on how to count amino acids and on the 60-day argument. Neither outcome has a date. Lilly has said since July that it plans to submit retatrutide in the first quarter of 2027, and it calls that a biologics application.[30][4] FDA says it belongs under the drug law.[1]

Strengths and limits

Strengths. We read the court filings, FDA’s decision, the statutes and the patent itself. Every amino-acid count comes from FDA’s substance registry.[1][2][14][27]

Limits. We checked one patent. Lilly may hold others on formulations or uses, and courts may cut any patent short. Our timeline is an illustration with an assumed approval year. Lobbying reports are often vague. We aren’t lawyers, and this isn’t legal or investing advice. Other coverage, such as AJMC’s explainer, walks through the exclusivity and Medicare rules.[33]

Bottom line

Lilly wants the 12-year biologic clock. FDA has now said twice that retatrutide is a drug, and a federal judge agreed on the amino-acid count. Neither label is likely to change when you can get the brand. The label decides how firmly that brand is shielded from cheaper copies, and whether compounding can ever return. If Lilly’s patents hold to about 2039, the practical gap is smaller than “12 versus 5” suggests. If the patents fall, the label decides everything.[1][2][10][11][14]

Frequently asked questions

Is retatrutide a biologic or a drug?
FDA says it is a drug. On October 2, 2026 the agency again concluded that retatrutide is neither a protein nor something analogous to a protein, so an application would go under the drug law. Lilly disagrees, and its appeal is pending at the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit.
Will the lawsuit delay retatrutide’s approval?
Not on its own. Lilly told the appeals court on October 8 that it plans to file for approval no later than the first quarter of 2027, and FDA’s drug center reviews it on either path. Lilly also warned that an unresolved fight could force it to choose between filing on time and filing under the label it wants.
Can retatrutide be legally compounded?
No. FDA says retatrutide cannot be used in compounding under federal law today. If it is approved as a drug, compounding could become possible only in narrow cases, such as an FDA-declared shortage. If it is a biologic, the compounding exemptions never apply.
When could a generic or cheaper copy of retatrutide come out?
Not soon either way. As a drug, a copy could apply 4 years after approval if it challenges Lilly’s patents, but the main patent runs to about January 2039. As a biologic, no copy could be approved for 12 years after approval, even if the patents fell.
Why does the number 40 matter?
FDA’s rule defines a protein as a chain of alpha amino acids with a defined sequence greater than 40 amino acids. Proteins are regulated as biologics. Retatrutide’s chain has 39; Lilly counts 41 by adding two building blocks from its side chain.
Is CagriSema a biologic?
No. Novo Nordisk filed CagriSema, which combines cagrilintide and semaglutide, as a New Drug Application in December 2025. Each of its parts has fewer than 40 amino acids.
Did Lilly lobby Congress or the White House on this?
We found no public lobbying report that names it. None of the 173 federal lobbying reports filed for Lilly from 2024 through September 2026 mention retatrutide or the protein definition, though many use broad labels like FDA policy issues.
