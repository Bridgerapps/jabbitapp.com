Is retatrutide better than Ozempic? In Lilly’s first head-to-head trial, announced October 28, 2026, adults with type 2 diabetes lost about 20% of their weight over 80 weeks on retatrutide vs 9.4% on Ozempic’s top dose, and A1C fell 2.2 vs 1.8 points. These are company topline numbers, not yet peer reviewed, and retatrutide is not approved.

The question most people ask about Eli Lilly’s triple-hormone shot is simple: is it better than Ozempic? On October 28, 2026, Lilly announced the first trial to test that directly. Our plain answer: on Lilly’s numbers, retatrutide roughly doubled the weight loss of Ozempic 2 mg, the highest approved dose, and lowered A1C a bit more, with somewhat more people stopping because of side effects. But this is a company press release, not a published paper, it reports only the most flattering of the standard ways to count results, and it says nothing yet about hypoglycemia, serious side effects, heart outcomes or eyes. Retatrutide is not approved anywhere.[1]

Why this matters now

Lilly released the topline results of TRANSCEND-T2D-2 at 6:45 a.m. ET on October 28, calling it “the first Phase 3 trial comparing retatrutide with another incretin medicine.”[1] Business Insider noted the results came out as Lilly prepares to report quarterly earnings on Thursday, October 29.[14] Lilly has said it plans to submit retatrutide to FDA in the first quarter of 2027.[9] Ozempic is the drug many readers already take, so this is the comparison that matters most for them, and it is likely to be quoted widely before any peer-reviewed paper exists.

What the trial tested

  • Who. 1,298 adults with type 2 diabetes not controlled on metformin, with or without an SGLT2 inhibitor, and an A1C of 7.0% to 10.5%. Average A1C at the start was 8.3%, weight 102.4 kg (225.8 lb) and BMI 36.8.[1]
  • Arms. Weekly retatrutide at one of two trial doses (9 or 12 mg) or Ozempic 2 mg, randomized 2:2:1, so roughly 260 people got Ozempic (Jabbit arithmetic).[1]
  • Design. Open label: participants and doctors knew which drug was given.[2][1]
  • Main question. Whether retatrutide 12 mg was at least as good as Ozempic 2 mg at lowering A1C after 80 weeks (a noninferiority test). Weight was a key secondary endpoint.[1]

What Lilly reported

  • Weight: −19.5% (−20.1 kg, −44.3 lb) on 9 mg and −20.9% (−21.5 kg, −47.4 lb) on 12 mg, vs −9.4% (−9.5 kg, −20.9 lb) on Ozempic.[1] That is 10.1 to 11.5 percentage points more, or about 10.6 to 12.0 kg (Jabbit arithmetic from the rounded figures).
  • A1C: −2.2 points on both doses vs −1.8 on Ozempic, from 8.3%. On average, that would take A1C to about 6.1% vs 6.5% (Jabbit arithmetic).[1]
  • Statistics. Lilly says all comparisons were controlled for multiple testing except the 9 mg dose’s A1C superiority over Ozempic and a systolic blood pressure comparison, which showed only “nominal statistical significance,” meaning they were not protected by that control.[1]
  • Heart risk factors. Lilly says retatrutide improved non-HDL cholesterol, triglycerides and systolic blood pressure more than Ozempic, but gave no numbers.[1]

Side effects

  • Stomach side effects were broadly similar. Diarrhea was a little more common on retatrutide (28.3% and 32.7% vs 26.4%) and constipation more common on Ozempic (19.8% vs 14.2% and 13.1%). Lilly says most were mild to moderate and typically resolved during treatment.[1]
  • Dysesthesia, an abnormal or burning skin sensation, was reported by 4.8% and 6.5% on retatrutide vs 2.7% on Ozempic.[1][14] The same signal appeared against placebo in TRIUMPH-2 and the sleep apnea trials.[8][13]
  • Stopping because of side effects: 4.8% and 7.9% on retatrutide vs 2.7% on Ozempic, so the 12 mg rate was nearly three times higher (Jabbit arithmetic).[1]
  • Not in the release: hypoglycemia, serious adverse events, deaths, heart rate, pancreatitis and gallbladder events, and eye findings. Earlier retatrutide trials reported a heart-rate rise of 5 to 7 beats per minute.[1][13]

How it compares with earlier head-to-heads

In SURPASS-2, Lilly’s tirzepatide 15 mg beat semaglutide 1 mg by 0.45 A1C points and 5.5 kg over 40 weeks.[5] In TRANSCEND-T2D-2, retatrutide beat semaglutide 2 mg by about 0.4 points and about 11 to 12 kg over 80 weeks.[1] Jabbit’s analysis: on blood sugar, the margin over semaglutide looks similar to tirzepatide’s; on weight, it looks roughly twice as large. But the trials differ in length, comparator dose and statistical method, so this does not rank retatrutide against tirzepatide. TRIUMPH-5, a double-blind trial testing those two drugs directly, is due for primary completion around November 2026, per its registry record.[10]

Ozempic itself did about what its earlier trials predict. In SUSTAIN FORTE, the 2 mg dose lowered A1C by 2.1 to 2.2 points from 8.9% over 40 weeks, with 6.4 to 6.9 kg of weight loss.[4][3] Here it lowered A1C 1.8 points from a lower start of 8.3% and weight by 9.5 kg over 80 weeks.[1] Our read: people who start with a lower A1C generally have less room to fall, so the comparator does not look handicapped, though the analysis methods differ.

What a careful reader should notice

  1. It’s a press release. Lilly says detailed results will come at a medical meeting and in a peer-reviewed journal.[1] Until then, nobody outside the company can check the analysis.
  2. The flattering estimand only. Every number is the “efficacy estimand,” which estimates results “had all randomized participants remained on study intervention.”[1] Lilly’s published retatrutide papers lead with the treatment-regimen estimand, which counts everyone who was randomized, including those who stopped.[7][8] In TRIUMPH-2, Lilly’s release figure for the 12 mg dose was 20.8%; the Lancet paper’s treatment-regimen figure was 18.8%.[1][8] More people stopped retatrutide for side effects here, so the treatment-regimen gap could be somewhat smaller than these numbers suggest.
  3. Open label. A1C and weight are measured objectively, but knowing your drug can affect how side effects are reported and who drops out.[2]
  4. Registered outcomes not yet reported. The trial registry lists the share of people reaching an A1C of 6.5% or less, and below 5.7% (the non-diabetic range), plus weight-loss thresholds. None appear in the release.[2][1]
  5. Lilly ran it. The trial is sponsored by Lilly, which also makes the competing drug Mounjaro.[2][1] Lilly also funded SURPASS-2, and Novo Nordisk, Ozempic’s maker, funded SUSTAIN FORTE.[5][4] Industry funding is normal for drug trials and does not make results wrong, but independent replication adds weight.

The longevity question

For healthy aging, A1C and weight are stand-ins; what matters is heart attacks, strokes, kidney failure and death. Here the drugs are not on equal footing. Ozempic’s label already includes reducing major cardiovascular events in people with type 2 diabetes and heart disease, and reducing kidney decline and cardiovascular death in people with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease.[3] Retatrutide has no outcomes data yet. Its heart and kidney outcomes trial, about 10,000 people, is not due to finish until 2029, per its registry record.[12] Our read: bigger weight loss and better risk factors make a benefit plausible, but it is unproven. One more thing to watch: the Ozempic label warns that rapid improvement in blood sugar “has been associated with a temporary worsening of diabetic retinopathy,” and the release gives no eye data for either drug.[3][1]

Can you switch from Ozempic to retatrutide?

Not legally outside a clinical trial. Lilly says retatrutide “has not been approved by FDA or any regulatory organization anywhere in the world” and that “anyone purporting to sell retatrutide to consumers is breaking the law.”[1] If Ozempic is not doing enough, approved options and dose changes are a conversation for your prescriber, and ClinicalTrials.gov lists retatrutide trials.[2]

FAQ

Is retatrutide better than Ozempic?
In Lilly’s TRANSCEND-T2D-2 trial, announced October 28, 2026, retatrutide produced about twice the weight loss of Ozempic 2 mg over 80 weeks and slightly more A1C lowering in adults with type 2 diabetes. These are company topline results, not yet peer reviewed, and there are no heart or kidney outcomes data for retatrutide yet.
How much weight did people lose on retatrutide vs Ozempic?
On average, 19.5% (about 44 lb) on retatrutide 9 mg and 20.9% (about 47 lb) on 12 mg, vs 9.4% (about 21 lb) on Ozempic 2 mg, from a starting weight of about 226 lb. Lilly’s figures assume everyone stayed on treatment.
Does retatrutide have more side effects than Ozempic?
Stomach side effects were broadly similar, with a little more diarrhea on retatrutide and more constipation on Ozempic. Abnormal skin sensations (dysesthesia) were more common on retatrutide, and more people stopped retatrutide because of side effects: 4.8% and 7.9% vs 2.7%.
Has TRANSCEND-T2D-2 been published?
Not yet. As of October 28, 2026, the results are in a Lilly press release. Lilly says full results will be presented at a medical meeting and published in a peer-reviewed journal.
Can I switch from Ozempic to retatrutide?
Not legally outside a clinical trial. Retatrutide is not approved anywhere, and Lilly says anyone selling it to consumers is breaking the law. Talk to your prescriber about approved options.
When could retatrutide be approved?
Lilly has said it plans to submit retatrutide to FDA in the first quarter of 2027. Any approval would depend on FDA’s review, and no approval date has been announced.

Bottom line

On Lilly’s topline numbers, retatrutide beat Ozempic’s highest dose clearly on weight and modestly on A1C in type 2 diabetes, with more people stopping for side effects and a skin-sensation signal seen across the program.[1][8][13] The full paper, the treatment-regimen numbers, hypoglycemia and serious-event data, and eventually heart and kidney outcomes will decide how much that matters. Ozempic remains the drug with proven outcome benefits; retatrutide remains investigational.[3][12]
