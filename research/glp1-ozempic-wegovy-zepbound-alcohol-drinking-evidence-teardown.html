Home / Research / GLP-1 drugs and alcohol teardown

Study teardown · Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide (Zepbound) and other GLP-1-type drugs · Randomized trials, health-record studies, company and NIH releases, registry records, reviews and PubMed counts · Sources checked October 26, 2026

Published October 26, 2026 · Updated October 26, 2026

Do Ozempic, Wegovy and Zepbound make you drink less? Heineken warned UK pubs in October 2026 that weight-loss drugs could cut beer sales. Six small randomized trials are mixed but mostly encouraging, the best one in people with alcohol use disorder and obesity, and health-record studies point the same way. No GLP-1 drug is approved for alcohol use disorder, and the first large trials report in 2028 at the earliest.

GLP-1 drugs such as semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) are approved for diabetes and weight loss, not for alcohol use disorder.[26] Many users report wanting to drink less, and on October 23, 2026, The Guardian reported, citing Reuters, that Heineken had warned UK pubs that the drugs “could reduce the desire to drink alcohol.”[1] Do they really cut drinking? Six small randomized trials, with 48 to 151 people each, have reported: three met their main goal, two missed, and one was a secondary analysis that found 29% less drinking.[5][2][9][6][4][8] The strongest, published in The Lancet in 2026, found semaglutide cut heavy drinking days by 13.7 percentage points more than placebo in people with alcohol use disorder and obesity.[2] Large health-record studies point the same way but cannot prove cause.[16][17][18] Tirzepatide has not yet reported a randomized alcohol trial in people, and the first large trials, of a newer Lilly drug, report in early 2028.[13][11][12]

Why this matters now

On October 23, 2026, The Guardian reported that Heineken had sent British bars and pubs a presentation, first revealed by Reuters, warning that weight-loss drug use could lead to lower beer and cider sales, citing “fullness and fewer serves.”[1] A Heineken spokesperson told the paper the company sees “some changes in consumption behaviour among (GLP-1) users” and will keep monitoring; the paper also reported that Carlsberg told Reuters its data did not support that view, and that Diageo has told investors any impact should be manageable.[1] Our analysis: these are business forecasts, not health evidence. The same month, two reviews took stock of the science: one counted only three randomized trials in alcohol use disorder, and another stressed that no GLP-1 drug is approved for it and that the evidence is limited by small samples, short follow-up, self-reported drinking and possible confounding by weight loss.[25][26] A PubMed search we ran on October 26, 2026 found 101 records naming a GLP-1 drug and alcohol use disorder in the title or abstract, far more papers than randomized trials.[15]

Why a diabetes drug might affect drinking

GLP-1 drugs mimic a gut hormone that slows the stomach and acts on appetite centers in the brain. In rodents, tirzepatide reduced drinking, binge drinking and relapse-like drinking, and semaglutide, tirzepatide and retatrutide all blunted how rats sense alcohol, an effect gone three days after the drug stopped.[23][24] One review of human brain-imaging and drinking studies describes reduced brain reactions to alcohol cues, mainly in people with overweight or obesity.[25]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • The best trial. In Copenhagen, 108 adults with alcohol use disorder and obesity got therapy plus weekly semaglutide or placebo for 26 weeks. Heavy drinking days fell 41.1 points on semaglutide vs 26.4 on placebo, a 13.7-point difference; 81% completed the study.[2] NIH said blood markers of drinking backed up self-reports, and that the number needed to treat was 4.3, compared with 7 or higher for approved medicines.[3]
  • The misses. Exenatide, an older GLP-1 drug, did not reduce heavy drinking days overall in 127 patients, though a blood marker of drinking diverged by week 26.[6][7] An oral semaglutide trial of 50 people missed its main goal, lab-measured craving, and did not cut drinks per day, but did cut heavy drinking days.[4]
  • Smaller wins. In 48 people not seeking treatment, semaglutide reduced drinking in a lab session and drinks per drinking day, but not drinks per day or drinking days overall.[5] In a smoking-cessation trial, people on dulaglutide drank 29% less at 12 weeks.[8]
  • Newer drugs. Altimmune says pemvidutide, an experimental glucagon/GLP-1 drug, cut heavy drinking days per week by 4.20 vs 2.75 on placebo over 24 weeks in its RECLAIM trial; these are company topline results, not yet peer reviewed.[9][10] Lilly’s 308-person mazdutide trial finished in March 2026 with no results posted.[12]
  • Health records. In Sweden, semaglutide users had fewer alcohol-related hospital stays (adjusted hazard ratio 0.64) than when not on the drug; US studies report similar or larger drops in new diagnoses, relapses and hospital visits.[16][17][19][20] But in a large US survey data set, the link was small (a 5% lower drinking score), and in a Texas cohort the drop in drinking lost significance after accounting for weight change.[21][22]

The key trials, in one chart

Our analysis: the trials disagree less than the headlines suggest. The clearest benefit is in people with alcohol use disorder who also have obesity, the group the exenatide trial singled out in 2022 and the Lancet trial then tested directly.[6][2][25] Whether the drugs help people without obesity, or people who drink moderately, is much less certain.[5][4][26]

The case for GLP-1 drugs, in fairness

Few medicines exist for alcohol use disorder, and NIH’s release says the approved ones are “vastly underutilized.”[3] The animal data are consistent across several drugs, the best randomized trial was blinded and placebo-controlled with blood-marker support, and register studies from several countries agree in direction.[23][24][2][3][16][18] A 2026 gastroenterology practice update says preliminary data suggest these drugs may reduce alcohol cravings and consumption.[30] Our analysis: that is a strong case for the large trials now under way, not proof that a weight-loss prescription treats alcohol use disorder.[12][13]

Safety

  • Stomach side effects. In the Lancet trial, side effects were mostly short-lived, mild to moderate gastrointestinal effects, more common on semaglutide; in RECLAIM, Altimmune reported nausea in 44% on pemvidutide vs 24% on placebo, and 10% stopped because of side effects vs none on placebo.[2][9]
  • Nutrition. A 2026 case report describes a man with obesity and alcohol use disorder who developed Korsakoff syndrome, a thiamine-deficiency brain disorder, during rapid weight loss on tirzepatide; the authors urge nutritional assessment and considering thiamine.[29] Our analysis: one case cannot show cause, but heavy drinking and rapid weight loss both strain nutrition.[29][30]
  • After stopping. In a Swedish study, the drop in alcohol-related hospital stays persisted for about six months after stopping, then disappeared; the authors advise monitoring substance use when treatment stops.[18]
  • What is unknown. No trial has run longer than 26 weeks, and none has tested the drugs in large groups of people without obesity.[26][2][6]

Legal status

No GLP-1 drug is approved by the FDA for alcohol use disorder; all such uses are investigational.[26] Lilly’s two phase 3 trials of brenipatide, each with about 1,100 people, are recruiting, with primary completion listed for April 2028; a VA trial of semaglutide in 622 veterans runs to 2029.[12][13] Altimmune says it plans to request an End-of-Phase 2 meeting with the FDA for pemvidutide.[9] One critical review counted 19 interventional trials in progress or completed but unpublished.[27][28][14]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. Heineken’s warning is a sales forecast. It is not evidence that the drugs treat alcohol use disorder.[1]
  2. The best trial was in people with obesity. Its results may not carry over to people of normal weight.[2][6]
  3. Check the main goal. Two of six trials missed theirs, including the only oral semaglutide trial.[6][4]
  4. Health-record studies cannot rule out other explanations. Weight loss itself may account for part of the drop.[22][26]
  5. Company toplines are not papers. The pemvidutide results have not been peer reviewed yet.[9]

Frequently asked questions

Does Ozempic or Wegovy reduce alcohol cravings?
Possibly. In trials, semaglutide reduced heavy drinking days and some craving measures, but an oral semaglutide trial missed its lab craving goal.[2][5][4]
Does Zepbound (tirzepatide) help people drink less?
It reduced drinking in rodents, and health-record studies include it, but no randomized human alcohol trial of tirzepatide has reported yet; several are recruiting.[23][19][13]
Are GLP-1 drugs approved to treat alcohol use disorder?
No. All uses for drinking are investigational, and the first large trials report in 2028 at the earliest.[26][12]
What did the 2026 Lancet trial find?
In 108 people with alcohol use disorder and obesity, semaglutide plus therapy cut heavy drinking days by 13.7 percentage points more than placebo plus therapy over 26 weeks.[2]
Why did Heineken warn UK pubs about weight-loss drugs?
The Guardian, citing Reuters, reported that a Heineken presentation said GLP-1 use could reduce the desire to drink; Carlsberg told Reuters its data did not support that view.[1]
Is it safe to drink alcohol on a GLP-1 drug?
The trials did not test that question. Both cause stomach upset, and one case report flags thiamine deficiency during rapid weight loss; ask the prescriber.[2][29]
Does the effect last after stopping?
One Swedish study found fewer alcohol-related hospital stays for about six months after stopping, but not after that.[18]

Bottom line

GLP-1 drugs reliably reduce drinking in animals, and in people the best evidence, a 108-person Lancet trial in alcohol use disorder with obesity, is encouraging.[23][2] But the human trials are small and short, two missed their main goals, and health-record studies cannot separate the drug from weight loss.[6][4][22][26] No GLP-1 drug is approved for alcohol use disorder; Lilly’s phase 3 brenipatide trials, due in early 2028, are the next big test.[26][11][12]
