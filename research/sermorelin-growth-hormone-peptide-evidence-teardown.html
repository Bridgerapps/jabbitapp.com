Study teardown · Sermorelin (sermorelin acetate, GHRH 1-29, former brand Geref) · FDA approval and withdrawal records, the aging trials, FDA recall and adverse event data, registry and compounding status · Sources checked October 24, 2026

Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

Sermorelin (Geref, the “growth hormone peptide” sold by anti-aging clinics): does it work, and is it safe? It was sold as an FDA-approved drug until 2008, and in healthy older adults it raised growth hormone, but strength and sleep did not improve, and 32 FDA recalls since 2012 have included compounded versions.

Sermorelin is the first 29 building blocks of the body’s own growth hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH), the signal that tells the pituitary gland to release growth hormone.[4] Unlike most peptides sold online, it was once an FDA-approved drug: Geref, approved in 1990 as a pituitary test and in 1997 to treat children with growth hormone deficiency.[1] Its maker stopped selling it in 2008 because the active ingredient was no longer being manufactured, and FDA later found the withdrawal was not for safety or effectiveness reasons.[2][1] What clinics sell today is compounded sermorelin, not Geref. In healthy older adults, sermorelin raised growth hormone and IGF-1, but the one six-month placebo-controlled trial found no gain in strength or fitness and no better deep sleep.[6][11]

Why this matters now

Hims & Hers told investors in August 2026 that it plans to offer “already allowed peptides” before the end of the year, including sermorelin.[21] In the meantime, FDA’s October 14, 2026 enforcement report listed a new recall of compounded sermorelin syringes for “lack of assurance of sterility,” bringing the number of recall events that included a sermorelin product since 2012 to 32.[15] FDA’s separate interim peptide compounding policy has been under White House review since October 14, 2026, with no public list.[22] We covered that policy in our FDA peptide compounding explainer. This page sets out what the record shows about sermorelin itself.

What sermorelin was approved for

Geref had two approved uses: a one-time test of whether the pituitary can release growth hormone, and nightly treatment of children whose growth failure came from too little growth hormone.[1] In the main pediatric study, 110 children were treated with no placebo group; average growth rose from 4.1 to 7.2 cm a year after 12 months.[5] A 1999 review concluded that sermorelin helped “some prepubertal children” and noted that growth gains with some sermorelin regimens were smaller than with daily growth hormone itself, though the recommended regimen had not been compared head to head.[4] Geref was never approved for adults, for aging, or for body composition.[1]

“FDA-approved” is often used loosely when sermorelin is marketed. The accurate version: a sermorelin product was approved for those two uses until 2009, FDA says the withdrawal was not for safety or effectiveness reasons, and no generic version has been approved since FDA said one could be.[1][3]

The aging evidence

The idea is simple. Growth hormone falls with age, and a drug that nudges the pituitary to release more of its own hormone might avoid some of the side effects of injecting growth hormone directly.[11] Researchers tested that idea in the 1990s and 2000s.

  • Hormone levels: in 10 older men, two weeks of twice-daily sermorelin brought growth hormone and IGF-1 up to the levels of young men, with no change in fasting glucose or blood pressure.[6] A once-nightly schedule in 11 older men raised nighttime growth hormone but not IGF-1.[7]
  • Body composition: the six-month University of Washington trial reported more lean mass and less body fat, especially belly fat, in preliminary results.[10][11] A 16-week study of a close analog found more lean mass in men only.[8]
  • Strength and fitness: the six-month trial found “no improvement in strength or aerobic fitness.”[11] The six-week study without a placebo group reported gains on 2 of 6 strength tests and no change in muscle or fat on scans.[7]
  • Sleep: despite the sleep-and-recovery marketing, the review by the trial’s investigators said sermorelin “failed to improve and may even have impaired deep sleep.”[11]
  • Thinking: the same trial, with 89 older adults, reported better scores on several timed reasoning tests.[9] A later trial in 152 adults found a similar benefit, but it used tesamorelin, a different, approved GHRH drug, not sermorelin.[12]

The trial’s own investigators called a physical-function result “tantalizing” but said it “needs confirmation,” and wrote that this drug class “has not been approved by regulatory authorities in any jurisdiction” for normal aging.[11] PubMed lists no randomized trial tagged with sermorelin in its title or abstract, and we found no registered trial of sermorelin treatment in healthy adults.[26][17] Much of the positive coverage of sermorelin for adults rests on opinion pieces rather than new trials, such as a two-page 2006 commentary.[14]

Recalls of compounded sermorelin

FDA’s enforcement data list 93 sermorelin product entries across 32 recall events by 28 firms since 2012.[15] Most were broad recalls after inspections found a firm could not assure the sterility of its injectable products; they are not test results showing that a given vial of sermorelin was contaminated. Two were Class I, FDA’s most serious category, including one that named a bacterial contaminant.[15] Others cited a potency problem, expiry dates the stability data did not support, and lapses in environmental monitoring. 19 of the events included blends of sermorelin with other peptides such as GHRP-2, GHRP-6 or ipamorelin, which have never been FDA-approved.[15] The most recent, started September 10, 2026, cited sterility concerns related to the formulation and processing equipment.[15]

Safety: what is and isn’t known

  • In the approved-era studies: sermorelin was generally well tolerated; the most common side effects were brief facial flushing and injection-site pain.[4][5] The aging studies were small and lasted weeks to months.[6][7][8][9]
  • Adverse event reports: FAERS lists 59 reports naming sermorelin, 30 of them as a suspect drug. Common terms include itching, nausea, hypersensitivity and injection-site reactions; 3 mention an anaphylactic reaction, and several describe dispensing or wrong-product errors.[16] These reports are unverified and do not prove cause.
  • What the growth hormone axis can do (context, not sermorelin data): in trials of growth hormone itself in healthy older adults, users had more fluid retention, joint pain, carpal tunnel syndrome and breast tissue growth in men, and somewhat more new diabetes.[13] Sermorelin raises growth hormone less directly, and whether it shares those risks over the long term has not been studied. A 2026 review lists blood sugar changes, fluid retention and hormone shifts among effects reported across this drug class.[24]
  • Product quality: compounded sermorelin is not FDA-reviewed for quality, and the recall record above shows repeated sterility problems.[15] FDA’s drug listings show 17 registered bulk sermorelin listings from 16 labelers, which tells you supply exists, not that any is good.[18]

Legal status

No sermorelin product is currently FDA-approved; both Geref approvals were withdrawn in 2009.[1][3] Under federal compounding law, a pharmacy may use a bulk substance that is a component of an FDA-approved drug, has a USP monograph, or is on FDA’s 503A bulks list.[19] Compounders and telehealth companies generally treat sermorelin as permitted because it was in an approved product, and Hims & Hers described it as “already allowed.”[21] We did not find an FDA statement that addresses sermorelin by name, and it does not appear on FDA’s list of compounding substances that may pose significant safety risks.[20] Whether FDA’s pending interim peptide policy touches sermorelin is not public.[22] Sermorelin is banned at all times in sport under the WADA list.[23]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the cited sources.

  1. Sermorelin’s FDA history is real but narrow: a pituitary test and a pediatric growth treatment, never an adult or anti-aging use.[1]
  2. The best-designed aging trial found that hormone levels rose while strength, fitness and deep sleep did not improve.[11]
  3. The most interesting adult result, better scores on thinking tests, comes from one trial, and the follow-up used a different drug.[9][12]
  4. The product sold today is compounded, not Geref, and compounded sermorelin has appeared in 32 recall events, mostly over sterility.[15]
  5. “Already allowed” describes a compounding pathway, not evidence that sermorelin works for aging.[21][19][11]

Frequently asked questions

Is sermorelin FDA approved?
Not anymore. Sermorelin was approved as Geref in 1990 (a pituitary test) and 1997 (children with growth hormone deficiency). Both were discontinued in 2008, and FDA found that was not for safety or effectiveness reasons. Today’s sermorelin is compounded.[1][2]
Does sermorelin work for anti-aging?
It raises growth hormone and IGF-1 in older adults, but in the main six-month placebo-controlled trial strength and aerobic fitness did not improve. Body fat fell a little in early results.[6][11]
Does sermorelin improve sleep?
The trial evidence says no. Investigators reported it “failed to improve and may even have impaired deep sleep” in healthy older adults.[11]
Is sermorelin safe?
In older studies it was generally well tolerated, mostly flushing and injection-site pain. Long-term safety in healthy adults has not been studied, and compounded products have faced repeated sterility recalls.[4][15]
Why was Geref discontinued?
EMD Serono said the active ingredient used to make it was no longer being manufactured, and FDA later found the withdrawal was not for safety or effectiveness reasons.[2][1]
What is the difference between sermorelin and tesamorelin?
Both mimic GHRH. Tesamorelin is a stabilized analog that FDA approved in 2010 as Egrifta, for excess belly fat in some people with HIV (see our tesamorelin page). Sermorelin has no current approval.[12][25][3]
Is sermorelin banned in sports?
Yes. WADA lists sermorelin by name among growth hormone releasing factors prohibited at all times.[23]

Bottom line

Sermorelin is a once-approved drug with a clean exit from the market and a modest record in older adults: hormone levels rise, but the trial evidence does not show stronger muscles or better sleep, and the one cognitive signal needs repeating.[1][11][9] What is sold now is compounded, with a recurring sterility recall record.[15] Anyone weighing it would be relying on small, decades-old studies.
