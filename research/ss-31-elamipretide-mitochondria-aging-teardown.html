Home / Research / SS-31 (elamipretide) teardown

Paper-trail teardown · FDA approval files, 13 placebo-controlled trials, trial registries, FDA listings and PubMed read · Sources checked October 17, 2026

Published October 17, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

Does SS-31 recharge aging mitochondria? No placebo trial clearly says yes, and FDA’s own reviewers said no.

SS-31, sold by prescription as Forzinity (elamipretide), is what its maker calls the first FDA-approved mitochondria-targeted therapy.[1] On X it’s being injected for aging, energy and focus. We read every placebo-controlled phase 2 or 3 trial of it on ClinicalTrials.gov. There are 13, in heart attacks, heart failure, kidney disease, muscle disease, eye disease and healthy older adults. None clearly met its main goal: 9 missed, 2 came close (P = 0.053 and 0.055), and 2 heart-failure trials that finished in 2017 never posted results.[32] Its one approval, for an ultra-rare disease in about 150 Americans, came after FDA’s clinical and statistical reviewers recommended rejecting it, and it rests on strength gains measured without a placebo group in 8 to 10 patients.[6][3] No trial has tested it for aging.[32][30]

Why this matters this week

On October 14, Mighty Therapeutics (the company was called Stealth BioTherapeutics until June) marked one year since approval. It reported $17.5 million in gross sales since December, and said it is working toward a “placebo-controlled clinical trial of elamipretide in older adults, which will run through 2029” after an XPRIZE Healthspan award with the University of Washington.[1][2] On X, the peptide is moving the other way, ahead of the evidence. Posts this week describe “daily SS-31 microdoses,” stacking it with MOTS-c, and a 10 mg dose for focus. One eye surgeon says he uses SS-31 and MOTS-c “on some of my patients.”[35] English posts naming it ran at 24 to 43 a day from October 11 to 16, by our count.[35] One gray-market seller lists 50 mg vials at $59.99. The brand costs an estimated $793,520 a year.[35][9]

What SS-31 is, and where it came from

SS-31 is a four-amino-acid peptide (D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-Phe-NH2). It started as an opioid project. Peter Schiller at the Montreal Clinical Research Institute designed the parent opioid peptide analog around 2000, and Hazel Szeto’s lab at Cornell studied how such highly charged peptides got into cells.[10][12] In 2004, Szeto’s team found “by happenstance” that one of them piled up in mitochondria. Stealth Peptides was founded and licensed the compounds from Cornell in 2006.[11] SS-31 was built without the opioid activity. It binds cardiolipin, a fat in the inner mitochondrial membrane, and in lab and animal studies it helped mitochondria make energy and cut oxidant leaks.[10][13] The name “Szeto-Schiller” is where the SS comes from.[13]

The case for it: real mouse data in old muscle

The aging hype isn’t made up. In 2013, University of Washington researchers gave 27-month-old mice (very old for mice) one 3 mg/kg injection. Within an hour, muscle energy output was back “to young levels.” It did nothing in young mice, and 8 days of treatment raised endurance.[14] A 2019 follow-up gave old mice 3 mg/kg a day for 8 weeks and again found better muscle energy and exercise tolerance.[15] By FDA’s standard conversion, that mouse dose equals about 17 mg for a 70 kg person.[36][14] PubMed lists 642 records mentioning SS-31 or elamipretide. Only 22 are tagged as clinical trials.[32]

Then came the human trials

The company first tried it as a heart drug. In the EMBRACE STEMI trial (published 2016), a one-hour infusion during stenting for heart attacks didn’t shrink heart damage.[17] There was one early hint: in a 2017 safety study, a single 4-hour infusion briefly shrank heart volumes at the highest dose, in 8 heart-failure patients.[19] The follow-up, PROGRESS-HF, in 71 heart-failure patients, found no change in heart size at 4 or 40 mg a day.[21] Two more heart-failure trials, with 308 and 46 patients, finished in 2017. Nine years later, neither has posted results, and we found no paper for either.[29] A small kidney pilot reported better blood flow, but its registered main measure, filtration rate, rose more on placebo (+12.0 vs +6.3 mL/min).[18]

Then muscle disease. MMPOWER-1 came close on the 6-minute walk at the high dose (P = 0.053), MMPOWER-2 missed, and the 218-person phase 3 MMPOWER-3 missed outright: patients on the drug walked 3.2 meters less than placebo.[20][22][25] In dry macular degeneration, ReCLAIM-2 (176 people aged 55 and up) missed both main goals, though a predefined scan measure of photoreceptor loss looked better.[26] Eye drops for an inherited optic nerve disease missed.[27] NuPower, a 102-person phase 3 in nuclear-DNA mitochondrial disease at 60 mg a day, posted results in April 2026: no more improvement than placebo at 48 weeks.[28]

The “27% more ATP in older adults” study

The human study behind the aging posts is a 2021 trial in healthy older adults, funded by the company.[16] Researchers screened 120 people and excluded 47 (39%) for having mitochondria that already worked well, then randomized 39. Each got one 2-hour infusion by vein, about 35 mg for a 70 kg person, and they measured one small hand muscle.[16] Peak ATP output rose 27% on the drug vs 12% on placebo. That missed the usual bar on the raw change (P = 0.055) and just made it on percent change (P = 0.045).[16] A week later the difference was gone, and the muscle was no more fatigue-resistant.[16] An X post this week cited the 27% figure without those details.[35] It’s a real signal in one muscle for a few hours. It isn’t evidence that daily shots slow aging.

How it got approved anyway

Barth syndrome is an inherited disease that damages cardiolipin. It affects about 150 people in the U.S.[6] In the randomized part of the Barth trial, 12 patients took elamipretide or placebo for 12 weeks each. It missed both main goals: walking distance (P = 0.97) and fatigue (P = 0.89).[5][23] FDA refused to even file the first application in 2021.[6] In October 2024, FDA’s advisers voted 10 to 6 that it was effective.[8] In May 2025, FDA rejected it. Its letter said open-label walking gains were “generally only 8-22 meters” for all but two patients, with “only a single subject” showing a larger increase that couldn’t be credited to the drug.[5] The 2021 paper, by contrast, had reported +95.9 meters at 36 weeks open-label.[23]

The company came back with knee strength. FDA’s Integrated Review shows its clinical and statistical reviewers, and the team leader, concluded there was “not substantial evidence of effectiveness on knee muscle strength” and recommended rejection again. They also wrote that elamipretide doesn’t “meaningfully impact the elevated cardiolipin ratio,” the defect it was supposed to fix.[6] The signing official, office director Hylton Joffe, approved it on September 19, 2025, accepting “more uncertainty than we would accept for more common diseases.”[6][4][7] On the label, median knee strength in the randomized part moved −5 N on placebo and +4 N on drug. The big gains came only in the open-label extension, +63 N in the 8 patients still there at week 168.[3][24] The confirmatory trial isn’t due until 2029.[4][31] Fairly stated: for a rare, serious disease with nothing else, FDA chose to accept weak evidence. That is a different thing from proof it works.

Doses on X vs. doses tested

The approved dose is 40 mg a day under the skin.[3] X posts describe tapering to “2-3mg daily” and “microdoses,” a twentieth to a thirteenth of that.[35] No trial has tested 2 to 3 mg under the skin for anything. The lowest daily dose tested, 4 mg in heart failure, did no better than placebo, and neither did 40 mg.[21] Higher wasn’t better either: 60 mg missed in NuPower.[28]

Safety: what the label and FDA’s files say

Injection-site reactions hit all 12 Barth patients on the drug, vs 8 of 12 on placebo. Redness occurred in every patient on drug vs a quarter on placebo.[3] The label warns of hypersensitivity, including serious reactions needing emergency care, “minutes to months” after starting.[3] In FDA’s files, a 20-year-old woman developed a cough and rash 15 minutes after an injection. Ambulance staff treated it as anaphylactic shock with adrenaline. FDA classified it as a serious allergic reaction, not anaphylaxis, because her breathing and blood pressure stayed normal.[6] In lab tests, elamipretide released histamine from rat mast cells at high concentrations, although rats on the drug didn’t reach those blood levels. The label says overdose signs are “likely to be histamine-related.”[6][3] The new UW aging study screens out people with “allergies/adverse effects from Fexofenadine,” an antihistamine.[30]

Eosinophils, a type of white blood cell, rose modestly, peaking around 90 days.[3] “Carcinogenicity studies have not been conducted”: FDA called the cancer risk “uncertain,” and the 2-year rat study isn’t due until 2029.[3][5][4] Elamipretide blocks MATE1, a kidney transporter that clears metformin, so FDA ordered an interaction study. The dose is halved for severe kidney disease.[3][4] No Barth patients 65 or older were studied.[3] In other trials, treatment-related side effects ran 76.5% vs 39.2% on placebo in NuPower. ReCLAIM-2 logged 18 serious adverse events in 117 patients on drug vs 6 in 59 on placebo, and 2 deaths vs none. In EMBRACE, 10 of 150 heart-attack patients on drug died vs 3 of 147 on placebo.[28][26][17] None of those differences was shown to be caused by the drug, and these trials weren’t built to test that. They are worth knowing when a paper or post calls it “safe.”

Who’s making the powder

FDA’s listing files from October 16 show 6 companies listing elamipretide as a bulk ingredient. 5 started after Forzinity’s approval, and 3 are labeled “for human prescription compounding.”[32][33] They are registered API makers in China (Qingdao Biopeptek since 2020, Shenzhen Alvantis and Shanghai-based El-Peptido since 2026), APISTER Lifesciences in India, and a Florida relabeler, DARMERICA. The sixth, SCH Global Services Private Limited, has no matching entry in FDA’s establishment registration file of the same date.[32][33] Elamipretide doesn’t need FDA’s peptide “bulks list,” the subject of the interim compounding policy HHS made public on October 28. Because it’s a component of an approved drug, a 503A pharmacy can compound it with a prescription, from registered-source powder with a certificate of analysis. The law bars pharmacies from compounding “essentially copies” of an approved drug “regularly or in inordinate amounts.”[34] The $59.99 “research” vials on X are outside that system entirely, and there is no public testing of what’s in them.[35]

What would change the picture

Three readouts matter. ReNEW, a 313-person phase 3 in dry macular degeneration, is due in the second half of 2027. It’s the trial most like an aging disease.[1][31] 4TAZPower, the confirmatory Barth trial, runs to 2029.[31][4] The only aging study registered now, UW’s SHAPE, is a 30-person, 4-week safety study with no placebo. The placebo-controlled older-adult trial the company describes isn’t on ClinicalTrials.gov yet.[30][1][32] Until then, the honest answer is that SS-31 helps old mouse muscle and gave a brief boost to one hand muscle in older adults, and it has not beaten placebo on any main goal in people.

Bottom line

SS-31 has a real mechanism and clean mouse data in aging muscle. In people, 13 placebo-controlled trials across heart, kidney, muscle and eye disease and healthy older adults produced no clear win on a main goal. FDA approved it for one ultra-rare disease after its own clinical and statistical reviewers recommended rejection, on open-label strength gains in a few patients.[6] Nobody has tested the doses posted on X, and cancer studies are still pending. If you’re considering it, that’s the evidence your doctor would be weighing.
