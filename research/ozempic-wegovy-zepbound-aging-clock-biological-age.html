Home / Research / GLP-1s and aging clocks

Evidence review · Ozempic, Wegovy and Rybelsus (semaglutide), Zepbound and Mounjaro (tirzepatide) · Novo preprint and ADA poster, Lilly conference data, independent trials, ClinicalTrials.gov review · Sources checked October 19, 2026

Published October 19, 2026 · Updated October 21, 2026

Do Ozempic, Wegovy and Zepbound slow aging? Some blood-protein aging clocks read younger in sick, mostly diabetic patients, but that is not proof anyone ages slower.

Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly say people on their GLP-1 drugs show younger “biological age” on molecular aging clocks, by about two to three years overall and up to four for a heart clock.[1] We read Novo’s preprint, its supplement and its earlier poster, and the public accounts of Lilly’s data. The readings are real and mostly point the same way, but they are narrower than the headlines. Of the 10,052 people Novo measured, 80% had type 2 diabetes and more than half took the Rybelsus pill, not a Wegovy shot.[4][22] In SELECT, the big Wegovy heart trial in people without diabetes, blood was tested once after the start, at week 20: 3 of 21 clocks read younger, one read older, and 17 didn’t change.[4][22] The clock that read older was the fat-tissue clock, because the drug lowered leptin, a hormone that falls with fat loss.[4][6] Lilly’s tirzepatide data had no placebo group.[2][10] No clock is an accepted measure of aging, and none of the 8 registered GLP-1 trials with an aging-clock outcome has results.[16][21]

Why this matters now

Both companies showed these data at the Aging Research & Drug Discovery meeting at Harvard, October 1–3.[9] MIT Technology Review broke the story on October 6, and Medical Daily and CNN followed on October 8 and 13.[1][3][2] On X, posts tying GLP-1 drugs to aging clocks or longevity have run at 16 to 33 a day since then. This morning one account was still circulating the line that semaglutide made hearts test “2 to 4 years biologically younger.”[23] For anyone weighing whether to start or stay on one of these drugs, “anti-aging” is now part of the pitch.

The short answer

  • What moved: blood-protein estimates of organ age, most consistently a heart clock, 2 to 4 years younger than placebo. Most of the broad changes came from diabetes trials after a year or more.[4][22]
  • What didn’t: the clock built from proteins outside the organ sets, the closest thing to a whole-body clock, was “unchanged” in people without diabetes. In one heart-failure trial without diabetes, no clock differed from placebo at one year.[5][4][22]
  • What went the wrong way: the fat-tissue clock read older early on, because the drug lowered leptin.[4][6]
  • Tirzepatide: two small analyses with no placebo, presented at a conference. There is no paper or preprint.[2][10][9]
  • What is proven: fewer heart attacks, strokes and kidney failures in high-risk patients, and fewer deaths from any cause in one kidney trial. None of this has been tested in healthy people.[17][18][19]

What Novo actually measured

Novo used blood samples it had already stored from five of its outcome trials. It ran them on a large protein panel (Olink Explore HT), then applied clocks published in 2025 by Goeminne and colleagues.[4][7] Those clocks were trained in UK Biobank to predict death. Each organ clock is a weighted sum of proteins linked to that organ, converted into “years.” There are 18 organ clocks and 3 combined ones, 21 in all.[4][7]

Who the 10,052 were. SOUL alone contributed 5,255 people with type 2 diabetes taking oral semaglutide, the Rybelsus pill. FLOW added 2,271 with diabetes and kidney disease on 1.0 mg, the Ozempic dose. Only 2,526 (25%) were in trials of the 2.4 mg Wegovy dose, and only 2,057 people did not have diabetes.[4][22] About half of each trial got placebo.[4] Every trial enrolled people who were already sick: heart disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease or high cardiovascular risk.[4]

SELECT, the Wegovy trial most readers would think of. Blood was tested only at the start and at week 20. The 1,641 people were not a random sample. They were picked to over-represent people who went on to have heart events: 781 of them had a heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death, against 1,270 of all 17,604 in the trial.[4] At week 20, the heart, kidney and thyroid clocks read younger than placebo, the fat-tissue clock read older, and the other 17 didn’t differ significantly. That included all three combined clocks.[4][22] When Novo weighted the sample to look like the full trial, 1 clock read younger and 1 older.[4][22]

Where the big numbers come from. The clocks moved more over time in the diabetes trials. In FLOW, 17 of 21 clocks read younger than placebo at week 156, and in SOUL 9 did at week 104. In STEP-HFpEF, a heart-failure trial without diabetes, 1 read younger and 2 read older at week 20, and none differed at week 52.[4][22] Novo’s June ADA abstract, which added the STEP 1 and STEP 2 weight-loss trials, reported the combined-organ clock 5.2% lower in SELECT, 4.5% lower in STEP 1 and 7.2% lower in STEP 2.[5]

The “whole body” clock didn’t move

Medical Daily listed a “whole body” clock among those that got younger.[3] The preprint abstract names a “multiorgan” clock. It combines the 569 proteins in the organ clocks.[4][6] A different clock, called organismal, uses the 2,347 proteins that are not organ-specific, so it is the nearest thing to a whole-body reading. Novo’s June ADA abstract says plainly: “We found no effect of semaglutide on organismal biological age.” On the cross-platform data, the poster calls it a “consistent but nonsignificant trend.”[5][6] In the preprint’s chart, the organismal clock did read lower at one year or later in two diabetes trials, STEP-HFpEF DM and FLOW. It did not in SELECT, STEP-HFpEF or SOUL. That reading is ours, from Figure 2a.[4]

What the “younger heart” is made of

The heart clock is seven proteins. Its top contributors include NPPB, the gene for BNP, and TNNI3, cardiac troponin I.[6][8] Doctors already test for those proteins as signs of heart strain and damage. Novo names NPPB among the proteins behind the lower heart score. In its outcome models, NPPB was also the protein most tied to the heart clock’s link with fewer deaths.[4] So in plain terms, a “2 to 4 years younger heart” means lower levels of a few heart-strain proteins. That fits the drug’s known benefits in heart disease and heart failure. It is a different claim from slower aging of the whole body.[4][17]

The clock that got older

In SELECT at week 20, the fat-tissue (adipose) clock read older on semaglutide than on placebo. It did the same early in three other trials.[4][6] The cause was leptin, a hormone fat cells release, which falls as people lose fat. In UK Biobank, where the clock was trained, low leptin may reflect unintended weight loss from frailty or illness. So the clock treats lower leptin as a sign of aging.[4][8] Novo’s authors say this “highlights an important limitation of mortality-trained clocks in interventional studies.” Weight loss was the main driver of this signal, and at later time points the fat-tissue clock read younger.[4] In Novo’s outcome model for SELECT, the adipose clock’s rise offset an estimated 48% of the effect on death from any cause. The heart clock explained an estimated 36%.[4] Lilly’s poster flags the same problem from the other side. The top protein in the artery clock was elastin, which rose more in people who lost more weight. Lilly’s authors say that clock may be tracking “weight-loss-driven elastin turnover, not arterial aging.”[11]

Is it just weight loss?

The companies answer differently. Novo’s mediation analysis found that most heart and kidney clock changes were not explained by weight change, HbA1c or C-reactive protein.[4] At the meeting, Novo’s Aguayo-Orozco added that this doesn’t prove the effect is independent of weight loss.[11] Lilly’s Kevin Duffin told the same audience that the drugs work through blood-sugar reduction and weight loss, and that “the effects we’re seeing are mostly due to that.”[2] One comparison cuts against weight loss as the whole story. A review of 41 human studies lists semaglutide among drugs that lowered at least one epigenetic clock, while a 12-month diet-based weight-loss trial in 112 adults with obesity showed no significant change.[15]

What Lilly showed for Zepbound and Mounjaro

Lilly’s data come from SURMOUNT-5, an open-label trial that randomized 751 adults without diabetes to tirzepatide or semaglutide for 72 weeks.[12] For DNA-methylation clocks, Lilly analyzed 71 people over 50, from the tirzepatide arm only. In the parent trial, which enrolled people without diabetes, average HbA1c was about 5.6%.[11] All 15 clocks advanced less than the 1.38 years that passed, but only 2 differences were statistically significant. DunedinPACE, which estimates pace of aging, went from a median 1.13 to 1.08.[2][10] There was no placebo group, and the reports show no comparison with the semaglutide arm, though the trial had one.[2][10][11] A separate Lilly poster used the same protein clocks as Novo on 301 tirzepatide patients. It found 7 of 18 clocks lower at week 72, again with no placebo.[9][11] We found no paper or preprint for either analysis. Lilly says it plans to extend the work to semaglutide.[10]

Independent studies

The strongest evidence from outside Novo and Lilly is a small randomized trial. Researchers reanalyzed blood from a 32-week trial of semaglutide 1.0 mg in 84 people with HIV and abnormal fat buildup. Several next-generation epigenetic clocks slowed compared with placebo; PhenoAge, for example, by 4.9 years per year, and DunedinPACE by 9%.[13] Clocks weren’t planned in that trial, and two authors work for the epigenetic-testing company TruDiagnostic.[13] In another group of 41 people with HIV and fatty liver, given semaglutide for 24 weeks with no control group, two clocks didn’t slow on average: DunedinPACE rose 0.018 and PCGrimAge 0.54 years.[14] In mice, semaglutide started late in life extended lifespan in females.[20]

What the hard outcomes already show

For the kinds of patients in these analyses, outcome trials have counted real events. In SELECT, semaglutide cut heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths by 20% over a mean of 39.8 months.[17] In FLOW, it cut death from any cause by 20% in people with diabetes and kidney disease.[18] In SOUL, the pill cut major cardiovascular events by 14%.[19] Those results are the real reason to believe these drugs help sick people live longer. The clocks add a guess at how. In SELECT, the heart clock change accounted for an estimated 36% of the drop in death from any cause. That analysis was exploratory, and the authors say it doesn’t establish “causal mediation.”[4][6]

What we counted on ClinicalTrials.gov

We pulled all 1,040 studies that list semaglutide, tirzepatide or retatrutide and read the 14 that mention biological age, aging clocks, epigenetic age, longevity or senescence. 8 register an aging clock as an outcome, and none has results. 5 are randomized, the largest with 110 people, and only 2 compare against placebo or no drug. All 8 together plan to enroll 1,036 people. None is run by Novo or Lilly, and none tests retatrutide. The trials behind the industry analyses (SELECT, STEP-HFpEF, STEP-HFpEF DM, FLOW, SOUL, STEP 1, STEP 2 and SURMOUNT-5) list no aging-clock outcome, because the clock work was done afterward.[21] The first registered completion date is December 31, 2026. An ARPA-H-funded trial of semaglutide in healthy people in their 60s has been announced, but recruitment isn’t due to start until 2027.[21][3][1]

Strengths and limits

Strengths. Novo’s data are large and randomized. They come from five placebo-controlled trials, and the clocks were published before the analysis and not retrained. The heart clock read younger in all five trials, and a check on a second protein platform in STEP 1 and STEP 2 pointed the same way.[4] The baseline clock readings also predicted later heart and kidney events in placebo patients, so these clocks track something that matters.[4]

Limits. The preprint isn’t peer reviewed, and Novo funded it; 8 of its 12 authors are current or former Novo employees. The two Harvard authors are inventors on a patent application for the clocks they applied.[4][7] Lilly’s data are conference slides without a control group.[10] The clocks both companies used were trained on observational data, not on treated patients, and can misread weight loss.[4][11][10] Each trial and time point was tested separately, with different drug doses and forms, so there is no pooled answer.[4] Researchers have not agreed how to validate any biomarker of aging, and clocks can vary day to day in the same person.[16][2] None of the people tested was a healthy, lean adult.[4][1]

Bottom line

Do Ozempic, Wegovy and Zepbound slow aging? The honest answer is that nobody has shown it. In sick, mostly diabetic patients, semaglutide made several blood-protein clocks read younger, especially a heart clock built largely from heart-strain proteins, and more of them over one to three years.[4][6] In SELECT at week 20, most clocks didn’t move and one moved the wrong way, and the whole-body clock was unchanged in people without diabetes.[4][5][22] Lilly’s tirzepatide data have no placebo.[10] What these drugs have proven is fewer heart, stroke and kidney events in high-risk patients.[17][18][19] Whether they slow aging in healthy people is a question for trials that haven’t reported, and of the 8 registered so far, none has results.[21]

Frequently asked questions

Do Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound slow aging?
Nobody knows yet. Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly report that some blood-based aging clocks read younger on their drugs, but those clocks are estimates that have not been validated as proof of slower aging. The people tested were mostly sick: in Novo’s data, about 80% had type 2 diabetes and all had heart disease, heart failure, kidney disease or high cardiovascular risk. What the drugs have shown in large trials is fewer heart attacks, strokes and kidney failures, and in one kidney trial fewer deaths, in those high-risk groups. [4][16][17][18][19]
What did Novo Nordisk find about semaglutide and biological age?
In a preprint that has not been peer reviewed, Novo applied 21 published blood-protein clocks to 10,052 people in five semaglutide trials. The heart clock read about 2 to 4 years younger than placebo, and kidney, brain, pancreas, lung and combined-organ clocks 1 to 4 years younger at some time points. In SELECT, the large Wegovy heart-outcomes trial in people without diabetes, 3 clocks read younger, 1 read older and 17 did not change at week 20, the only time point measured there. [4][22]
What did Lilly find about Zepbound or Mounjaro (tirzepatide) and aging?
Lilly presented two small analyses at a conference, with no paper or preprint. In 71 people over 50 on tirzepatide, 15 DNA-methylation clocks all advanced less than the 1.38 years that passed, but only 2 of the differences were statistically significant, and there was no placebo or comparison group. A second Lilly poster found 7 of 18 protein clocks lower in the tirzepatide arm, again with no placebo, and its authors said the artery clock may just be tracking elastin changes from weight loss. [2][9][10][11]
Why did one aging clock get older on semaglutide?
The adipose (fat-tissue) clock read older early in treatment because leptin fell, as it does when people lose fat. The clock was built from UK Biobank data, where low leptin may reflect frailty or illness, so it counts low leptin as a bad sign. Novo’s own authors say this shows how clocks trained on observational data can misread intentional weight loss. At later time points the adipose clock read younger. [4][8]
Should healthy people take a GLP-1 to slow aging?
There is no evidence for that yet. None of these analyses studied lean, healthy people, and Gladyshev, a Harvard scientist who helped Novo with the analysis, told MIT Technology Review that in a healthy population “no one knows.” Trials in older adults are planned or recruiting. Of 8 registered trials of these drugs that list an aging-clock outcome, none has results yet. The drugs have side effects, and in SELECT twice as many people stopped semaglutide as placebo because of adverse events. [1][3][4][17][21]
Are aging clocks accepted proof that a drug slows aging?
No. Researchers have not agreed how to validate biomarkers of aging, and Novo’s preprint itself says its clock readouts are “not yet surrogate endpoints.” Clocks can also vary from day to day in the same person, which is why one expert quoted by CNN warned against over-reading them. [2][4][16]
