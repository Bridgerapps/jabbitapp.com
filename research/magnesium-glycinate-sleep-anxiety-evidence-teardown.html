Research review · Magnesium glycinate · Sleep · Anxiety · Older adults · Ozempic · Zepbound · Updated October 28, 2026

Published October 28, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

Does magnesium glycinate help sleep or anxiety? The evidence is thin. The one placebo-controlled glycinate trial we found showed a small four-week drop in insomnia scores and no change in stress, anxiety or mood, and the widely shared “248 mg” anxiety trial tested a different form, without a placebo.

Magnesium glycinate is having a moment on X. Posts this week credit it with calmer nerves and deeper sleep, and a trending summary cites a trial in which 248 mg a day “cut depression and anxiety scores.” Readers want to know whether it actually helps sleep or anxiety. Our plain answer: the best glycinate trial we found showed a real but small improvement in one insomnia score over four weeks, with no effect on stress, anxiety or mood scores. The “248 mg” trial used magnesium chloride and had no placebo group. A 2026 review of 12 trials concluded that current evidence “does not support oral magnesium as a routine treatment for insomnia.”[3][5][7][6]

Why this matters now

Posts on X mentioning magnesium glycinate rose from 126 on October 22 to 261 on October 27, 2026, by Jabbit’s count.[4] On October 27, entrepreneur Pieter Levels (@levelsio) posted that his diet gives him “about 200mg of magnesium per day” against a need of “about 420mg,” and that “About 60% of people are magnesium deficient.” The post had about 138,000 impressions by the evening of October 28.[2] The next morning he posted “+30min REM sleep with magnesium glycinate!” adding, fairly, “It's just one day so too early to conclude.”[1] The same day, an X trending summary said “a trial showed 248 mg daily cut depression and anxiety scores noticeably in weeks.”[3] We check those claims against the published research. We do not assess the people who made them.

The glycinate trial: what it found

  • The design. 155 adults aged 18 to 65 with self-reported poor sleep took magnesium bisglycinate or a placebo for four weeks, at sites across Germany. The trial arm got 250 mg of elemental magnesium a day.[5]
  • The main result. Insomnia Severity Index scores fell 3.9 points with magnesium and 2.3 with placebo. The 1.6-point gap was just statistically significant (p = 0.049). The authors call the effect “small,” at a Cohen’s d of 0.2, “indicating a modest benefit.”[5]
  • Placebo did a lot. Jabbit’s analysis: by these figures, the placebo group got more than half of the improvement seen with magnesium.[5]
  • Everything else. The trial measured 10 other outcomes, including a second insomnia scale, sleep quality, daytime sleepiness, fatigue, perceived stress, mood and a short anxiety-and-depression screen. None differed from placebo.[5]
  • A subgroup signal. In exploratory analyses, people who reported lower dietary magnesium seemed to improve more. The authors note their diet tool was not validated.[5]

The “248 mg” anxiety trial, checked

The 248 mg figure in the trending summary matches a 2017 US trial run in primary care clinics.[3][7] In it, 126 adults with mild-to-moderate depression took 248 mg of elemental magnesium a day for six weeks, compared with six weeks of no treatment. Depression scores (PHQ-9) improved by a net 6.0 points and anxiety scores (GAD-7) by 4.5 points.[7] Three things matter here:

  • It was not glycinate. The tablets were magnesium chloride.[7]
  • There was no placebo. The paper says “There was no placebo arm and randomization was not blinded for either the study team or the volunteer.”[7] People who know they are getting a treatment often report feeling better.
  • It enrolled people with depression. It did not test magnesium in healthy people with everyday stress.[7]

Jabbit’s analysis: when the glycinate form was tested against placebo, anxiety and stress scores did not move.[5]

What reviews of all the trials say

  • Sleep, 2026. A review of 12 randomized trials found results “inconsistent” across sleep scores, brain-wave sleep recordings, actigraphy, consumer wearables and lab markers. Certainty was “low to very low.” It says magnesium “may provide modest benefits for selected subjective outcomes in some adults.”[6]
  • Older adults, 2021. Pooling 3 trials in 151 older adults, people fell asleep 17.4 minutes faster with magnesium. Total sleep time rose 16 minutes, but that result was not significant. All three trials were at moderate-to-high risk of bias, and none used glycinate. They used magnesium oxide or citrate.[8]
  • Sleep and anxiety, 2024. A review of 15 studies found improvements in 5 of 8 sleep studies and 5 of 7 anxiety studies. The authors say firm conclusions were limited by small, varied studies.[10]
  • Anxiety and stress, 2017. A review of 18 studies called the evidence quality “poor.” None used a validated stress measure.[11]

Sleep in later life: the longevity angle

Much of the older magnesium sleep research was done in older adults. That group may have the most reason to pay attention. NIH says older adults eat less magnesium than younger adults, absorb less from the gut, and lose more through the kidneys.[14] Even so, the trials in older adults are small. They used forms other than glycinate and carry a high risk of bias.[8][9] In the 2012 trial in 46 older adults, total sleep time did not differ from placebo.[9] Jabbit’s analysis: no glycinate trial we found tested people over 65. The 2025 trial’s upper age limit was 65.[5]

Is it the magnesium or the glycine?

Magnesium glycinate is magnesium bound to the amino acid glycine. Glycine has been studied for sleep on its own. In the 2025 trial, the daily dose included 1,523 mg of glycine, and the authors note that some studies suggest about 3 g of glycine may help sleep quality.[5] An older glycine study used 3 g.[13] Jabbit’s analysis: the trial dose had about half that amount of glycine, and the trial was not designed to tell the two apart.[5][13]

Claims in the posts, checked

  • “+30min REM sleep.” One night of wearable data cannot show an effect, as the poster himself said.[1] The 2026 review found consumer wearable estimates among the outcomes that were inconsistent across trials.[6]
  • “About 420mg per day.” NIH’s adult RDAs range from 310 to 420 mg depending on age and sex. That includes food.[14][2]
  • “About 60% of people are magnesium deficient.” We could not match this figure in NIH’s fact sheet. NIH says 48% of Americans take in less magnesium from food than the average requirement. It also says there is “no current data on magnesium status” in the US, and that symptomatic deficiency from low intake is “uncommon” in otherwise healthy people.[14] Jabbit’s reading: low intake is common, but low intake is not the same as a measured deficiency.
  • Is glycinate the better-absorbed form? The evidence is thinner than often claimed. NIH lists aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride as better absorbed than oxide and sulfate, and does not list glycinate.[14] In one small 1994 study, glycinate and oxide were absorbed about equally, though glycinate was better tolerated.[12]

Safety: what is known

  • Upper limit. NIH sets the adult upper limit for magnesium from supplements and medicines at 350 mg a day. That limit does not count food.[14]
  • Diarrhea. NIH says carbonate, chloride, gluconate and oxide are the forms most often reported to cause diarrhea.[14] In the 2025 trial, there were 7 adverse events with placebo vs 2 with magnesium. Two placebo-group participants withdrew because of stomach pain.[5]
  • Kidneys. The risk of magnesium toxicity “increases with impaired renal function or kidney failure.”[14]
  • Medicine interactions. NIH notes that magnesium can bind some antibiotics (tetracyclines and quinolones) and lower absorption of bisphosphonates used for bone loss. It advises taking those antibiotics at least 2 hours before or 4 to 6 hours after magnesium. Long-term proton pump inhibitor use can lower magnesium levels.[14] Ask a pharmacist about timing.

What a careful reader should notice

  1. One trial, one product, four weeks. The glycinate evidence rests mainly on a single trial with self-reported outcomes. Fewer than 10% of participants completed their sleep diaries.[5]
  2. Who ran it. One co-author directs a contract research organization that “receives research funding from nutraceutical companies,” per the paper.[5] That does not make the data wrong, and the trial’s own modest result is reported plainly.
  3. The p-value was borderline. At p = 0.049, with 10 other outcomes not significant, the main finding is fragile. That is our analysis.[5]
  4. Forms get mixed up. Viral claims often cite chloride or oxide trials as evidence for glycinate.[7][8][9]
  5. Some findings are positive. Small trials and reviews do report benefits for sleep onset and self-reported anxiety. Better trials could still confirm a modest effect.[8][10]

If you are on a GLP-1 drug

People on Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound or Mounjaro often eat much less. Jabbit’s analysis: eating less food likely also means getting less magnesium from food. We found no trial of magnesium supplements in GLP-1 users. Stomach side effects matter too. Some magnesium forms can cause diarrhea, and GLP-1 drugs commonly cause gut side effects of their own, including constipation.[14][15] Our reading: a change in bowel habits after adding magnesium could be hard to pin on one cause. Our GLP-1 constipation guide covers what helps there. Tell your prescriber or pharmacist about any supplement, especially if you have kidney disease or take the medicines listed above. FAQ

Does magnesium glycinate help you sleep?
Possibly a little. In the largest placebo-controlled glycinate trial, 155 adults’ insomnia scores improved 1.6 points more than with placebo over four weeks, a small effect. Other sleep measures did not differ. A 2026 review says magnesium is not supported as a routine insomnia treatment.
Does magnesium glycinate help anxiety?
It is not proven. In the glycinate trial, stress and anxiety scores did not differ from placebo. A widely cited 2017 trial that found lower anxiety scores used magnesium chloride and had no placebo group.
Was the “248 mg” trial about magnesium glycinate?
No. The 2017 trial gave 248 mg of elemental magnesium a day as magnesium chloride, to adults with depression, compared with no treatment. It was open-label, with no placebo.
Is magnesium glycinate better absorbed than other forms?
That is not well established. NIH lists aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride as better absorbed than oxide and sulfate. A small 1994 study found glycinate and oxide absorbed about equally, though glycinate was better tolerated.
Are most people magnesium deficient?
NIH says 48% of Americans get less magnesium from food than the average requirement, but US magnesium status is not tracked, and symptomatic deficiency is uncommon in otherwise healthy people.
Can I take magnesium with Ozempic or Zepbound?
No trial has tested it in GLP-1 users. Both can affect your gut, and magnesium interacts with some antibiotics and bone drugs. Ask your prescriber or pharmacist, especially if you have kidney disease.

Bottom line

Magnesium glycinate may modestly help self-reported insomnia, based mainly on one four-week trial with a small, borderline result. The same trial found no effect on stress, anxiety or mood. The anxiety trial behind the viral “248 mg” claim used a different form and had no placebo, and one night of wearable data cannot settle anything. In our read, the honest claim today is “small, uncertain benefit for sleep, unproven for anxiety.” Longer independent trials, including in older adults and people on GLP-1 drugs, would answer the question better than any post.
