Status tracker · FDA category lists, compounding advisory committee votes, Drugs@FDA and the human evidence for every peptide Jabbit has covered · Sources checked October 24, 2026

Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

Which peptides is FDA reviewing, and can pharmacies compound them in 2026? A sourced status table for 21 peptides and NAD+, from BPC-157 to sermorelin.

FDA has not added a single peptide to the list of bulk ingredients that pharmacies may compound under section 503A; that list holds six substances, none of them peptides.[6] Of the 21 peptides we have covered, 16 sit on FDA’s page of substances that may present significant safety risks, at least for some routes; 10 have already gone before FDA’s compounding advisory committee, and 5 more are due before the end of February 2027.[1][8][18][19][7] Four are or were ingredients of FDA-approved drugs, which is a different legal path.[21] Meanwhile an interim FDA peptide policy cleared White House review on October 27, 2026, with changes, but its text isn’t public, so nobody outside government knows which peptides it covers yet.[20] The table below shows where each one stands, with every status linked to an FDA source.

How a pharmacy can legally compound a peptide

A traditional compounding pharmacy (section 503A) may make a drug from a bulk ingredient only if the ingredient has a USP or NF monograph, is a component of an FDA-approved drug, or is on FDA’s 503A bulks list.[4][5] FDA builds that list by regulation, after consulting its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee, and so far the list holds six mostly topical substances.[5][6] While the list is incomplete, FDA’s interim policy says it does not intend to take action against compounding with substances in its “category 1,” under evaluation, if other conditions are met.[3] Category 2 holds substances that raise significant safety risks, and FDA also keeps a page of nominations that were withdrawn, with its safety findings attached.[1][2]

Two points are easy to miss. A committee vote is advice, not a decision: FDA can disagree with it and must still go through rulemaking.[5][6] And a withdrawn nomination does not mean a substance was cleared. Our reading, which is not legal advice, is that a peptide that is neither in category 1 nor in an approved drug is not covered by the interim policy’s enforcement discretion; a medical publisher summarizing the July votes lists those peptides as “Not on 503A list, not in Category 1.”[3][16]

What the table shows

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. The evidence and the votes point in different directions. For the six peptides the committee backed in July, FDA’s staff concluded the balance of the criteria weighed against them. Our teardowns found the human evidence thin for all six: no human data for KPV itself, TB-500 or MOTS-c, and small, mostly uncontrolled or developer-run studies for BPC-157, semax and epitalon.[9][10][11][12][13][14][16]
  2. The 2024 and 2026 committees voted very differently. In 2024 the panel sided with FDA staff on every peptide we track, often unanimously; in July 2026 it overrode staff on six of seven.[19][18][16] Our DSIP teardown looks at how the July votes split.
  3. “Approved” usually means one narrow use. Each of the four approved peptides is approved for a specific condition, from Barth syndrome to HIV-associated belly fat, not for the anti-aging or recovery uses they are often marketed for.[24][26][27][22]
  4. Some companies treat a peptide as allowed when it has an approved-drug path. One large telehealth company described sermorelin, glutathione and NAD+ as “already allowed peptides” in August 2026.[23] That matches the paths in the table for sermorelin (former approved drug) and for glutathione and NAD+ (category 1), but FDA’s own 2019 proposed rule would have left NAD off the list.[22][2][29]
  5. The biggest unknown is the interim policy. Its text is not public, so whether it changes any row above is unknown; we will update the table when it is published.[20]

Frequently asked questions

Which peptides is FDA reviewing in 2026?
FDA’s compounding advisory committee voted on BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-c, semax, epitalon and DSIP in July 2026, and FDA says it will bring GHK-Cu, LL-37, dihexa, melanotan II and PEG-MGF to the committee before the end of February 2027. FDA has not announced final decisions.[8][16][7]
Can pharmacies compound BPC-157 in 2026?
BPC-157 is not on FDA’s 503A bulks list, is not in category 1 and is not in any approved drug. FDA staff recommended against listing it; the committee voted for it, 8-6-1 as reported, but that vote is advisory and FDA has not acted. Our reading, not legal advice, is that nothing has yet changed its status.[6][2][9][16][21]
What does FDA “category 2” mean for peptides?
Category 2 holds bulk ingredients FDA says raise significant safety risks. Most peptides sold online appear on FDA’s related page of nominations that were withdrawn, with FDA’s safety findings listed, often that it has little or no human safety data.[2][1]
Does a committee vote make a peptide legal to compound?
No. The committee advises FDA; a substance joins the 503A bulks list only through FDA rulemaking, and the list currently contains no peptides.[5][6]
Is sermorelin legal to compound?
Sermorelin was the active ingredient in Geref, an approved drug that was discontinued, and FDA determined it was not withdrawn for safety or effectiveness reasons. Compounders generally treat it as permitted on that basis. Whether a specific product is lawful depends on how it is made, and this is not legal advice.[22][21][4][23]
Which peptides are FDA approved?
Of those we cover, bremelanotide (Vyleesi), elamipretide (Forzinity) and tesamorelin (Egrifta) are approved, each for one narrow use, and sermorelin was approved as Geref until it was discontinued.[21][24][26][27][22]
Is GHK-Cu allowed in compounded creams?
FDA put non-injectable GHK-Cu back in category 1, under evaluation, in May 2026, while injectable GHK-Cu remains on its safety-risk page. GHK-Cu is due before the committee by the end of February 2027.[2][1][7]

Bottom line

As of October 24, 2026, no peptide is on FDA’s 503A bulks list, and most of the peptides marketed for recovery, longevity or the brain sit on FDA’s safety-risk page with little or no human safety data.[6][1] The July committee votes raised hopes, but they are advisory and FDA’s own staff recommended the opposite.[16][9] The only peptides with clear legal paths are those in approved drugs or in category 1, and even those have limits.[4][3][2] We will update this tracker when FDA publishes its interim policy or announces decisions.
