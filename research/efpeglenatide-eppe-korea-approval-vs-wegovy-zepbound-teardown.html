Study teardown · Efpeglenatide (Eppe) vs semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) · Korea approval · Korean label, trial registries, outcome trials and US labels · Sources checked October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

What is efpeglenatide (Eppe), Korea’s new weight-loss shot, and how does it compare with Wegovy and Zepbound? South Korea approved it on October 7, 2026, for adults with obesity or overweight who do not have diabetes. In its 40-week Korean trial, people lost about 8 percentage points more weight than on placebo, less than Wegovy or Zepbound in their own longer trials, with fewer reported stomach side effects but a larger rise in heart rate. It is not approved in the US.

The short answer: efpeglenatide is a once-weekly GLP-1 injection from Hanmi Pharmaceutical, the same drug class as Wegovy. Korea’s drug regulator, the MFDS, approved it on October 7, 2026, as the first GLP-1 obesity drug developed in Korea.[1] Its approved Korean label reports that, over 40 weeks, people on the drug lost 9.02% of their body weight vs 0.89% on placebo, a difference of 8.13 percentage points.[2] That is a real effect, but smaller than what Wegovy and Zepbound showed in their own, longer trials.[17][18][15] It has not been approved by the FDA.[19]

Why this matters now

Aju Press reported on October 23 that Eppe “will become available at some clinics from Oct. 29,” and that Hanmi disclosed the date at a Seoul medical symposium.[6] It is a new entrant in the GLP-1 class, and one that already has a completed cardiovascular outcomes trial, run in people with type 2 diabetes.[10] Readers in the US are asking whether it is better than Wegovy or Zepbound and whether they can get it. This page sets the Korean label next to the US labels and the East Asian trials of Wegovy and Zepbound.

Two numbers are circulating. Hanmi’s October 2025 release and its October 2026 approval release give an interim topline weight loss of 9.75% vs 0.95%.[3][4] The approved label gives 9.02% vs 0.89%, from the full analysis set using multiple imputation for missing weights.[2] Our analysis: different analysis methods often give different numbers; the company release does not state its method, so we lead with the regulator-approved label figure.

What the Korean trial showed

  • Who was in it. 448 Korean adults without diabetes, randomized 2 to 1 to drug or placebo; average age 43, 62% women, average weight 89 kg and BMI 32. At the start, 56% had high blood pressure and 61% abnormal blood fats.[2]
  • Weight. 9.02% loss vs 0.89% at 40 weeks; 75% vs 14% lost at least 5% of their weight. The label says the loss tended to be maintained through week 64 in the open-label extension, where everyone got the drug.[2]
  • Dropouts. 8.4% stopped the drug during the placebo period vs 8.7% on placebo; 1.2% stopped permanently because of stomach side effects.[2]
  • Not yet in a journal. We found no peer-reviewed paper for this trial, and ClinicalTrials.gov had no posted results when we checked. The label is the most detailed public source.[7][2]

How it compares with Wegovy and Zepbound

No trial has compared efpeglenatide directly with Wegovy or Zepbound. Side by side, the separate trials show a smaller effect: 8.13 percentage points beyond placebo at 40 weeks, against 11.1 points for Wegovy in STEP 6 (Japan and Korea, 68 weeks) and 12.4 points in its US label trial, and 17.8 points for the highest Zepbound dose in its US label trial.[2][15][17][18] Tirzepatide’s Japanese trial reported up to 21.1 points, using an on-treatment estimate that tends to give larger numbers.[16]

The trial’s principal investigator, as reported by Korea Biomedical Review, said trials of the imported drugs enrolled people with an average BMI around 38, vs about 32 here, and that this should be considered.[5] Our analysis: that is a fair point for the US trials, but STEP 6 also enrolled East Asian adults and still showed a larger effect over a longer trial, so a lower starting weight does not seem to explain the whole gap. Trial length matters too: weight loss on these drugs usually keeps building past 40 weeks.

Side effects

  • Stomach effects. Nausea (16.6% vs 5.4% on placebo), diarrhea (17.6% vs 4.8%) and vomiting (11.6% vs 2.0%) were the most common, mostly mild to moderate and more frequent while the dose was being raised.[2] Those rates are lower than in the Wegovy and Zepbound labels.[17][18] Hanmi says its slower, stepwise dose increases are meant to reduce these effects, and has described its rates as lower than competitors’.[4][3] Our analysis: without a head-to-head trial, smaller weight loss and a different population could explain part of the difference. Korea Biomedical Review likewise notes that direct comparative trials would be needed to show better tolerability.[5]
  • Heart rate. Resting heart rate rose by 6.8 beats per minute on average vs a 0.1 fall on placebo, and 8.4% vs 0.7% had a reading above 100 with a rise of at least 15.[2] The Wegovy and Zepbound labels list average rises of 1 to 4 and 1 to 3 beats per minute.[17][18] Our analysis: this is a cross-trial comparison, but the gap is large enough to be worth watching.
  • Gallbladder, thyroid and pancreas. Gallstones occurred in 1.4% over the whole trial, and 2 people needed their gallbladder removed. Thyroid disorders (lumps, cysts, worsening Graves’ disease or an overactive thyroid) occurred in 1.4%, with none on placebo. Pancreatic enzyme levels (amylase and lipase) rose more on the drug than on placebo.[2]
  • Antibodies. 17.5% developed antibodies to the drug and 3% neutralizing antibodies. The label says no evidence was found that antibodies affected how the drug worked, but allergic-type and injection-site reactions were more frequent in people who developed them.[2]
  • Thyroid tumor warning. As with Wegovy and Zepbound, the label warns that the drug caused thyroid C-cell tumors in rats and that human relevance cannot be ruled out, and rules out use in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer or MEN 2.[2][17][18]

Who it is approved for, and who was not studied

In Korea it is approved, alongside diet and exercise, for adults without diabetes with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with at least one weight-related condition.[2] The label does not recommend it for groups not studied: people with diabetes, moderate-to-severe kidney impairment, liver impairment, a history of severe psychiatric illness, severe heart failure, or those using other weight-loss products. It should be stopped at least 9 weeks before a planned pregnancy, and it was studied only in Korean adults.[2] We do not list doses here; dosing belongs with a prescriber and the label.

Healthy aging: why this matters for longevity

The longevity case for efpeglenatide rests on AMPLITUDE-O. In 4,076 people with type 2 diabetes and heart or kidney disease, major cardiovascular events fell from 9.2% to 7.0% (hazard ratio 0.73), and a kidney composite from 18.4% to 13.0%, over a median of 1.81 years.[10] The higher dose did better than the lower one, and a later analysis found that changes in LDL cholesterol and urine albumin, not weight, explained part of the benefit.[11][12] Our analysis: that is encouraging for heart and kidney health, but it was a diabetes trial funded by Sanofi, and there is no outcome trial in people with obesity alone, as Korea Biomedical Review also notes.[10][5]

The case for caution

The obesity evidence is one 40-week trial in one country, reported so far through a label and company releases rather than a peer-reviewed paper.[2][7] The two main numbers differ because of different analyses.[3][2] The heart-rate rise was larger than in the US labels of the leading drugs.[2][17][18] An earlier phase 2 trial saw 5% to 19% stop for side effects, though with different dosing.[14] And Sanofi’s global program was ended for reasons registries and the trial authors describe as not related to safety; the trial paper describes funding as the reason.[9][13]

What a careful reader should notice

  1. The label figure (9.02%) and the company topline (9.75%) come from different analyses; neither is “wrong.”[2][3]
  2. Comparing 40 weeks with 68 or 72 weeks favors the longer trials.[2][15][18]
  3. Lower stomach side-effect rates are not proven in a direct comparison.[2][5]
  4. The cardiovascular benefit comes from people with diabetes, not from the obesity trial.[10][5]
  5. Hanmi has not disclosed a price, as of the October 8 report.[5]

These points are our analysis.

Frequently asked questions

What is Eppe (efpeglenatide)?
A once-weekly GLP-1 injection from Hanmi Pharmaceutical, approved in South Korea on October 7, 2026, for weight management in adults with obesity or overweight without diabetes.[1][2]
Is efpeglenatide better than Wegovy or Zepbound?
No direct trial exists. In separate trials it showed smaller weight loss (8.13 points beyond placebo at 40 weeks) than Wegovy or Zepbound in their longer trials, with lower reported stomach side-effect rates.[2][17][18][15]
How much weight do people lose on efpeglenatide?
9.02% on average at 40 weeks vs 0.89% on placebo, per the Korean label; 75% lost at least 5%. Hanmi’s earlier interim topline reported 9.75%.[2][3]
Is efpeglenatide FDA-approved or available in the US?
No. We found no FDA approval and no recruiting US trial; Hanmi’s current trial is in South Korea.[19][8]
Does efpeglenatide raise heart rate?
Yes, in its trial resting heart rate rose 6.8 beats per minute on average vs a 0.1 fall on placebo, more than the 1 to 4 beats listed for Wegovy and Zepbound in their own trials.[2][17][18]
Does efpeglenatide protect the heart?
In people with type 2 diabetes and heart or kidney disease, the AMPLITUDE-O trial found 27% fewer major cardiovascular events than placebo. It has not been tested for that in people with obesity alone.[10][5]

Bottom line

Efpeglenatide is a real addition to the GLP-1 class: approved in Korea, with a placebo-controlled weight-loss trial and a cardiovascular outcomes trial in diabetes behind it.[1][2][10] On weight loss, it looks smaller than Wegovy or Zepbound in separate trials, with fewer reported stomach side effects and a larger heart-rate rise.[2][17][18] Our analysis: for US readers it is not an option today, and for anyone else the right comparison will come from longer trials and, ideally, a head-to-head study.
