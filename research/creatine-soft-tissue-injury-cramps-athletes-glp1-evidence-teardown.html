Research review · Creatine · Pre-workout · Soft-tissue injuries · Cramps · Ozempic · Zepbound · Updated October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

Does creatine cause soft-tissue injuries or cramps? The published evidence does not show it. In college football players followed over three seasons, creatine users had injury rates lower than or in line with their share of the team, and a 2025 analysis of 685 trials found no overall rise in side effects. A few small studies do flag caveats.

A viral post this week told athletes to stop taking creatine, saying it is “like filling a balloon with air” and that this is “why soft tissue injuries happen.” Many people now take creatine, including some on Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound or Mounjaro who want to protect muscle. The reader’s question: does creatine cause muscle strains, pulls or cramps? Our plain answer: the studies that counted injuries in athletes did not find more of them in creatine users, and pooled trial data show no overall rise in side effects. The evidence has real limits, though: the football studies were open-label and run largely by researchers with creatine-industry ties, and a few small studies found pressure and muscle-damage signals worth knowing about.[1][4][7]

Why this matters now

On October 27, 2026, a public X account whose bio describes him as a retired NFL defensive tackle posted “STOP TAKING CREATINE,” comparing it to a balloon that fails “under stress.” The post had about 1.09 million impressions by that evening. The night before, the same account posted that “All soft tissue injuries among athletes come from creatine and pre-workout.”[1][2] English posts on X pairing creatine with injuries jumped from 3 to 5 a day earlier in the week to 145 on October 27, by Jabbit’s count.[3] We assess the claim, not the person.

What the injury studies show

  • Three football seasons. At one NCAA Division IA program, 34% to 56% of players took creatine from 1998 to 2000. Creatine users accounted for 39% of cramping cases, 49% of muscle pulls and strains, 43% of muscle tightness and 39% of all injuries and missed practices, which the authors describe as “generally lower or proportional to the creatine use rate.”[4] Jabbit’s reading: if creatine raised injury risk, users’ share of injuries should sit above their share of the team. It did not.
  • One season, more detail. In 1999, 38 of 72 players chose creatine. Users had significantly less cramping and no difference in non-contact joint injuries.[5]
  • Health markers. Over 21 months, 98 players showed no adverse change in a 69-item blood and urine panel.[6]
  • A newer randomized pilot. In 23 amateur soccer players over 14 weeks, injuries were 8.3% with creatine vs 36.4% with placebo. The confidence interval is wide and the result was not significant (p = 0.155).[21]
  • Women’s football. In 71 female players taking creatine, kidney and liver tests stayed normal. Creatine kinase, a muscle-damage marker, varied in a way the authors attribute to training load. There was no placebo group.[22]
  • Cramps and heat. A meta-analysis of 10 studies found no evidence that creatine hurts heat tolerance or fluid balance.[12] An earlier review called the cramping-and-dehydration idea “a fallacy” driven by “media claims and anecdotal reports.”[11]

What 685 trials report

A 2025 analysis pooled side effects from 685 published creatine trials with about 12,800 people on creatine and 13,450 on placebo. Side effects were reported in 13.7% of creatine study arms vs 13.2% of placebo arms, not a significant difference. Per participant, any side effect was reported by 4.60% vs 4.21%.[7] A 2026 follow-up analysis concluded creatine “does not increase the risk of gastrointestinal, renal, liver, musculoskeletal, or other SEs compared to placebo.”[8]

One signal to note: counted by study, muscle cramping or pain was reported in 2.9% of creatine arms vs 0.9% of placebo arms (p = 0.008). Counted by person, it was 0.52% vs 0.07%, which was not significant (p = 0.085).[7] Both rates are low, and the papers’ authors read them as no clear risk. Jabbit’s analysis: a small cramping signal cannot be ruled out from these data.

The “balloon” claim: water and muscle

  • Creatine does add water. It raises total body water, and it can raise lean mass on scans partly for that reason.[16][18]
  • But the water goes where it normally does. In 32 men and women, creatine raised muscle creatine, body mass and total body water, but “fluid distribution was not changed.”[16] In 27 trained men, the ratio of water inside cells to muscle mass did not change, which suggests the muscle gained was not just water.[17]
  • Pressure in the lower leg. This is the closest evidence to the balloon idea. In a blinded study of 14 men, creatine raised pressure in the front lower-leg muscle compartment at rest and after running.[13] One man’s pressure rose markedly and fell after he stopped.[14] In 11 trained men exercising in heat, pressure also rose, but the changes “did not induce symptoms” and were transient.[15] None of these studies measured injuries.

Safety: what is known

  • Muscle-damage markers. A 2022 meta-analysis of 23 studies found lower damage markers 2 to 4 days after a single hard workout with creatine, but higher markers at 24 hours with long-term use during training. The authors write that training stress “could be exacerbated.”[19] Markers in blood are not the same as injuries.
  • Reported cramps. In a 2000 survey, 38% of creatine-using college athletes reported perceived negative effects, mainly cramping or stomach upset.[20] Surveys can’t separate creatine from training, heat or expectation.
  • Case reports. One 23-year-old man developed severe rhabdomyolysis (muscle breakdown) after a training session of more than 4 hours while taking several supplements.[23] A 17-year-old developed acute kidney injury after a high-dose creatine loading phase.[24] Case reports can’t show cause, but they are a reason for caution, especially in teens.
  • Kidney labs. Creatine can raise blood creatinine, which is used to estimate kidney function. In a cited review, most studies showed no rise or a rise within normal range, and reported kidney cases were confounded by medicines, existing kidney disease or other supplements.[9] Tell your clinician you take creatine before kidney tests.
  • Adverse-event reports. Of FDA adverse-event reports mentioning creatine, 63% involved other forms of creatine or use with other supplements or drugs, per the 2025 analysis.[7]

What about pre-workout?

“Pre-workout” is a category, not one ingredient. A review of multi-ingredient pre-workout products found them relatively safe in short studies, mostly under 8 weeks, but warned that “many formulations may intentionally contain banned substances as ingredients or unintentionally as contaminants” and flagged “proprietary blends” that hide amounts.[25] A review of synephrine, a stimulant in some products, gathered 30 case reports of 35 people with medical complaints, mostly heart-related, while noting that caffeine may have played a role.[26] We found no study linking pre-workout as a category to soft-tissue injuries. Its stimulant and contamination risks are a different issue from creatine.

The case for the warning

The concern is not baseless. Creatine adds water and can raise compartment pressure in the lower leg.[16][13][14] A 2022 meta-analysis found higher damage markers with long-term use.[19] Athletes have long reported cramping, and a small study-level cramping signal appears in pooled data.[20][7] An American College of Sports Medicine statement advised caution in 2000.[11] Supplements with several ingredients carry contamination and stimulant risks.[25] And good diet and sleep matter for recovery regardless.

What a careful reader should notice

  1. No large randomized injury trial. The football data are open-label, at one university, with players choosing creatine. Users may have differed from non-users in other ways.[5][4]
  2. Who ran the studies. Several key papers come from researchers who disclose creatine-industry ties. One analysis’s lead author chairs the scientific advisory board of a creatine maker, which paid the publication charges, per the paper. A review was prepared with support from a supplement trade group.[7][8][9][10] That does not make the data wrong. The one independent injury pilot we found, with no external funding, pointed the same way but was too small to be conclusive.[21]
  3. Side effects were self-reported. Pooled trial data depend on how each trial recorded side effects, and many did not look for injuries.[7]
  4. Pressure is not injury. Compartment-pressure studies are small and short and did not track injuries.[13][15]
  5. “All” is a strong word. Soft-tissue injuries happened among non-users too in every study cited here.[4][21]
  6. Some claims go the other way. An industry-linked position stand says creatine may help prevent or reduce injuries. That is not proven either.[10]

If you take creatine, on or off a GLP-1

Some people on Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound or Mounjaro take creatine to protect muscle. No trial has reported results in GLP-1 users yet. The only registered trial we found, NCT07625202, is still recruiting 40 people, with results expected after May 2027.[27] Our muscle-loss teardown covers what trials show for protein, resistance training and creatine.[28] Creatine can also make lean mass on a scan look higher, partly from water.[16][18] Talk with your prescriber, especially if you have kidney disease, are under 18, or take other supplements. FAQ

Does creatine cause soft-tissue injuries?
The studies that counted injuries did not find more in creatine users. In three college football seasons, creatine users’ share of strains, pulls and other injuries was lower than or in line with their share of the team. Those studies were open-label, and there is no large randomized injury trial.
Does creatine cause muscle cramps?
Most evidence says no. In football players, users had less cramping, and a meta-analysis found no harm to heat tolerance or fluid balance. In pooled trial data, cramping or pain was a bit more often reported in creatine arms, but per person the difference was small and not significant.
Is creatine just water weight?
Partly. Creatine raises total body water, which can inflate lean mass on scans. But in the studies here the water was not redistributed abnormally, and the ratio of water inside cells to muscle mass did not change in trained men.
Does pre-workout cause injuries?
We found no study linking pre-workout as a category to soft-tissue injuries. The bigger documented risks are stimulants, hidden amounts in proprietary blends and possible contamination with banned substances.
Is creatine safe for your kidneys?
In healthy people, pooled trials have not found kidney harm. Creatine can raise blood creatinine, a lab marker of kidney function, and there are rare case reports of kidney injury, including in a teenager after high-dose loading. If you have kidney disease or take other medicines, ask your clinician first.
Can I take creatine with Ozempic or Zepbound?
There are no trial results yet. One trial testing creatine with GLP-1 drugs is recruiting, with results expected after May 2027. Ask your prescriber, and tell them before any kidney tests.

Bottom line

Creatine is one of the most-studied supplements, and the data available do not show that it causes soft-tissue injuries or cramps. Injury rates in creatine-using athletes were lower than or in line with non-users, and pooled trials show no overall rise in side effects. The limits are real: the injury data are open-label and often industry-linked, a small cramping signal and higher muscle-damage markers with long-term use cannot be ruled out, and creatine has not been tested in GLP-1 users. In our read, the evidence does not support blaming “all soft tissue injuries” on creatine, though a larger independent injury trial would settle the question better than any post.
