Selank anxiety teardown: what the Russian trials show, and what they don't

Selank (Thr-Lys-Pro-Arg-Pro-Gly-Pro) is a synthetic heptapeptide. In Russia it is sold as 0.15% nasal drops, without a prescription, for anxiety and related conditions.[4] It is not an approved drug in the United States.[5][6] Claims that it is a “non-sedating anxiolytic” trace back to a few Russian studies. We read the most-cited one, Medvedev et al. 2014 (selank vs phenazepam), and its 2015 follow-up in the original Russian. We translated the key passages and rebuilt the published numbers.

The short version

  • Main study: 60 outpatients with anxiety-phobic or somatoform disorders were randomized to selank 2.7 mg/day or phenazepam 2 mg/day (a benzodiazepine) for 14 days, then followed for 7 days off drug.[1]
  • Efficacy: on drug, the benzodiazepine worked better. By day 14, anxiety scores fell 26.3% with selank vs 55% with phenazepam, and responders were 5 vs 7 of 30. One week after stopping, the order reversed: 17 vs 9 responders (p = 0.037).[1]
  • Side effects: this is the strongest signal. At day 14 sedation was 0% with selank vs 100% with phenazepam, and attention problems were 7% vs 97%.[1]
  • Design limits: the study was open-label (the authors call it “open naturalistic”). It had no placebo, the randomization method isn't described, and there were only 30 patients per group.[1]
  • Funding: both the 2014 and 2015 papers say they were supported by Peptogen, the company that holds the Russian registration for selank, plus a Russian state science grant.[1][2][4]
  • Replication: we found no trial outside Russia, and ClinicalTrials.gov lists no selank studies.[7]

What the 2014 study tested

The study ran at two Moscow hospitals used as teaching sites by the psychiatry department of the Peoples' Friendship University of Russia (RUDN).[1] It enrolled adults aged 18–55 with mild-to-moderate anxiety-phobic, anxiety-hypochondriac, or somatoform disorders (ICD-10 F40.2–9, F41.1–9, F45.0–2) and a CGI severity score of at least 4. Depression, psychosis, substance dependence, and any psychotropic use in the prior year were exclusions.[1]

«Цель открытого натуралистического проспективного фармакотерапевтического исследования - сравнение терапевтической эффективности и переносимости препаратов селанк и феназепам»

Our translation: “The aim of this open, naturalistic, prospective pharmacotherapy study is to compare the therapeutic efficacy and tolerability of selank and phenazepam”[1]

«Случайным образом больные были рандомизированы в две группы. Пациенты 1-й группы (30 человек) получали монотерапию селанком в дозе 2,7 мг/сут, больные 2-й группы (30) - феназепамом в дозе 2 мг/сут. Терапию проводили в течение 14 дней.»

Our translation: “Patients were randomly assigned to two groups. Group 1 (30 people) received selank monotherapy at 2.7 mg/day, group 2 (30) phenazepam at 2 mg/day. Treatment lasted 14 days.”[1]

Assessments were at baseline, day 7, day 14, and day 21 (one week after stopping). Measures were the anxiety and depression subscales of a hospital anxiety/depression scale (labeled “HDRS” in the paper), the Spielberger self-report questionnaire, CGI, SF-36 quality of life, the Stroop test, verbal fluency, and the UKU side-effect scale. A “responder” was defined as a reduction of at least 50% in the anxiety score by day 14.[1]

Group 1 had 17 men and 13 women, group 2 had 12 men and 18 women, and mean ages were 44 and 43.[1]

The numbers

«выраженность тревоги к 14-му дню терапии уменьшалась на 26,3% при приеме селанка и на 55% - феназепама»

Our translation: “by day 14 of treatment, anxiety severity fell by 26.3% on selank and by 55% on phenazepam”[1]

While patients were taking the drugs, phenazepam did better on anxiety: a larger score drop, more responders (7 vs 5), and almost three times as many CGI-I “marked improvement” ratings (14 vs 5).[1] The case for selank rests on day 21. The paper says selank's effect held and grew after it was stopped, while phenazepam's faded quickly, and reports 17 vs 9 responders (χ² = 4.34, p = 0.037).[1] That fits a benzodiazepine wearing off. It does not show selank beat phenazepam at relieving anxiety during treatment.

Quality of life: the SF-36 mental-health component improved by 43.3% vs 3.1% at day 14 and 53.3% vs 6.3% at day 21, favoring selank. The physical component didn't differ.[1]

Side effects and safety

The tolerability gap is large and consistent across Table 3 and the cognitive tests. Phenazepam slowed Stroop performance and reduced verbal fluency; selank did not.[1] The paper says selank's most prominent complaint was nasal dryness. Table 3 records it for 7% of patients at day 21 and 0% at day 14.[1] One oddity: Table 3 gives sexual dysfunction for selank at day 7 as “1%”. With 30 patients, the smallest possible non-zero value is 3.3%, so this figure is probably a typo.

The phenazepam arm also isn't a neutral benchmark. It is a potent benzodiazepine, and all-day sedation in 100% of patients suggests a dose that is heavy for outpatients. That makes any lighter-touch drug look good by comparison. The Russian prescribing information for selank lists possible allergic reactions and an unpleasant taste if drops reach the throat, and contraindicates it in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and under 18.[4] In the US, FDA's compounding page says selank acetate “may pose risk for immunogenicity” and that FDA “lacks important information regarding any safety issues raised by selank acetate administered to humans.”[5]

What it got right

The study used an active comparator that Russian clinicians actually prescribe, not a straw man. It reported counts, not just p-values. It followed patients off drug to look for rebound, and it measured cognition objectively instead of relying only on complaints. The safety contrast is big enough that bias alone is unlikely to explain all of it.[1]

Design: what it can't tell you

  • Open-label. Patients and raters knew who got nasal drops and who got pills. Anxiety scales and side-effect reports are subjective, so this is the biggest weakness.[1]
  • No placebo. Anxiety often improves on its own over 3 weeks. Without a placebo arm, the selank group's improvement can't be separated from time, attention, and expectation.
  • Randomization not described. The paper says “randomly” but gives no method, concealment, or registration. Sex balance differed between groups (17/13 vs 12/18).[1]
  • Small and short. 30 per group, 14 days of treatment, and 7 days of follow-up. Many endpoints were compared with no prespecified primary outcome.[1]
  • Scale labeling. The paper calls its main scale “HDRS” but describes it as the hospital anxiety and depression scale. Baseline anxiety scores of 19–20 fit that scale's 0–21 range better than the Hamilton scale, but the exact instrument is not unambiguous.[1]

The follow-up and earlier studies

2015 add-on study (full text read). The same group randomized 70 patients to phenazepam alone (30) or phenazepam plus selank (40) for 14 days. It was again “open naturalistic,” and 20 of the combination patients continued selank alone to day 21.[2] Day-14 responders were 23% vs 40% (p = 0.14, not significant). At day 21, 75% of those who kept taking selank were responders, vs 30% after stopping phenazepam (p = 0.0018).[2] The authors report that adding selank reduced phenazepam's side effects.[2]

Our translation: “The work was supported by CJSC Innovative Production Center Peptogen and Russian Foundation for Basic Research grant No. 11-08-01327.”[2]

2008 selank vs medazepam (abstract only). Zozulia et al. compared selank (30) with medazepam (32) in generalized anxiety disorder and neurasthenia. They report similar anxiolytic effects, with additional “antiasthenic and psychostimulant” effects for selank. PubMed indexes it as a randomized controlled trial.[3] We have not found an open full text on the publisher's site, so we haven't checked its tables.

Funding and conflicts

«Работа была поддержана ЗАО «Инновационный научно-производственный центр «Пептоген» и РФФИ грант №11-08-01327.»

Our translation: “The work was supported by CJSC Innovative Research and Production Center Peptogen and Russian Foundation for Basic Research grant No. 11-08-01327.”[1]

Peptogen holds the Russian registration for Selank.[4] N.F. Myasoedov is a co-author of the 2014, 2015, and 2008 papers.[1][2][3] In the 2015 paper one author group is affiliated with the Institute of Molecular Genetics, Russian Academy of Sciences, which the 2014 paper also credits for the receptor-binding work behind its proposed mechanism.[2][1] The 2014 paper has no separate conflict-of-interest statement beyond the funding note. Manufacturer funding with no blinding is the combination most likely to inflate results.

Replication outside Russia

Every human selank study we found on PubMed is from Russian groups, and most share authors. A ClinicalTrials.gov search for “selank” returns only unrelated studies matched by spelling similarity, with no selank trial registered.[7] There is no independent, blinded, placebo-controlled anxiety trial of selank in the sources we checked.

Regulatory status

  • United States: not FDA approved. Drugs@FDA returns no selank application,[6] and FDA's compounding page flags immunogenicity and safety-data gaps.[5]
  • Russia: Selank 0.15% nasal drops are registered to JSC “INPC Peptogen” and sold without a prescription. The registration number is ЛП-№(010951)-(РГ-RU), dated July 15, 2025, replacing ЛСР-003338/09. Listed indications include anxiety states, neurasthenia, and adjustment disorders.[4]

Bottom line: in one small, open-label, manufacturer-funded study, selank caused far fewer benzodiazepine-type side effects than phenazepam. It relieved anxiety less than phenazepam during treatment, and patients did better than phenazepam patients in the week after both drugs were stopped. Whether selank beats placebo has never been tested in a blinded trial.
