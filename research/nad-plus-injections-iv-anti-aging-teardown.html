Home / Research / NAD+ shots and drips teardown

Paper-trail teardown · trials, preprints, FDA files, 1961–1973 papers, trial registry and PubMed counts read · Sources checked October 17, 2026

Published October 17, 2026 · Updated October 17, 2026

Do NAD+ shots and IV drips slow aging? No placebo-controlled trial has tested that.

NAD+ IV drips and at-home NAD+ shots are sold as anti-aging. We found five tests that gave NAD+ or NADH by vein against a placebo, going back to 1994. None measured aging, energy or lifespan, and the largest used 10 mg a day, about a fiftieth of a typical clinic drip.[13][8][5][4][16][7] The drip’s own history starts with two short “preliminary reports” on addiction in 1961, and the next big NAD claim, a 1960s schizophrenia cure, fell apart in a double-blind test.[10][11][12] In one 2026 review of clinic records, every NAD+ drip client reported moderate to severe cramping, nausea and chest pressure.[7] FDA proposed in 2019 not to let pharmacies compound NAD. It never finished that rule, so under an interim policy they still can.[17][18][19]

Why this matters this week

On October 8, JAMA published a large, year-long test of an NAD+ booster in a disease of aging: 410 people with early Parkinson’s took oral nicotinamide riboside (NR, sold as Niagen) or a placebo for a year in Norway. The NR group did slightly worse than placebo.[1][2] Six days later, ChromaDex, the company that makes Niagen, posted a new study of daily NR shots, in a medRxiv preprint. It had no placebo group.[3] Meanwhile NAD+ is selling on X: Katie Price told followers on October 10 to message a seller for NAD+ “at cheap price,” calling it “amazing for anti aging,” and another account argued for drips because “NAD benefits taken orally are lost through the digestion process.”[33] By our count, English posts pairing NAD+ with health terms ran at about 45 to 73 a day from October 11 to 16. That’s steady chatter, not a spike.[33]

What NAD+ is, and what’s in the bag

NAD+ (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is a molecule every cell uses to turn food into energy and to run enzymes that repair DNA. It was once called DPN, and in Chinese hospitals it’s sold as “coenzyme I.”[10][32] Three different things get sold under the NAD+ label. An NAD+ drip or shot is the whole molecule, compounded by a pharmacy. NR and NMN are smaller building blocks that cells turn into NAD+, sold mostly as pills.[26][22] Nearly all the controlled human evidence is for the pills, not the drips.[26][32] ChromaDex, which owns the Niagen ingredient, funded the three U.S. injection trials discussed below.[5][3][4] No NAD+ or NR injection is approved by FDA.[34]

Where the drip came from: addiction in 1961, schizophrenia in 1966

When ChromaDex’s researchers wrote up their October preprint, they traced IV NAD+ to a single source: “first documented by O’Hollaren in 1961.”[3] That source is two short papers by Paul O’Hollaren in the Western Journal of Surgery, Obstetrics and Gynecology, three and four pages long, each subtitled “a preliminary report.” One was on problem drinkers, the other on drug addiction.[10] PubMed lists no abstract and no later follow-up by him.[10]

Then came a bigger claim. In March 1966 the psychiatrist Abram Hoffer told a symposium that NAD had treated 13 of 17 schizophrenia patients in 3 to 5 days, and his paper with Humphry Osmond said oral NAD made 11 of 18 chronic patients well within days.[11][12] It got wide press. The American College of Neuropsychopharmacology quickly ran a double-blind test: 20 patients in matched pairs got NAD or placebo for two months. Both groups improved slightly, and the placebo group improved more on interviews.[11][12] Other groups tried it, including by vein in four patients, and found nothing.[12] In 1973 an American Psychiatric Association task force wrote that “evidence that NAD is effective has been refuted clinically.”[12] It also argued that NAD probably doesn’t enter cells intact.[12]

A small double-blind test of NADH (the reduced form) by vein in Parkinson’s patients in 1994 also found no difference from saline.[13] The modern drip came back through Louisiana. Springfield Wellness Center says it “introduced NAD+ treatment to the United States” and was founded in 2001. Its BR+ NAD addiction program ran 500 to 1,500 mg a day, 5 to 10 hours a day, for about 10 days. The evidence posted by NAD Research, Inc., whose director is also the clinic’s medical director, is a poster: a look back at 60 patients’ own craving ratings, with no comparison group.[14][15][8] The clinic’s home page now says NAD+ can “slow the progression of Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s” and “promote anti-aging.”[14]

The “longevity genes” study is a one-page abstract

One human study still cited for IV NAD+ and aging has a sweeping title: IV NAD+ “increased expression of longevity genes safely in elderly humans.” It’s a 2018 one-page abstract by Nady Braidy, in the Journal of Cellular Neuroscience and Oxidative Stress. It describes 8 healthy people aged 70 to 80 given 1,000 mg by vein for 6 days, with no control group described. It says the treatment is “a safe and efficient way to slow down age-related decline in NAD+.”[9] We found no full paper of that study in PubMed, but a September 2026 review of IV NAD+ still cites it.[9][31]

The one full paper from the same group came a year later. In 2019, Ross Grant, Braidy and colleagues gave 8 men a 750 mg drip over 6 hours, against 3 on saline, at the Louisiana clinic. They picked the dose because it “reflects a common dosing regimen in clinics.” For the first two hours, blood NAD+ didn’t rise at all. The infused NAD+ was “rapidly and completely removed from the plasma,” apparently broken down. By hour six it was up 398%. The study measured blood chemistry, not health. It was funded in part by NAD+ Research Inc., whose director, the clinic’s medical director, was a co-author.[8]

What’s actually been tested against a placebo

We read every human NAD+ injection study we could find that had a placebo group. There are five. Two are company-funded preprints in healthy adults: a single 500 mg drip in 2024, and three days of 100 mg in 2026 with five people per drip group.[5][4] Both measured comfort and blood levels. The 2019 drip study measured blood levels in 11 men.[8] The 1994 Parkinson’s test had 9 patients.[13] The only one with a real health outcome is a Chinese trial in 180 heart-failure patients: 10 mg a day by vein for a week improved heart pumping (ejection fraction) at one month, 45.4% vs 42.4%. It didn’t significantly cut heart events.[16] That’s a promising result in a sick population, at a dose about one-fiftieth of the 500 mg drip clinics sell as a “loading dose.” It says nothing about aging.[16][7]

What we counted

We searched ClinicalTrials.gov for every trial giving NAD+, NADH, NR or NMN and found 162. Only 9 give NAD+ or NR by vein or injection, and we read each one. None has posted results.[32] Three are ChromaDex studies in healthy or tired adults. The other six are Chinese hospital trials of “coenzyme I for injection,” mostly in diseases like liver cancer and hearing loss.[32] Exactly one registered trial puts an NAD+ drip against a placebo on an aging measure: a 60-person study of blood-vessel aging in Shanghai, listed as not yet recruiting.[32] No registered trial tests a 500–1,000 mg wellness drip against placebo on aging, energy or lifespan.[32]

On PubMed, 490 human records mention NAD+ or NADH alongside injection or infusion. Just 13 are tagged as randomized trials. When we read those, only two had actually randomized people to injected NAD+ or NADH: the 2026 heart trial and the 1994 Parkinson’s test. The rest were about other drugs.[32][16][13]

This month’s two new studies

The Parkinson’s trial (oral NR). NOPARK was large, double-blind and independently funded by Norwegian research money. ChromaDex supplied the pills and placebo.[1] After a year, symptoms had worsened 2.45 points on NR and improved 0.27 on placebo, a gap of 2.72 points favoring placebo. That gap is below the 5.2 points the authors count as clinically meaningful worsening, so the fair reading is “no benefit, maybe slight harm,” not “NR is toxic.” Serious adverse events were actually less common on NR (8.3% vs 14.1%).[1] It tested pills in Parkinson’s, not drips in healthy people. The study’s leader put the lesson plainly: “NR clearly increased NAD metabolism, but this did not translate into clinical improvement.” His university calls it the largest trial yet of an NAD-boosting treatment in a neurological disease.[2]

The NR shot study. ChromaDex’s October preprint gave 39 tired adults 50 or 100 mg of NR by shot into muscle or under the skin for three days, at a Florida clinic that also sells NAD+ infusions.[3] Fatigue scores fell in some groups, but there was no placebo. To enroll, people had to report high fatigue, so scores were likely to drift down on retest anyway (regression to the mean), and some participants knew each other and “may have discussed” the study.[3] The authors flag both problems themselves.[3] The headline biology also didn’t land: “three days of injections did not result in statistically significant increases in whole blood NAD+ with each administration.” Peak times ranged from 15 minutes to 24 hours.[3] One more thing we noticed: the preprint cites the Restore clinic study with a DOI that points to an unrelated kidney-injury paper.[35][7]

Drips vs pills: what blood levels do and don’t prove

The pitch for drips is that pills get digested. That’s partly right for NAD+ itself. FDA wrote in 2019 that NADH is “likely to be degraded before absorption after oral dosing.”[17] But the drip doesn’t cleanly deliver NAD+ either. In the 2019 drip study, infused NAD+ vanished from blood for two hours.[8] In ChromaDex’s 2024 test, a 500 mg NR drip raised blood NAD+ more than a 500 mg NAD+ drip at three hours.[5] The precursor pills do raise blood NAD+ in controlled trials, and that’s their strongest result.[27][26]

A higher blood level isn’t a health benefit. That was the lesson of the Parkinson’s trial, where NR raised NAD metabolism and patients still did no better.[2] In aged men, NR pills raised NAD+-related molecules in muscle but didn’t change how the muscle’s mitochondria made energy.[30] In obese men, 2,000 mg a day for 12 weeks didn’t improve insulin sensitivity.[29] A 2025 review in Nature Metabolism summed up human trials of NAD+ precursors as showing “limited efficacy.”[26]

Is it legal?

No NAD+ or NR injection is FDA-approved.[34] Drips and shots come from compounding pharmacies. FDA reviewed NAD for pharmacy compounding years ago. Its advisers voted against it on May 8, 2017, and FDA’s 2019 proposal found NAD “degrades substantially” at room temperature and that there were “no published studies” for the use it was nominated for. It proposed keeping NAD off the list.[17] FDA never finalized that rule. NAD is still listed as “under evaluation” (Category 1) as of May 2026. While it sits there, FDA’s interim policy says it “does not intend to take regulatory action” against pharmacies that compound it, as long as they meet other conditions.[17][18][19][20] Injectable NR goes through a different door. Niagen says its IV and shot products are made by 503B outsourcing facilities for clinics, by prescription, and NR chloride sits on FDA’s 503B “under evaluation” list.[21][22]

NMN pills had their own fight. In 2022 FDA said NMN couldn’t be sold as a supplement because it had first been studied as a drug. In September 2025, answering an industry petition, FDA reversed that and concluded NMN “is not excluded” from the supplement definition.[23]

Safety

The clearest harm is the drip itself. In a 2026 review of clinic records at Restore Hyper Wellness in Austin, all six clients who got 500 mg NAD+ drips on four days in a row reported “moderate to severe abdominal cramping, diarrhea, nausea, vomiting, increased heart rates, pain in throat, congestion, and chest pressure.” Symptoms stopped when the drip did, and the drips took about 97 minutes because clients slowed them down.[7] In ChromaDex’s 2024 trial, NAD+ drips raised white blood cell counts, which the authors read as an inflammatory response.[5] In the April 2026 safety test, NAD+ drips drew 14 unpleasant symptom reports against 2 for saline drips, including chest tightness and shortness of breath. Five minutes after dosing, one person in the NAD+ drip group and one in the NAD+ shot group described “paralysis/can’t move.” These were free-text reports from a handful of people.[4] We found no published long-term safety data on repeated NAD+ or NR injections in people, and the published NR toxicity tests so far are single-dose animal studies.[6][3]

What holds up

NAD+ biology is real and important, and the precursor pills have a fair record. They reliably raise blood NAD+, they’re generally well tolerated in trials, and a few small trials found signals worth testing: blood pressure and artery stiffness in older adults, and muscle insulin sensitivity in women with prediabetes taking NMN.[27][28][29] The Chinese heart-failure trial is a real randomized result for injected NAD+ in a sick population.[16] And in fairness to the drip clinics, short-term drips at these doses haven’t produced serious harm in the small studies so far. The problems found were discomfort, not injury.[5][7][8]

Checking the claims going around this week

“By age 50 your NAD+ levels have dropped by roughly 50%.”[33] A widely cited source for the decline claim is a 2012 study of skin removed during surgery, by the same Australian group that later ran the drip studies.[24] In a 2022 blood study of 1,518 adults, women showed no significant difference across age groups. In men, only those 60 and older differed from the youngest group, by about 2 μmol/L out of 34.5, roughly 6%.[25] A September 2026 review says large human datasets show whole-blood NAD+ doesn’t decline with healthy aging as such.[31][26]

“NAD taken orally is lost through digestion,” so drips are better.[33] Partly true for whole NAD+, but drip NAD+ is also broken down fast, and NR by vein raised blood NAD+ more than NAD+ by vein at three hours in the company’s own test.[17][8][5]

Side effects are “mostly about speed and dose.”[33] Speed matters. Clients in the Restore records slowed their own drips to cope.[7] But at 100 mg, within the range X sellers call a starting dose, NAD+ drips still drew 14 symptom reports to saline’s 2, including chest tightness.[4][33]

“The injections make me feel like I’m 30 again.”[33] That’s exactly what an unblinded test can’t separate from expectation. In the 1967 double-blind test of the last NAD craze, the placebo patients improved more on interviews.[12][11]

Bottom line

Do NAD+ shots and drips slow aging? Nobody has run the test that could show it. The placebo-controlled trials measured comfort, blood levels, or symptoms in sick patients, at doses as low as a fiftieth of what clinics sell.[4][5][16][32] The company studies are small and posted as preprints, and the newest one had no placebo.[3][4][5] The drip has been sold on preliminary reports since 1961, and the controlled tests have mostly come back flat, from schizophrenia in 1967 to Parkinson’s this month.[10][12][13][1] If you want the version with the most evidence, it’s a pill, and even those trials show “limited efficacy.”[26]
