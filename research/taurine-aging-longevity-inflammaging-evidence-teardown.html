Taurine for aging and longevity: does it work, and is it safe? A 2023 study said falling taurine drives aging and that taurine lengthened mouse lives; a Yale-led Cell study published October 23, 2026 found taurine rises with age in people and mice, and instead casts it as a brake on age-related inflammation. No human trial has tested lifespan, human results are modest and mixed, and short-term safety looks good.

Taurine is an amino acid the body makes and also gets from animal foods such as meat and fish; it is added to energy drinks and infant formula, and sold as a supplement.[28][27] Does it slow aging? In 2023, a Columbia-led team reported that blood taurine falls with age and that taurine lengthened average mouse life by 10 to 12%.[3][4] Since then, a 2025 NIA-led study and a Yale-led Cell study published October 23, 2026 found taurine rises or stays flat with age in people, and the Yale team says taurine “does not drive aging,” while reporting that raising it further made old mice healthier by calming inflammation.[5][1][2] No human trial has tested lifespan; one 6-month trial of biological age finished in November 2025 with no results posted.[29][31] Human trials show modest drops in blood pressure, blood sugar and lipids, no clear benefit for thinking, and misses in hip fracture and cirrhosis patients.[19][22][17][18] Is it safe? Short trials report few side effects, and in Japan it has been a prescription drug for heart failure since 1987.[20][14][11]

Why this matters now

On October 23, 2026, Cell published a study led by Yale School of Medicine with Pennington Biomedical among the affiliations; the authors declare no competing interests, and Yale says the work was supported by the NIH, among other funders.[1][2] It reports that “aging increases circulating taurine concentrations in humans and mice,” the opposite of the 2023 finding, and that immune cells called macrophages release taurine as they switch on the NLRP3 inflammasome, a driver of the chronic inflammation of aging (“inflammaging”).[1][2] Raising taurine further in old mice improved healthspan and reduced deaths from sepsis and inflammasome-driven inflammation.[1] In people, sustained calorie restriction, in a study lasting up to a year, raised taurine in fat tissue alongside lower inflammation.[1][2] In the same month, a Nutrition Reviews meta-analysis of 34 trials reported modest improvements in blood pressure and blood sugar, and an October 6 genetic study linked taurine levels to several diseases, pending validation.[19][26]

What taurine is

Taurine is a sulfur-containing amino acid that is not used to build proteins. It was first described in ox bile in 1827.[6] In cats, low taurine was linked to blindness in 1975 and to a reversible heart failure in 1987.[7][8] People make it from other sulfur-containing amino acids and get it from animal foods, with cow’s milk a noted exception; infant formulas add it.[28] In the US and Europe it is not licensed as a medicine, while Japan approves it as a drug for three uses.[14][10]

The aging fight: does taurine fall or rise with age?

  • 2023: “deficiency as a driver of aging.” The Columbia-led Science paper reported lower taurine with age in mice, monkeys and people, and longer life and better health in mice given taurine; it called for human trials.[3] Columbia’s release put the average mouse lifespan gain at 12% in females and 10% in males.[4] The paper discloses that Columbia filed provisional patent applications naming one of its authors as an inventor.[3]
  • 2025: the rebuttal. A team including NIA scientists found taurine increased or stayed flat with age in three human groups, monkeys and mice, and concluded that falling taurine is “not a universal feature of aging.”[5]
  • October 2026: a different role. The Yale-led Cell study found taurine rises with age and casts it as part of the body’s response to inflammation: macrophages released up to 80% of their taurine as an inflammasome formed, and adding taurine dampened that response.[1][2]

Our analysis: both camps report that extra taurine helped old mice; they disagree on whether a shortage of taurine is part of aging. Neither shows that it slows aging in people.[3][1][5]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • Lifespan in people. We found no human trial that measured lifespan.[31][30] The closest is a Technical University of Munich trial of 90 adults aged 55 to 75, testing whether 6 months of taurine changes blood and DNA-methylation measures of “biological age”; it is listed as completed, with no results posted.[29]
  • Heart and metabolism. In 120 people with prehypertension, systolic blood pressure fell 7.2 mmHg on taurine vs 2.6 on placebo over 12 weeks.[15] Meta-analyses report modest drops in blood pressure, blood sugar and lipids; the newest pooled 34 trials and found systolic pressure 4.38 mmHg lower and HbA1c 0.21 points lower.[19][21][20]
  • Heart failure. Small trials and a 2024 meta-analysis report better symptoms and heart measures, but a 2022 review found only one high-quality study among 11 and said “A formal clinical trial is needed.”[9][20][14]
  • Older and sick patients. In older hip fracture patients, taurine lowered oxidative-stress markers but did not reduce complications or deaths in the first year.[17] In 220 people with advanced cirrhosis, it did not improve fatigue.[18] In 24 women aged 55 to 70, it kept an antioxidant enzyme from falling, a lab marker rather than a health outcome.[16]
  • Brain and exercise. Two reviews found no clear effect of taurine alone on thinking and memory; the benefits seen with energy-drink style mixes could not be pinned on taurine.[22][23] A single serving before exercise gave a small performance gain in pooled small trials, with low to very low certainty.[24]

The case for taurine, in fairness

Taurine is not a fringe compound: Japan has approved it as a drug for heart failure since 1987, and in 2019 for MELAS, where 6 of 10 patients in an open-label trial had no stroke-like episodes over a year.[11][10][13] Cats proved that too little taurine can cause blindness and heart failure.[7][8] Its blood-pressure and metabolic effects are modest but repeat across meta-analyses.[19][21][20] Two independent mouse lines of work, from teams that disagree about the biology, both found extra taurine helped old mice.[3][1] Our analysis: that is a reason for the human trials already under way, not proof that it slows aging.[29]

Safety

  • Short-term tolerability. Meta-analyses report no significant side effects compared with control groups; in the cirrhosis trial, 11.8% of patients on taurine had side effects, all mild; the MELAS trial reported no serious side effects tied to taurine.[20][18][13][14]
  • Energy drinks. EFSA judged taurine in energy drinks, at the exposures it assessed, not a safety concern, and noted it assessed the ingredients, not energy drinks themselves.[27]
  • An open question on blood cancers. A 2025 Nature study in mice and human cells found leukemia cells depend on taurine supplied by surrounding bone-marrow cells, and blocking its uptake slowed the disease in mice.[25] Our analysis: this does not show that taurine supplements cause or worsen leukemia in people, which has not been tested, but it is a reason for caution and for more study. An October 2026 genetic study also flagged possible links between taurine levels and cancers and blood pressure that need confirming.[25][26]
  • What is unknown. Most trials were small and lasted weeks to a year; there are no long-term data in healthy older people taking it to slow aging.[14][29][31]

Purity

Japan’s approved product is a pharmaceutical-grade powder reviewed by its drug regulator.[10] Our analysis: supplements and energy drinks are regulated as foods, and the trial results do not automatically apply to products that mix taurine with caffeine or other ingredients.[23][27] We do not name or rate brands or sellers.

Legal status

Taurine is not licensed as a medicine in the US or Europe; it is sold in foods, energy drinks and supplements.[14][27] In Japan, Taisho’s taurine powder is a prescription drug for congestive heart failure, a liver-function indication and, since 2019, MELAS, with orphan-drug status for that use.[10][11][12]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. “Extends lifespan” is a mouse result. No human trial has measured lifespan.[3][31]
  2. The deficiency story is disputed. Two later studies, including the October 2026 Cell paper, found taurine rises or stays flat with age in people.[5][1]
  3. Mechanism is not benefit. The Cell study is mostly mice and cells; its human data come from blood samples and a calorie-restriction study, not from giving people taurine.[1][2]
  4. Look for the main goal. The largest trials in older or sick patients missed theirs.[17][18]
  5. Energy drinks are not taurine trials. Benefits from taurine-caffeine mixes cannot be credited to taurine alone.[23]

Frequently asked questions

Does taurine slow aging or extend lifespan?
In mice, a 2023 study reported 10 to 12% longer average life. No human trial has measured lifespan, and a 6-month biological-age trial has no results posted yet.[4][29][31]
Does taurine decline with age?
It is disputed. A 2023 study said yes; a 2025 NIA-led study and the October 2026 Yale-led Cell study found it rises or stays flat in people.[3][5][1]
What did the October 2026 Cell study find?
Taurine rose with age in people and mice, immune cells released it as they switched on inflammation, and extra taurine made old mice healthier and less inflamed.[1][2]
Is taurine safe?
Trials of weeks to a year report few, mostly mild side effects. A 2025 mouse study tied taurine to leukemia growth, which has not been tested in people taking supplements.[20][18][25]
Is taurine FDA approved?
No. It is not licensed as a medicine in the US or Europe; it is sold in foods and supplements. Japan approves it as a drug for heart failure, a liver indication and MELAS.[14][10]
Does taurine lower blood pressure?
Modestly. A 34-trial meta-analysis found systolic pressure about 4 mmHg lower, and a 120-person trial found a larger drop.[19][15]
Is the taurine in energy drinks good for you?
EFSA found it is not a safety concern at the levels it assessed, but reviews could not credit taurine alone for energy-drink effects on thinking.[27][23]

Bottom line

Taurine made old mice healthier in studies from teams that disagree on why, and the newest, from Yale, says taurine rises with age rather than falls.[3][1][5] In people, it is an approved drug in Japan with modest blood-pressure and metabolic effects, but no trial has shown it slows aging, and the largest trials in older or sick patients missed their goals.[10][19][17][18] It looks safe in the short term; results from the first biological-age trial are the next thing to watch.[20][29]
