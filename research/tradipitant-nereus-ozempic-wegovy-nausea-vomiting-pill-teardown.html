Evidence teardown · Ozempic · Wegovy · Zepbound · Side effects · Updated October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

Is there a pill to stop Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound nausea and vomiting? Not an approved one yet. Tradipitant (NEREUS), a Vanda Pharmaceuticals pill approved only for motion sickness, cut vomiting from 58.5% to 43.0% after a semaglutide shot in a Phase 3 trial Vanda reported on October 27, 2026. The effect was smaller than in Phase 2, the trial skipped the usual dose step-up, and the results are not yet peer-reviewed.

Nausea and vomiting are the side effects people on Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound and Mounjaro complain about most, and on October 27, 2026, Vanda Pharmaceuticals reported a Phase 3 trial of a pill meant to prevent them. The reader’s question: is there finally a pill that stops GLP-1 nausea and vomiting, and should you ask for it? Our plain answer: tradipitant (brand name NEREUS) did reduce vomiting in two company-run trials, but it is approved only for motion sickness, the GLP-1 trials used a harsher-than-normal start, and the newest result is smaller than the first. For now, the proven tools are still the slow dose step-up in the Wegovy and Zepbound labels and a conversation with your prescriber.

Why this matters now

On October 27, 2026, Vanda announced that Study 3601, a Phase 3 trial, met its main goal: 43.0% of people given tradipitant vomited after a semaglutide injection, vs 58.5% on placebo.[1] Vanda says it will discuss the results with FDA and plans to apply to add GLP-1 vomiting to the NEREUS label; its own statements point to FDA feedback “during 2027.”[1] Meanwhile, GI side effects are common on these drugs: in the Wegovy label, 44% of adults had nausea and 24% vomited, vs 16% and 6% on placebo.[11]

What tradipitant is

  • An NK-1 blocker. Tradipitant blocks the NK-1 receptor, where the brain chemical substance P helps trigger vomiting.[10]
  • Approved, but for something else. FDA approved NEREUS in December 2025 to prevent vomiting caused by motion in adults.[6][5] In Vanda’s two boat-trip Phase 3 trials, vomiting fell from 44.3% to 18.3–19.5% and from 37.7% to 10.4–18.3%.[7][8]
  • Not approved for GLP-1 side effects. GLP-1 vomiting is still an investigational use. A drug profile notes Vanda is also developing it for gastroparesis.[9]

What the two GLP-1 trials show

  • The setup. In both trials, adults with overweight or obesity who had not used GLP-1s got one semaglutide injection at a dose that, Vanda notes, normally takes weeks of step-up to reach.[2][1] They took tradipitant or placebo pills for about 2 weeks around the shot.[3][2] We are not listing tradipitant doses.
  • Phase 2 (116 people): vomiting 29.3% vs 58.6%, a 50% relative reduction. Vomiting plus what Vanda calls significant nausea: 22.4% vs 48.3%.[2]
  • Phase 3 (306 people analyzed): vomiting 43.0% vs 58.5%, a 27% relative reduction. Vomiting plus nausea rated 3 or higher on Vanda’s worst-nausea score: 33.1% vs 50.6%.[1]
  • Heavy vomiting. Fewer people on tradipitant vomited on several days (6.3% vs 15.9%) or more than 10 times (2.8% vs 14.6%). Vanda says these were post hoc analyses, run after the fact, and presents them as “supportive.”[1]
  • Timing. “Almost all vomiting episodes in either group occurred by Day 2.”[1]

Jabbit’s analysis: put in absolute terms, about 15 fewer people in every 100 vomited in Phase 3 (58.5% minus 43.0%), compared with about 29 in every 100 in Phase 2.[2][1] That is a real effect in a company-reported trial, but even on tradipitant, more than 4 in 10 people still vomited.

Safety: what is known

  • In the GLP-1 trials: Vanda says tradipitant was “generally well tolerated” and that “No new safety signals were observed.” It has not published side-effect rates for these trials.[1][2]
  • From the NEREUS label: drowsiness (6% and 12% across the two doses studied) and fatigue (6% and 8%), with a warning that it may impair driving. The most common side effects are drowsiness, headache and fatigue.[5]
  • Interactions and who should avoid it: strong CYP3A4 inhibitors can raise tradipitant levels. The label says to avoid it with any degree of liver impairment.[5]
  • Long use is untested. The label says safety and effectiveness beyond 90 doses has not been established.[5] The GLP-1 trials covered about 2 weeks.[3]
  • Vomiting itself has risks. The Wegovy and Zepbound labels link most reported kidney injury cases to GI side effects that led to dehydration.[11][12] Preventing heavy vomiting could matter for that reason, though no trial has tested whether tradipitant changes it.

The case for tradipitant

There is a real problem to solve. In a US survey of people who stopped a GLP-1, 33.3% cited side effects, second only to cost.[15] In one health system’s records, 14.6% stopped because they could not tolerate side effects, though cost and insurance were far more common (47.6%).[14] Tradipitant has now beaten placebo in two randomized GLP-1 trials and two motion-sickness Phase 3 trials, and it is already FDA-approved, so its basic safety profile is on a label.[2][1][7][5] Vanda argues that because most vomiting happens in the first two days, a short course around starting or stepping up a dose is the right window.[1]

What a careful reader should notice

  1. The test was harsher than real life. Both trials skipped the gradual step-up, which Vanda calls “a rigorous test.”[1] With the usual step-up, vomiting rates in the label trials were lower: 24% on Wegovy and 8% to 13% on Zepbound, vs 58% on placebo in Vanda’s trial.[11][12][1] Vanda says a pooled analysis supports applying the effect to real-world dosing; that analysis has not been published.[1]
  2. The effect shrank. The relative reduction fell from 50% in Phase 2 to 27% in Phase 3.[2][1] Our read: larger, later trials often show smaller effects than early ones.
  3. Not peer-reviewed yet. Both results come from press releases. Neither trial has results posted on ClinicalTrials.gov, and on October 27, 2026 the Phase 3 record still listed the study as recruiting, with 280 estimated participants; Vanda reports 306 analyzed.[3][4][1] Registry records often lag.
  4. Vomiting is not nausea. The main outcome was vomiting. Nausea was measured only together with vomiting in the published endpoints.[1][3]
  5. Most GI side effects already fade. In the STEP trials, 98.1% of semaglutide GI events were mild to moderate, most were short-lived, and they clustered around dose increases; 4.3% stopped treatment for them.[13]
  6. All trials are Vanda’s. That does not make them wrong, but independent replication would carry more weight.[3][4]

What to do about GLP-1 nausea today

The labels’ main tool is the slow step-up: Wegovy and Zepbound both tell prescribers to follow the dose escalation “to reduce the risk of gastrointestinal adverse reactions.”[11][12] Wegovy’s label adds that if a dose is not tolerated during escalation, prescribers can consider delaying the next step by 4 weeks, and Zepbound’s label says most nausea and vomiting “occurred during dose escalation and decreased over time.”[11][12] If you are vomiting repeatedly or cannot keep fluids down, contact your prescriber; dehydration is a labeled risk.[11] Logging dose changes alongside symptoms can make that conversation easier.

FAQ

Is there a pill to stop Ozempic or Wegovy nausea and vomiting?
Not one approved for that use. Tradipitant (NEREUS), approved for motion sickness, reduced vomiting after a semaglutide shot in two Vanda trials, and Vanda plans to seek FDA approval for GLP-1 vomiting. Until then, the labels rely on slow dose increases, and prescribers manage symptoms case by case.
What is NEREUS (tradipitant)?
NEREUS is an oral NK-1 receptor blocker from Vanda Pharmaceuticals. FDA approved it in December 2025 to prevent vomiting caused by motion in adults. It is being studied for vomiting caused by GLP-1 drugs and for gastroparesis.
How well did tradipitant work in the Phase 3 GLP-1 trial?
Vanda reported that 43.0% of people on tradipitant vomited after one semaglutide injection, vs 58.5% on placebo, a 27% relative reduction. In the earlier Phase 2 trial it was 29.3% vs 58.6%. Both results are company-reported and not yet peer-reviewed.
Can my doctor prescribe NEREUS for Ozempic or Zepbound side effects now?
NEREUS is approved only for motion-induced vomiting, so any other use would be off-label and a decision for your prescriber. The GLP-1 trials used a different, 2-week schedule than the label, and long-term safety has not been established.
What are the side effects of tradipitant?
The NEREUS label lists drowsiness, headache and fatigue as the most common, warns that it may impair driving, and says to avoid it with liver impairment. Strong CYP3A4 inhibitors can raise its levels. Vanda reported no new safety signals in the GLP-1 trials.
How common is vomiting on Wegovy and Zepbound?
In the Wegovy label, 24% of adults vomited vs 6% on placebo; nausea was 44% vs 16%. In the Zepbound label, vomiting was 8% to 13% by dose vs 2% on placebo. Most of it happens while the dose is being increased.

Bottom line

Tradipitant is the first drug to beat placebo in a Phase 3 trial aimed at GLP-1 vomiting, and that is genuine news. But the trial used an artificially harsh start, the benefit was about half the size seen in Phase 2, the data are a press release, and the GLP-1 use is not approved. If it reaches the label, it may help the minority who vomit badly in the first days of a new dose. Today, slow titration and your prescriber remain the evidence-based route.
