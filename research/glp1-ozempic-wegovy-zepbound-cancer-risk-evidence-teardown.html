Home / Research / GLP-1 drugs and cancer risk teardown

Study teardown · Semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) and other GLP-1 drugs · Cancer risk · Meta-analyses of randomized trials, health-record studies, FDA labels and trial registries · Sources checked October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

Do Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound raise or lower cancer risk? Pooled placebo-controlled trials show little or no effect on most cancers either way, while health-record studies link GLP-1 drugs to fewer obesity-related cancers, which cannot prove prevention. Thyroid cancer is still unsettled: the labels carry a boxed warning based on rodent studies, and human studies conflict. New in October 2026: a tirzepatide endometrial cancer study, a cancer review, and a breast cancer clinic study that found much less weight loss than in the big trials.

The short answer: so far, randomized trials do not show that GLP-1 drugs cause or prevent cancer. A 2026 pool of 48 placebo-controlled trials, with 94,245 people, found they “probably have little or no effect” on thyroid, pancreatic, breast or kidney cancer, while noting the trials “were not designed to evaluate cancer outcomes and had short follow-up.”[4] Health-record studies report fewer obesity-related cancers on these drugs, but those are associations, and the results change with the comparison group.[11][12] Thyroid cancer is the open question, and the Wegovy and Zepbound labels carry a boxed warning about it.[15][16]

Why this matters now

Three papers published in October 2026 revisit GLP-1 drugs and cancer. On October 13, a study of US health records reported that women with overweight or obesity who started tirzepatide had about 28% fewer new endometrial cancer diagnoses in the short term than matched women who started lifestyle programs; the authors say their “observational findings do not establish a preventive effect.”[3] A review in Obesity Reviews, indexed October 22, concludes that GLP-1 drugs “do not increase overall cancer risk and may reduce the risk of certain obesity-related cancers,” while flagging thyroid safety concerns.[2] And a breast cancer study indexed October 26 found that 269 patients on hormone-blocking (endocrine) therapy lost an average of 4.5% of their weight in a year on GLP-1 drugs, well below the 10% to 20% the authors cite from postmenopausal groups in the big trials.[1]

Our analysis: interest in GLP-1 drugs as cancer prevention is running ahead of the evidence. The strongest data, randomized trials, point to little or no effect so far, mainly because those trials were short and not built to count cancers.[4]

The evidence, rung by rung

What randomized trials show

  • Most cancers: little or no effect. In 48 placebo-controlled trials, odds ratios for thyroid, pancreatic, breast and kidney cancer all crossed 1, rated moderate certainty. In absolute terms, the thyroid estimate runs from 1 fewer to 9 more cases per 10,000 people treated. Results held for semaglutide and tirzepatide separately.[4]
  • All cancers combined: no difference. A 50-trial meta-analysis found no overall change (odds ratio 1.05).[5] A 25-trial pool of weight-loss medicines found no link with obesity-related cancer (risk ratio 1.03).[6]
  • Possible bright spots. Uterine cancer was less common on GLP-1 drugs in obesity trials (odds ratio 0.24), though with a wide range and few cases.[5] A subgroup of combination drugs, including tirzepatide, showed fewer obesity-related cancers (risk ratio 0.43, range 0.19 to 0.97).[6] Subgroup results like these are less reliable than the main results.
  • Colorectal: likely more looking, not more cancer. The 50-trial analysis saw slightly more colorectal cancer only in shorter trials, and its authors suggest more diagnostic procedures in the drug groups may explain it.[5]

Thyroid cancer: the open question

Both labels say the drug caused thyroid C-cell tumors in rodents and that “it is unknown” whether it does in humans. They rule out use in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) or MEN 2, and list warning symptoms: a lump in the neck, trouble swallowing, shortness of breath or lasting hoarseness.[15][16]

  • Trials: pooled estimates sit around 1.4 to 1.6 times the odds. One 2024 analysis was just significant, with a fragility index of 1, meaning one fewer case would erase significance; it estimated 1 extra case per 1,349 people treated for 5 years.[8] A 2026 analysis of 15 trials counted 28 vs 15 cases, found no significant link and rated certainty very low, but said it cannot rule out “a clinically relevant increase.”[7]
  • Health records: a French study linked 1 to 3 years of use with more thyroid cancer (hazard ratio 1.58) and more MTC (1.78).[9] A Scandinavian cohort found no increase (0.93), with MTC too rare to call (1.19, range 0.37 to 3.86).[10]
  • Screening: the labels say routine calcitonin blood tests or thyroid ultrasound are “of uncertain value” for catching MTC early.[15][16] See our thyroid monitoring guide.

Our analysis: people on these drugs may see doctors and get scans more often, which could turn up small thyroid cancers that would otherwise go unnoticed. None of the studies above settles this, so the label precautions stand.

What health records show

  • People with obesity and without diabetes. In 161,798 matched US patients, GLP-1 users had 41% fewer obesity-associated cancers over a median of 2 years than people given diet or exercise counseling. The lower rate held in every subgroup except Black patients, and the authors say “prospective trials are needed to confirm causality.”[11]
  • The comparison group matters. In 1.65 million people with type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 drugs were linked to lower risk of 10 of 13 obesity-associated cancers compared with insulin. Compared with metformin, there was no lower risk for any cancer, and kidney cancer was more common (hazard ratio 1.54).[12]
  • Semaglutide vs tirzepatide. Against DPP-4 inhibitor diabetes pills, semaglutide was linked to 12% fewer obesity-associated cancers; tirzepatide’s estimate was similar but not significant.[13]
  • Against surgery. One study found fewer colorectal and pancreatic cancers with full-strength GLP-1 treatment than after bariatric surgery, but no significant difference when all strengths were counted.[14]
  • Endometrial cancer, October 2026. Tirzepatide users had a hazard ratio of 0.717 for new endometrial cancer diagnoses vs matched women who started lifestyle programs, which the authors call a short-term association.[3]

Breast cancer: the newest study

Women on endocrine therapy after breast cancer often struggle with weight. In the October 2026 single-center study, only 21.3% of 269 patients on a GLP-1 drug lost 10% or more of their weight in a year. Semaglutide and tirzepatide users lost more (5.8% and 4.7%) than dulaglutide users, and patients with progesterone receptor-negative tumors lost more than those with receptor-positive tumors. Most of the patients (70.6%) had type 2 diabetes, and the authors call for prospective trials.[1]

On cancer risk itself, the 48-trial pool found little or no effect on breast cancer, and a large diabetes record study found no lower risk of postmenopausal breast cancer.[4][12] Whether these drugs change breast cancer outcomes is being tested only in small trials so far (see below). Decisions during cancer treatment belong with the oncology team.

Healthy aging: why this matters for longevity

Researchers count 13 cancers as obesity-associated, meaning excess body fat raises their risk, which is why researchers are asking whether drug-driven weight loss lowers that risk.[12][11] Cancer usually takes years to develop, though, while most trials and record studies here followed people for about two to three years.[4][11][13] The review authors also flag an open question for long-term health: what happens to any benefit if weight comes back after stopping treatment.[2] Our analysis: for healthy aging, the reassuring part is that trials so far show no broad cancer increase; the hoped-for prevention benefit is unproven.

The case for caution

The trials pooled were built to test weight or blood sugar, not cancer, and were short.[4] Health-record studies compare people who were prescribed GLP-1 drugs with people who were not, and the result changes with the comparison: favorable against insulin or counseling, neutral against metformin.[12][11] Several of the record studies draw on the same US research network, TriNetX.[11][13][14][3] Thyroid findings conflict across countries and designs.[9][10] Some meta-analysis authors disclose fees from drug makers, outside the submitted work.[5][6]

Trials coming

  • CLARA (University Hospitals Leuven, Belgium): randomized phase 2 trial of tirzepatide with letrozole in 168 people with breast cancer, measuring a tumor growth marker (Ki67); primary completion listed as March 2028.[17]
  • Breast cancer, US: a Baylor phase 2 trial in 48 people tracks circulating tumor DNA and distant recurrence through 2029; Rutgers and Weill Cornell trials of 40 and 30 people test feasibility and weight loss.[18]
  • Endometrial: a US National Cancer Institute trial pairs tirzepatide with a progestin IUD in 55 people, and a UNC randomized trial in 40 people measures tumor-cell growth; both list 2029 completion dates.[19]
  • None of the registry records we checked is placebo-controlled or large enough to answer whether these drugs prevent cancer.[17][18][19]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. “Little or no effect” in trials and “fewer cancers” in records can both be true: the trials were short and small for cancer counts, and records carry confounding.[4][11]
  2. A record study’s comparison group can flip its message.[12]
  3. The thyroid trial signal is fragile: a handful of cases decides significance.[8][7]
  4. Subgroup wins, such as uterine cancer or combination drugs, need confirmation.[5][6]
  5. The October endometrial study calls its own result an association, not prevention.[3]
  6. The breast cancer study was retrospective, at one center, mostly in people with diabetes.[1]

Frequently asked questions

Does Ozempic or Wegovy cause cancer?
A 2026 analysis of 48 placebo-controlled trials found semaglutide and similar drugs probably have little or no effect on thyroid, pancreatic, breast or kidney cancer. The trials were short and not designed for cancer.[4]
Does Zepbound or Mounjaro cause thyroid cancer?
Tirzepatide caused thyroid C-cell tumors in rats, and the label says it is unknown whether it does in humans. Human studies conflict, and the label rules out use with a personal or family history of MTC or MEN 2.[16][7][10][9]
Can GLP-1 drugs prevent cancer?
Unproven. Health-record studies link them to fewer obesity-related cancers, but those are associations, and placebo-controlled trials so far show little or no effect.[11][3][4]
Does tirzepatide lower endometrial cancer risk?
An October 2026 record study found about 28% fewer new diagnoses on tirzepatide vs lifestyle programs, and a trial pool found fewer uterine cancers in obesity trials. Neither proves prevention.[3][5]
Do GLP-1 drugs work for weight loss during breast cancer treatment?
In one 2026 study of 269 women on endocrine therapy, average one-year weight loss was 4.5%, well below big-trial results. Prospective trials are under way; treatment decisions belong with the oncology team.[1][17][18]
Should I get calcitonin tests or a thyroid ultrasound on these drugs?
The Wegovy and Zepbound labels say routine calcitonin or thyroid ultrasound is of uncertain value for catching MTC early. Report a neck lump, trouble swallowing or lasting hoarseness to a doctor.[15][16]

Bottom line

Pooled randomized trials so far show no broad increase or decrease in cancer on Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound or similar drugs.[4][5] Health-record studies, including the October 2026 tirzepatide endometrial study, link the drugs to fewer obesity-related cancers, but cannot prove prevention.[11][3] Thyroid cancer remains unsettled and the boxed warning stands.[15][16][7] Our analysis: for longevity, the cancer case for these drugs is a hypothesis to test, not a reason to start one.
