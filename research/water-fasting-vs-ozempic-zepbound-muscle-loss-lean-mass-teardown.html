Home › Research › Water fasting vs GLP-1s: muscle and lean mass

Research review · Water fasting · Ozempic · Wegovy · Zepbound · Muscle and lean mass · Updated October 27, 2026

Published October 27, 2026 · Updated October 27, 2026

Does a long water fast burn more muscle than Ozempic or Zepbound? On the scale, it seems to, though the evidence is thin. In the only two water-fast studies a 2024 review found that measured body composition, lean mass made up roughly 57% to 67% of the weight lost over 8 to 10 days, vs about 29% to 34% in the drug arms of recent semaglutide and tirzepatide trials. But much of a fast’s “lean” loss is water and glycogen, and the studies are tiny.

A viral post this week said a long water fast cost one person about 1 pound of lean mass for every 3 pounds lost, and many people on Ozempic, Wegovy, Zepbound or Mounjaro worry about the same trade-off. The reader’s question: is a multi-day water fast a “muscle-sparing” shortcut, and how does it compare with GLP-1 drugs? Our plain answer: the few fasting studies that measured body composition found a larger lean share of weight loss than recent GLP-1 trials, but a big part of fasting’s lean loss is water and stored glycogen, the studies are small and mostly uncontrolled, and the risks of fasting for days are real. Neither approach is “all fat.”

Why this matters now

On October 26, 2026, a public post on X described a well-known YouTuber’s 14-day water-only fast with DEXA scans before and after: about 13 lb of fat and about 7 lb of lean mass lost, by the post’s account. The post reported 988,584 impressions by the evening of October 27.[1] English posts on X mentioning a “water fast” ran at roughly 40 to 90 a day over the past week, by Jabbit’s count.[2] We have not seen the scans and cannot verify the numbers, so we treat them as one person’s reported result and compare them with published studies.

What “lean mass” means on a scan

  • Lean is not the same as muscle. Lean or fat-free mass on DXA and similar scans includes “not only muscle but also organs, bone, fluids, and water in fat tissue,” a 2024 review notes.[21]
  • Glycogen is heavy with water. The body stores glycogen in hydrated form, with “three to four parts water,” and its loss can distort body-composition estimates during dieting.[12] Early in a fast, glycogen and its water are among the first things lost.[5]
  • The method matters. The studies below used DXA, bioimpedance, air displacement or calculations from urine nitrogen, and these can disagree during rapid fluid shifts.[3][5][8]

What the fasting studies show

  • Water-only, 8 to 10 days: a 2024 review found only two studies that measured fat and lean mass. One (DXA) reported a 7 kg weight loss after a 10-day water fast, 3 kg fat and 4 kg lean; the other (bioimpedance) reported 6 kg lost after 8 days, 2 kg fat and 4 kg lean. The review sums it up as about two-thirds lean and one-third fat.[3] Jabbit’s arithmetic: that is about 57% and 67% lean, from rounded figures.
  • 49 studies pooled: a 2026 meta-analysis found standardized drops in fat-free mass (g = −0.51) and total body water (g = −0.68) that were larger than the drop in fat mass (g = −0.25), and concluded that fasting weight loss “reflects complex changes in body composition rather than isolated fat loss.”[4]
  • A 7-day water-only fast: 12 volunteers lost an average of 5.7 kg, and blood-protein changes across the body showed up only after about 3 days without calories.[6]
  • A breakdown of the lean loss: in 16 healthy men on a 10-day fast with about 200–250 kcal a day of juice and broth (not water-only), lean soft tissue was about 60% of the 5.9 kg lost. The authors estimate that 44% of that lean loss was extracellular water (including stool lost with enemas), 14% glycogen and its water, and 42% metabolically active tissue such as muscle, liver and gut, about 25% of total weight lost.[5]
  • A randomized 5-day fast (about 250 kcal a day, not water-only): in the LEANER trial of 38 healthy adults, weight loss was “predominantly driven by loss of fat mass,” and the lean mass lost during fasting “completely reverted” by the 12-week follow-up.[8]
  • The viral case: 7 of 20 pounds is 35% lean, by Jabbit’s arithmetic from the post’s figures.[1] That is lower than the two water-fast studies above, closer to what GLP-1 trials report, and one person’s scan pair.

How GLP-1 drugs compare

In the drug-only arms of four recent trials of semaglutide or tirzepatide with DXA scans, lean mass was 28.9% to 34% of the weight lost, over 16 to 48 weeks.[22] A widely cited rule of thumb from diet studies is that about a quarter of weight lost is fat-free mass.[11] Reported lean shares vary widely across weight-loss studies, from about 15% or less to 40%–60%.[21]

Jabbit’s analysis: on these numbers, a long water fast looks lean-heavier per pound lost than a GLP-1, mainly in the first days, when water and glycogen go first. But the comparison is weak: different people, methods and time frames, with no head-to-head trial. A GLP-1 also produces far more total weight loss over months than a 1- to 2-week fast, so lean mass lost in kilograms can be larger on a drug even if the share is smaller.

Safety: what is known

  • Common side effects. In 768 supervised fasting visits at one US facility (median 7 days), the most common reported events were fatigue (48.2%), insomnia (33.5%), nausea (32.2%), headache (30.1%), high blood pressure (29.2%, mostly in people who arrived with it) and near-fainting (28.3%).[9]
  • Serious events happen. In the same review, one 73-year-old man had dehydration on day 3, and one 70-year-old man had grade 4 hyponatremia (low blood sodium) on day 9; no deaths were reported.[9] A 2024 review of trials found no serious adverse events or deaths, but noted metabolic acidosis, headaches, insomnia and hunger.[3]
  • Refeeding syndrome. UK NICE criteria list “little or no nutritional intake for >5 days” as a risk factor, and a BMJ review notes that low phosphate is its hallmark and that NICE advises restarting at no more than half of energy needs in that situation.[18]
  • Electrolytes and the heart. Older reports of long therapeutic starvation describe “a breakdown in electrolyte homoeostasis,” low magnesium in a 382-day supervised fast, and a 1969 Lancet paper titled “Gross fragmentation of cardiac myofibrils after therapeutic starvation for obesity.”[16][15][17] Those were far longer fasts, but they are why fasting clinics monitor patients.
  • Inflammation signal. A 2025 study of water-only fasts averaging 9.8 days found a rise in C-reactive protein, and its authors wrote that this “raises concerns regarding potential cardiometabolic effects that could persist after refeeding.”[7]
  • Slower metabolism. In the 10-day study, basal metabolic rate fell 12%.[5] The 2024 review notes that lean loss “can translate into lower resting metabolic rate after fasting,” a possible risk for regain.[3]
  • With a GLP-1. We found one published case of a water fast combined with tirzepatide, done as a 27-day inpatient stay with structured refeeding, and no trials.[19][20] Prolonged fasting on a GLP-1 has not been tested in trials, and fasting while on insulin, sulfonylureas or blood pressure drugs needs a prescriber’s input.

The case for prolonged fasting

It produces fast weight loss: 2% to 10% over 5 to 20 days, per the 2024 review, with no serious events in the trials it covered.[3] Physiology studies going back to the 1960s show the body shifting to ketones and cutting protein breakdown as a fast continues, and the 10-day study saw protein loss early that eased “as ketogenesis increases.”[13][14][5] Strength held in that study and rose 33% in weight-bearing muscles.[5] In the randomized LEANER trial, a 5-day, roughly 250 kcal-a-day fast mostly removed fat, and lean losses recovered by 12 weeks.[8] An observational study of 1,422 people on a supervised 4- to 21-day low-calorie fast reported adverse effects in under 1%.[10]

What a careful reader should notice

  1. Tiny evidence base. The two-thirds lean figure rests on two water-only studies with rounded numbers, and the 10-day breakdown on 16 men without a control group.[3][5]
  2. Water-only vs “fasting.” Several studies, including the 10-day breakdown and LEANER, allowed about 200–250 kcal a day, which may not match a strict water fast.[5][8]
  3. Lean is mostly water early on. In the 10-day study, more than half of the lean loss was water and glycogen, which tends to return with food.[5][12] The muscle and organ share was about a quarter of weight lost, close to the diet rule of thumb.[5][11]
  4. But not all of it came back. At 3 months in that study, weight and lean tissue were still lower than before, while fat mass was not.[5] In LEANER, the opposite: fat stayed down and lean recovered.[8]
  5. Benefits fade. The 2024 review says metabolic benefits were gone 3 to 4 months after fasting, even when weight loss was kept, and that the effects of refeeding on body composition remain unknown.[3]
  6. Who ran the studies. Several safety datasets come from fasting facilities or authors tied to them, as their own funding and competing-interest statements disclose.[9][7][5] That does not make the data wrong, but independent replication is thin.
  7. A likely typo. The 768-visit review prints “2 (0.002%)” serious events; by Jabbit’s arithmetic, 2 of 768 is about 0.26%.[9]
  8. One viral scan pair is an anecdote. DXA after a fast is affected by hydration and glycogen, and we cannot check the post’s figures.[1][12]

If you are thinking about a long fast, on or off a GLP-1

Talk with your prescriber first, especially if you take a GLP-1, insulin, a sulfonylurea or blood pressure medicine, or are older, pregnant or have heart, kidney or eating-disorder history. The randomized LEANER trial, for example, excluded people on prescription drugs or with heart, lung, liver or kidney disease needing treatment.[8] For protecting muscle during weight loss on Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound, our muscle-loss teardown covers what trials show for protein and resistance training. FAQ

Does water fasting burn muscle?
Some, along with a lot of water and glycogen. In the two water-only studies a 2024 review found, about two-thirds of the weight lost was lean mass. In a detailed 10-day study, muscle and organ tissue was about a quarter of the weight lost; the rest of the lean loss was water and glycogen.
Do you lose more muscle with a water fast than on Ozempic or Zepbound?
Per pound lost, the small fasting studies show a higher lean share (about 57% to 67%) than the drug arms of recent semaglutide and tirzepatide trials (about 29% to 34%). No trial has compared them directly, and much of the fasting loss is water.
Does extended fasting “spare muscle”?
Partly. Classic studies show protein breakdown falls as the body shifts to ketones over a long fast, and strength held in one 10-day study. But lean mass still fell in every fasting study cited here, and a 2026 meta-analysis found fat-free mass fell more than fat mass.
Does the lean mass come back after you eat again?
Much of the water and glycogen does. In the randomized LEANER trial, lean mass lost in a 5-day, roughly 250 kcal-a-day fast fully recovered by 12 weeks. In a 10-day study, lean tissue was still lower at 3 months, while fat had returned to baseline.
Is it safe to water fast while taking a GLP-1?
It has not been studied in trials. We found one published case, done as a supervised inpatient stay. Insulin, some diabetes pills and blood pressure drugs may need adjusting when you stop eating, so talk with your prescriber first.
What are the risks of a long water fast?
Common ones are fatigue, insomnia, nausea, headache and near-fainting. Rarer but serious ones include dehydration, low blood sodium and refeeding syndrome, which UK guidance flags after more than 5 days of little or no food.

Bottom line

A long water fast is not a muscle-free way to lose weight. In the little data there is, lean mass made up a larger share of fasting weight loss than in recent GLP-1 trials, though much of it is water and glycogen, and one randomized 5-day low-calorie fasting trial found lean mass recovered. The benefits seen at the end of a fast tend to fade, the studies are small and often run by fasting clinics, and the safety risks grow with length. If muscle is your worry, our read of the evidence points to what GLP-1 muscle trials focus on, such as protein, resistance training and medical follow-up, rather than longer fasts.
