Home / Research / Glutathione IV and injections

Evidence teardown · FDA and CDC outbreak notices, recall notices, human trials, history and two viral claims · Sources checked October 23–24, 2026

Published October 23, 2026 · Updated October 25, 2026

Do glutathione IV drips and shots work, and are they safe? There is no FDA-approved glutathione product in the US, the few IV trials we found showed no clear benefit, and contaminated batches made in 2026 have been linked to at least 30 illnesses.

Glutathione drips and shots are sold as skin brighteners, detox and “anti-aging” boosts. In 2026, FDA said at least 30 patients had reactions after IV glutathione made by compounding pharmacies from one lot of an ingredient labeled dietary supplement grade, and CDC counted more than 25 people sick in at least four states, three or more of them hospitalized.[1][2] Four Texas pharmacies have recalled glutathione products.[4][5][6][7] The trials behind the drips are thin: the IV skin-lightening evidence rests on one small study that reviewers call flawed, and a small IV trial in Parkinson’s disease found no significant benefit.[23][27] Oral glutathione is better studied, with mixed results.[17][18][19] We also checked two claims from a widely shared October 20 X post. One is unsupported, the other only partly right.[10]

Update, October 25, 2026: We added glutathione’s FDA compounding status. FDA lists glutathione in category 1, “under evaluation,” on its list of substances nominated for pharmacy compounding, and under a January 2025 policy FDA says it does not intend to take action against pharmacies compounding with category 1 substances if certain sourcing and other conditions are all met. That is not an approval.[41][42] Details are below, and our FDA peptide status tracker shows glutathione next to 20 peptides and NAD+.

Why this matters this week

Glutathione is having a moment on X. Original posts mentioning it rose from 86–110 a day on October 17–19 to 164 on October 21 and 141 on October 22, by our count, after a widely shared October 20 post claiming that ejaculation “depletes glutathione” passed 240,782 views.[10] The same week, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. said the administration wants “a regulated marketplace” for compounded peptides, and the policy is still under White House review.[38][39] Glutathione doesn’t depend on that policy for a pharmacy route: FDA already lists it in category 1 of substances under evaluation for compounding.[41] We don’t know whether the new policy would change anything for glutathione, because its text isn’t public. But this year’s outbreak is a live example of what can go wrong inside the pharmacy route that policy relies on. (More on that below.)

The 2026 outbreak, in the official record

CDC says Texas health officials told it in July 2026 about patients who got sick after IV glutathione at an outpatient wellness clinic. By its September 15 update, CDC had “reports of over 25 people becoming sick in at least four states: Texas, Washington, Florida, and New York,” after IV, under-the-skin or intramuscular doses. At least three were hospitalized, all after IV doses, and one needed intensive care for shock and abnormal clotting. CDC says the illnesses are likely linked to endotoxins, toxic fragments of bacteria, and that every linked product was made by a compounding pharmacy from glutathione intended for dietary supplements.[1]

FDA’s August 27 alert counts “at least 30 patients” with reactions after IV glutathione. It says the pharmacies involved “all … obtained their glutathione (Lot #229536) from Medisca Inc.,” and that the ingredient “was labeled as dietary supplement grade, which is not an appropriate ingredient to make injectable drugs.” FDA also reports that “Medisca informed the agency on August 20, 2026, that it sent its U.S. customers a warning not to use its dietary supplement grade glutathione in the compounding of injectable drugs.”[2] The two agencies count differently (FDA: IV reactions; CDC: illnesses across routes), and as of October 23 we found no newer count from either.[1][2]

Two details stand out in our reading. First, none of the four recall notices names an ingredient supplier, and FDA’s alert names no pharmacies, so the public record doesn’t say which recalls involve the lot FDA identified.[2][4][5][6][7] Second, Greenwich Rx’s recall letter says “each of the recalled lots was tested for bacterial endotoxins prior to release, and all results met specifications,” and that it recalled them because the glutathione was “compounded using an ingredient that was labeled as dietary supplement grade.”[8] That is the situation CDC’s advice to pharmacies addresses: “Do not rely on testing alone.”[1] (The FDA-posted notice lists 10 lots; the pharmacy’s letter on the California board’s site lists 12.[7][8]) We found no glutathione recall posted after October 1.[40]

This has happened before. In 2015, seven people in Sydney got probable endotoxin poisoning from compounded glutathione infusions, five of them being treated for Lyme disease.[11] In 2019, FDA warned about L-glutathione repackaged by Letco Medical after seven clinic patients reacted within minutes of IV doses; FDA’s lab found endotoxin “as high as five times the appropriate limit,” and the manufacturer told FDA its ingredient was sold in the US only for supplements.[3] In our analysis, the 2026 cluster is the third on record we found in which the same failure, supplement-grade glutathione ending up in injections, has made people sick.[1][3][11]

What glutathione is: a 138-year paper trail

Glutathione is a small molecule of three amino acids (glutamate, cysteine and glycine) that nearly every cell makes for itself.[12] Its story starts in 1888, when the French scientist de Rey-Pailhade described something in yeast extracts that turned sulfur into hydrogen sulfide. He called it “philothion,” Greek for “love of sulfur.”[12] In 1921 Frederick Gowland Hopkins at Cambridge isolated it, thought it was a two-amino-acid peptide of glutamate and cysteine, and named it glutathione.[12][13] In 1929 he and, separately, Edward Kendall’s group at the Mayo Clinic showed it has three amino acids, including glycine.[14] Hopkins won a Nobel Prize that year, though for vitamins, not glutathione.[16] The first lab synthesis is often credited to Vincent du Vigneaud in 1936, but C. R. Harington and T. H. Mead published “Synthesis of glutathione” in the Biochemical Journal in July 1935.[12][15]

Why the history matters: glutathione has been a mainstream research molecule for a century, and nobody disputes that the body depends on it. The open question is whether putting more of it into your bloodstream from outside does anything useful.

What the human trials show

By mouth. A 1992 study gave 7 volunteers a single 3 g dose and found plasma glutathione did not rise significantly; the authors called its systemic availability “negligible.”[17] A 4-week randomized trial in 40 adults in 2011 found no change in oxidative stress markers.[18] A larger, longer 2015 trial in 54 adults found the opposite for body stores: after 6 months on the higher dose, glutathione was 30–35% higher in blood cells and plasma, and immune natural-killer cell activity had more than doubled at 3 months. Levels fell back to baseline a month after stopping.[19] In our reading, oral glutathione can raise body stores with months of use, but the trials haven’t shown that this improves health outcomes.

Skin lightening. A 2019 systematic review found 4 small studies, none of IV glutathione, and called the evidence “still inconclusive.”[20] One 4-week oral trial in 60 medical students found lighter skin at 2 of 6 sites measured, and its authors wrote that “long-term safety has not been established.”[21] A 12-week Thai trial of 250 mg a day by mouth found the melanin index only “tended to be lower” than with placebo.[22] For IV use, a 2018 review by dermatologists found the evidence “limited to a single study with a dubious study design and apparently flawed analysis”: 25 patients per group, 9 of 25 treated patients dropped out, and the results “did not favor IV glutathione.”[23][24] The Philippine FDA warned in 2019 that “to date there are no published clinical trials that have evaluated the use of injectable glutathione for skin lightening,” listing possible harms including liver, kidney and nerve toxicity and the severe skin reaction Stevens-Johnson syndrome.[25] The US FDA has said it has not approved any injectable product for skin lightening.[26]

Parkinson’s disease. In a 2009 randomized pilot, 21 patients got IV glutathione or placebo three times a week for 4 weeks. Parkinson’s rating-scale scores did not differ significantly during treatment (P = .32) or after (P = .54). The drug was well tolerated, and the authors suggested a larger study to test a possible mild effect.[27]

Precursors and aging. Another approach is to give the building blocks instead. In a 2023 trial, 24 older adults were randomized to glycine plus N-acetylcysteine (NAC), sold together as “GlyNAC,” or placebo for 16 weeks. The authors report improvements in glutathione deficiency, oxidative stress and several aging markers.[28] It is one small trial from one group, and it tested oral precursors, not glutathione infusions. For longevity, it is the more interesting lead, and it needs replication.

Fact-check: two claims from the viral post

Claim 1: “Ejaculation depletes glutathione.” Rating: unsupported. Semen does contain glutathione; studies measure it in both seminal fluid and sperm, and levels tend to be lower in infertile men.[33][34][35] But the amounts are tiny. Using deliberately generous inputs (5 mL of semen at the top quartile of a 2026 study, plus 500 million sperm, both well above WHO’s lower reference limits), we estimate about 64.5 micrograms of glutathione per ejaculate.[33][34][36][37] Blood alone makes roughly 1.15 g a day, based on a 2000 tracer study and an assumed 5 L of blood, so one ejaculate is about 1/18,000 of a day’s blood production, before counting the liver and other tissues.[29][37] Measurements of seminal glutathione vary widely by method, and one study found levels lower still.[35] We found no human study measuring blood or tissue glutathione against how often people ejaculate.[37] That is our analysis, not a measured result, and a study could change it.

Claim 2: Glutathione is the “master antioxidant” that vitamin C, vitamin E and alpha-lipoic acid “depend on” to be recycled. Rating: partly supported. Glutathione does help regenerate vitamin C from its oxidized form.[30] Vitamin E is regenerated mainly by vitamin C, so glutathione’s role there is indirect.[31] For alpha-lipoic acid, the direction is partly reversed: lipoic acid is reduced by enzymes that use NADH and NADPH, and the reduced form can in turn help restore glutathione.[32] “Master antioxidant” is a popular nickname, not a scientific term, and the claim that every other antioxidant depends on glutathione goes beyond what these studies show.[30][31][32]

Is a pharmacy route enough?

On October 20, Kennedy said the goal is “a regulated marketplace, so that what they’re getting is what they claim to be, that is safe, that the factories are inspected,” Reuters reported.[38] The interim compounding policy for peptides, RIN 0910-ZD94, was still listed as pending at the White House on October 23, and its text is not public.[39] Our policy explainer covers what is known.

Glutathione already has a pharmacy route. FDA lists it in category 1, “under evaluation,” of the substances nominated for the 503A bulks list, the list of ingredients pharmacies may compound with (May 14, 2026 version).[41] Under FDA’s January 2025 interim policy, until it decides in a final rule, FDA says it “does not intend to take action” against a state-licensed pharmacy or doctor compounding with a category 1 substance, but only “if all of the following circumstances are present,” including that “the original manufacturer and all subsequent manufacturers” of the ingredient are registered with FDA and that it comes with a valid certificate of analysis.[42] The same guidance says FDA does not consider USP monographs for dietary supplements to be applicable monographs for drug compounding.[42] Category 1 is not an approval or a finding that glutathione is safe or works; FDA describes such substances as ones that “may be eligible” for the list.[42] Our FDA peptide status tracker shows how that compares with 20 peptides and NAD+.

In our analysis, glutathione shows that a pharmacy route alone doesn’t guarantee quality. Every product tied to this outbreak was made by a compounding pharmacy, CDC says, and the problem was the starting ingredient.[1] In one recall, the pharmacy says its release tests passed.[8] FDA’s and CDC’s fixes are about sourcing, ingredient grade and full certificates of analysis, not only testing the final vial.[1][2] In our reading, the outbreak shows why the sourcing conditions in FDA’s policy matter; the public notices we found don’t say whether any particular pharmacy met them.[2][4][5][6][7][42] Any new marketplace for peptides would face the same supply-chain question.

Before an injection: CDC’s questions

We don’t give doses or recommend for or against glutathione. If you are considering an injection or drip, CDC suggests asking your healthcare provider these five questions, quoted from its September 15 guidance:[1]

CDC: before getting injectable glutathione, ask your healthcare provider
#Question
1Was the product made with ingredients intended for injection?
2Are there other treatment options that don’t involve an injection?
3Who made (compounded) the injectable glutathione? Is the pharmacy or facility licensed or registered, and are there any current recalls or safety warnings for it or this product?
4What steps were taken to make sure it is sterile and does not contain unsafe levels of endotoxins?
5What symptoms should I watch for after the injection, and what should I do if they occur?

CDC also says to contact your provider promptly if you develop fever, chills, severe body aches, nausea, vomiting, dizziness or injection-site pain or swelling after an injection, to seek emergency care for severe symptoms such as difficulty breathing, fainting or signs of shock, and to report reactions to FDA MedWatch.[1]

What holds up

Glutathione is a real, essential molecule with a century of research behind it.[12][13][14] Oral glutathione has GRAS (“generally recognized as safe”) status in the US, according to a dermatology review, and a 6-month randomized trial found it raised body stores.[19][23] IV glutathione was well tolerated in the small Parkinson’s trial, and some countries approve injectable glutathione for specific medical uses, such as an add-on to cisplatin chemotherapy.[23][25][27] The precursor approach has a promising, small trial in older adults.[28] What doesn’t hold up yet: good evidence that IV or injected glutathione lightens skin, slows aging or treats Parkinson’s, and there is no FDA-approved glutathione product in the US.[1][23][25][27]

Frequently asked questions

Is IV glutathione FDA-approved?
No. CDC says “there are no FDA-approved glutathione products in the United States”; injectable versions are compounded.[1]
Can pharmacies compound glutathione?
FDA lists glutathione in category 1, “under evaluation,” for 503A pharmacy compounding. Under its January 2025 interim policy, FDA says it does not intend to take action if all its conditions are met, including an FDA-registered manufacturer and a valid certificate of analysis. That is not an FDA approval.[41][42]
What made people sick in the 2026 outbreak?
CDC and FDA say the linked injections were compounded from glutathione labeled dietary supplement grade, and the illnesses are consistent with endotoxin exposure.[1][2]
Does oral glutathione get absorbed?
A single large dose did not raise blood levels in a 1992 study, but a 6-month randomized trial in 2015 found body stores rose 30–35% on the higher dose.[17][19]
Does glutathione lighten skin?
Small oral trials found modest effects, a 2019 review called the evidence inconclusive, and reviewers describe the IV evidence as a single flawed study.[20][21][23]
Does ejaculation deplete glutathione?
We found no human study showing that. By our estimate, one ejaculate contains about 65 micrograms, a tiny fraction of what the blood makes each day.[29][33][37]

Bottom line

Glutathione is essential and well studied, and by mouth it is generally recognized as safe in the US, with mixed evidence of benefit.[17][18][19][23] Glutathione drips and shots have little trial support for skin, aging or Parkinson’s, and in 2026 contaminated batches made from supplement-grade powder sent people to the hospital.[1][2][23][27] If you choose one anyway, CDC’s five questions are the place to start.[1]
