Home / Research / Who is lobbying on the peptide policy

Policy teardown · White House meeting log, the group’s own board and partner pages, FDA roster and warning letters · Sources checked October 19–21, 2026

Published October 19, 2026 · Updated October 21, 2026

Who is meeting the White House about BPC-157, TB-500 and the other peptides on Wednesday? A 2025 group whose boards include the heads of big compounding pharmacies.

FDA’s policy on letting pharmacies make BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and epitalon has been at the White House budget office since October 14, and its text still isn’t public.[2] When we first checked the office’s meeting log on October 19, it listed one outside meeting about it: Wednesday, October 21, at 3 PM ET, requested by the American Academy of Peptide Medicine (AAPM) and its chairman, Lee Rosebush.[1][3] By October 20 it also listed a second, requested by the telehealth company Hims & Hers Health for Friday, October 23, at 2 PM ET.[25] By the morning of October 21 it listed a third, requested by the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding, a pharmacy trade group, for Tuesday, October 27.[27][28] AAPM calls itself a nonprofit academic society. It was founded in 2025. Its own pages list the founder and CEO of Empower Pharmacy, the CEO of Strive Pharmacy and the chairman of Thesis Pharmacy on its industry board, and name Noom and Ivím Health, two telehealth sellers of compounded weight-loss shots, as partners.[6][7][19] Its chairman also chairs the trade group that sued FDA in 2024 to keep compounded Zepbound copies legal.[10] None of that makes their case wrong. It does tell you who is in the room.

Why this matters this week

Semafor reported early Monday, October 19, that AAPM “is slotted to meet with the Office of Management and Budget on Wednesday” about the pending policy.[3] Within hours, a Hims & Hers stock account on X was posting it as breaking news.[4] The meeting is real and on the public record.[1] Most coverage stops at the name of the group. Here is what the group’s own pages, and FDA’s, say about who it is.

Update, Tuesday, October 20: Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. was asked about the pending policy on Fox News’ “The Will Cain Show.” Reuters quoted him: “Americans are going to be buying these anyway. Our objective was to make sure that there is a regulated marketplace, so that what they’re getting is what they claim to be, that is safe, that the factories are inspected.”[26] That is a quality and sourcing argument, the same one AAPM makes below.[21][22] It doesn’t say which peptides, and it isn’t evidence that they work. When we rechecked at about 10 PM ET on October 20, the policy was still under review with no text public, and the meeting log still showed only the two meetings.[1][2][25]

Update, Wednesday, October 21: The meeting log now lists a third meeting, requested by Scott Brunner of the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding for Tuesday, October 27, at 2 PM ET.[27] The alliance describes itself as “the industry trade association and the voice for pharmacy compounding,” representing more than 600 compounding businesses.[28] Brunner told Semafor on October 19 that “we don’t yet know what the policy says, which peptides it covers, or what conditions FDA may attach.”[3] When we rechecked at about 8 AM ET on October 21, the policy was still under review and its text was still not public.[2]

The short answer

The group asking for the meeting is led by a pharmacy-industry lawyer and a physician whose company distributes peptides. Its boards include the leaders of pharmacies and telehealth companies that could make or sell these peptides if FDA allows it.[6][7][8] Its argument is a real one: people already inject “research use only” vials from overseas, and a licensed US pharmacy would be a cleaner source.[21] What it can’t point to is human evidence that the peptides work. FDA’s own scientists recommended against all seven, and PeptideBase’s webcast transcription (FDA minutes not posted) shows every yes vote coming from the 8 members appointed this year.[12][13][14]

What Wednesday’s meeting is, and isn’t

When a rule or policy is under White House review, outside groups can ask the Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs (OIRA) for a meeting. Under the 1993 executive order that governs these reviews, FDA must be invited, written materials go to FDA, and OIRA has to disclose the meeting publicly.[5] That is why the log shows the date, the group and the person who asked.[1] A meeting doesn’t decide anything. As of Monday morning, October 19, AAPM’s was the only meeting on file for this policy. By Tuesday, October 20, the log also showed the Hims & Hers Health meeting.[1][25] By Wednesday morning, October 21, it showed a third, from the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding.[27] As of that check, no patient-safety group or medical society had asked for one.[27]

Who the American Academy of Peptide Medicine is

Its leaders. AAPM says it was founded in 2025 and lists two leaders.[6] Lee Rosebush, a pharmacist and lawyer, is chairman. He also chairs the Outsourcing Facilities Association, a trade group for large compounding facilities.[6] In October 2024 that association sued FDA for taking tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound) off the shortage list, the step that ended the legal basis for mass-producing compounded copies. The court sent the decision back to FDA, FDA reached the same conclusion, and a federal judge later ruled for FDA and Lilly.[10] Reporters have described Rosebush as an attorney who has represented compounding pharmacies and peptide makers suing FDA over the 2023 restrictions.[13][21] The founder, Dr. Pradeep Albert, lists himself on his own site as CEO of Vesalius Longevity Labs, “peptide distribution & longevity services.”[8]

Its industry board and partners. AAPM lists four industry advisers: Shaun Noorian, founder and CEO of Empower Pharmacy, which AAPM calls “a leading producer of compounded peptide therapies”; Nate Hill, CEO of Strive Pharmacy; Jay Bhaumik, chairman of Thesis Pharmacy and BMK Pharma; and Dan DeNeui, CEO of EVEXIAS Health Solutions.[7] Its listed partners are Ivím Health, CRE8 Pharmacy, Thesis, Empower, EVEXIAS and Noom.[7] Ivím advertises compounded tirzepatide at $117 a month and compounded semaglutide at $65 a month. Noom sells a compounded semaglutide “microdose” program.[19]

Its policy board. Two former governors, Phil Bryant of Mississippi and Ricardo Rosselló of Puerto Rico, sit on its government board, alongside the chief executive of MAHA Holdings, a former senior HHS adviser, a Republican strategist and Brigham Buhler, CEO of the Ways2Well clinics. Buhler co-wrote a book on peptides with Rosebush.[7][22]

Its link to the nominations. The medical board includes James LaValle, chair of the International Peptide Society.[7] Nominations filed for that society were among those withdrawn in April, which let FDA take the peptides off its “significant safety risks” list and send them to the July advisory meeting.[13][22]

Who spoke at its September congress

AAPM held its first “PepMed” congress in Washington on September 17–18. Its published lineup, marked as a draft confirmed on a rolling basis, lists the HHS Assistant Secretary for Health, Adm. Brian Christine, and HHS senior adviser Calley Means.[9] It also lists two people tied to FDA’s advisory committee:

  • Rep. Diana Harshbarger of Tennessee was listed as the featured keynote, “A Pharmacist in Congress.”[9] Her only son, state Sen. Bobby Harshbarger, a pharmacist at the family’s Premier Pharmacy in Kingsport, sits on FDA’s compounding advisory committee.[11][15] He voted yes on all seven peptides in July, as Law Street Media reported in September and the Center for Science in the Public Interest’s member-by-member vote table in its Oct 14 letter to FDA shows; PeptideBase’s webcast tracker matches. FDA has not posted official minutes yet.[13][24][12]
  • Timothy Fensky, the pharmacy-boards representative on the same committee, is listed on a congress panel about making the recommended peptides, under “The Peptide Council.”[9][11] An AAPM board member calls the council AAPM’s “sister organization,” writing quality specifications for the six peptides.[16] PeptideBase’s webcast tracker shows Fensky abstained on every peptide vote in July (FDA minutes not posted).[12]

Speaking at a conference isn’t a conflict by itself, and we found no rule it breaks. It does show how small this world is.

How the July votes broke

FDA’s compounding advisory committee voted on seven peptides on July 23–24. FDA’s staff had recommended against all seven.[13] Member-level ballots as transcribed by PeptideBase from the webcast — FDA has not posted official minutes — show that across the 8 roll-call ballots, the 8 members new to the committee in 2026 cast 60 yes votes and 1 no. The other 11 voters, including holdovers, the pharmacopeia, consumer and patient representatives and temporary experts, cast 0 yes votes, 44 no and 8 abstentions. All 8 abstentions came from Fensky. Those group totals are a Jabbit reconstruction of PeptideBase’s transcription, cross-checked against FDA’s roster topic limits.[12] Reporters found that most of the new members run clinics or businesses that sell peptides.[13][14] The committee’s votes are advice. FDA still has to decide.[13]

The “pharmacies are the safe source” argument

AAPM’s case is that the 2023 restrictions pushed people to overseas powder and unlabeled vials, and that licensed pharmacies that test their products are safer.[21][22] There’s evidence for the problem it describes. In the first published lab test of gray-market retatrutide vials, three vials labeled 10 mg held 5 to 19 mg.[23] A tested pharmacy product would very likely beat that.

But a pharmacy isn’t automatically clean. In September, FDA sent Empower Pharmacy, whose CEO sits on AAPM’s industry board, its second warning letter in 18 months.[17][18] After a November 2025 inspection, FDA said Empower made sterile products under insanitary conditions. It also said Empower compounded copies of Zepbound and Wegovy “regularly and in inordinate amounts,” with “significant difference” statements that were “repeated verbatim across many records, suggesting that they may be pre-generated.”[17] FDA said some of Empower’s fixes looked adequate and others weren’t documented well enough to judge.[17] The letter is about GLP-1 copies, not BPC-157. In our view, it is still the most detailed recent FDA record we found on a large compounder’s practices.

And making a peptide cleanly isn’t the same as knowing it works. Rosebush told the committee that the bulks list carries “no requirement to prove safety or efficacy.”[20] That’s accurate. It’s also the problem FDA’s reviewers raised, since none of the six has a solid human trial behind it.[13] Pharmacies that compound under these rules also don’t have to report side effects.[20]

What to watch

  • Wednesday, October 21, 3 PM ET: the AAPM meeting itself. Any materials AAPM hands over should be disclosed on the OIRA meeting record.[1][5]
  • Friday, October 23, 2 PM ET: the Hims & Hers Health meeting.[25]
  • Tuesday, October 27, 2 PM ET: the Alliance for Pharmacy Compounding meeting.[27]
  • The policy text: which peptides, which routes (injection, nasal), what testing and sourcing rules, and whether pharmacies must report side effects.[3]
  • Other meetings: whether anyone else, such as safety groups, medical societies or drug makers, asks for one before the review ends.[1]

Bottom line

The first group on record to meet the White House about FDA’s peptide policy is a 2025 society whose own pages list the leaders of big compounding pharmacies and GLP-1 telehealth companies as advisers and partners. Its chairman runs the trade group that sued FDA to keep compounded Zepbound going.[1][6][7][10] Its harm-reduction argument is fair. But several businesses on its board and partner pages make or sell compounded drugs, so FDA’s answer affects them commercially, and we found no published trial showing that BPC-157, TB-500 or the others work in people.[13][21]
