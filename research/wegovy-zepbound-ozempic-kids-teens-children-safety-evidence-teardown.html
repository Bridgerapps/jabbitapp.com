Published October 28, 2026 · Updated October 28, 2026

Can kids and teens take Wegovy, Ozempic or Zepbound? In the US, Wegovy and Saxenda are approved for obesity from age 12, while Zepbound and Ozempic are not approved for anyone under 18, and WHO advises against weight-loss drugs before age 10. Teen trials show big BMI drops, more vomiting and gallstones than in adults, and regain after stopping. Long-term effects on growth and development are unknown.

Parents are asking this more often. On October 7, 2026, the World Health Organization published its first global guideline on child and adolescent obesity, and it draws a line at age 10.[11] Novo Nordisk has said detailed results from STEP Young, its Wegovy trial in children aged 6 to under 12, will be presented at ObesityWeek on November 14 to 17.[12] On October 26, a University of Georgia researcher told UGA Today that these drugs are sometimes prescribed “even younger in special circumstances.”[23] Our plain answer: in the US, two GLP-1 drugs are approved for obesity from age 12, and in teen trials they lowered BMI a lot.[1][5][2] Teens also had more vomiting and gallbladder problems than adults on the same drug, weight came back after stopping, and nobody yet knows the long-term effects on growth, puberty or the developing brain.[1][6][22]

Why this matters now

Three things landed within a few weeks. WHO’s new guideline says it “does not recommend pharmacological treatment” for children aged 0 to 9, and that for ages 10 to 19 “approved medicines may be considered only when a supervized multimodal lifestyle programme has not achieved the desired results.”[11] Novo Nordisk reported topline STEP Young results in September, saying 40.4% of children aged 6 to under 12 on semaglutide were no longer classified as having obesity at 68 weeks, compared with none on placebo.[12] That figure is company-reported and not yet peer-reviewed. And Eli Lilly’s SURMOUNT-ADOLESCENTS trial of tirzepatide, the drug in Zepbound, reached its primary completion date on June 2, 2026, according to its registry record, which had no results posted when we checked.[14]

Use was already rising. A 2024 JAMA research letter found that the number of US adolescents and young adults (ages 12 to 25) filling GLP-1 prescriptions rose from 8,722 in 2020 to 60,567 in 2023. Among adolescents alone, the number of girls rose from 961 to 6,607 and boys from 692 to 4,178.[21] The authors pointed to “the absence of data regarding the health effects of long-term use.”[21]

The JAMA letter also notes that three older GLP-1 drugs, dulaglutide, exenatide and liraglutide, are approved for type 2 diabetes in adolescents.[21] A child on one of these drugs off label, outside its approved age or use, is outside what the trials studied.

What the teen trials show

STEP TEENS randomized 201 teens aged 12 to 17 with obesity to weekly semaglutide or placebo for 68 weeks, after a 12-week lifestyle run-in. BMI fell 16.1% on semaglutide and rose 0.6% on placebo; 73% vs 18% lost at least 5%.[2][1] In a follow-up analysis, 44.9% on semaglutide moved below the obesity BMI cutoff, vs 12.1% on placebo, and the share with the most severe class of obesity fell from 37.3% to 13.6%.[3] Insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) fell 35.0% vs 5.3%, and the liver enzyme ALT fell 17.9% vs 3.3%.[4] The trial was funded by Novo Nordisk.[2]

The older daily drug did less. In the 2020 liraglutide (Saxenda) teen trial, the BMI difference vs placebo was 4.64 percentage points.[6] In SURPASS-PEDS, tirzepatide lowered blood sugar in youth aged 10 to 17 with type 2 diabetes, and BMI fell 7.4% and 11.2% on the 5 mg and 10 mg arms vs 0.4% on placebo at 30 weeks; the trial was funded by Eli Lilly.[10]

A 2025 JAMA Pediatrics meta-analysis pooled 18 placebo-controlled trials with 1,402 children and teens. GLP-1 drugs improved weight, blood sugar and blood pressure, but the median treatment time was about half a year (0.51 years), and the authors describe the follow-up as “relatively short.”[17]

Children under 12: STEP Young

STEP Young is the first large semaglutide obesity trial in children under 12. Novo Nordisk’s release describes 165 children aged 6 to under 12 treated for 68 weeks, with more than 85% having class II or III obesity at the start. Novo says 40.4% on semaglutide were no longer classified as having obesity, vs none on placebo, and that “no safety concerns were identified for growth or pubertal development.”[12] Three caveats. First, the 40.4% uses the “trial product estimand,” which Novo defines as the effect “if all children adhered to treatment.” Second, these are topline numbers from the company, with no peer-reviewed paper yet. Third, the registry record lists 212 participants aged 6 to 18,[13] while the release reports 165 children aged 6 to under 12. Public documents do not explain the gap, which may reflect cohorts not covered in the release. Novo says the trial is run under regulators’ post-marketing requirements.[12]

WHO’s guideline does not recommend drug treatment below age 10.[11] Whether regulators approve any drug for younger children will depend on their review of the full data.

Side effects: what is different for teens

The Wegovy label states it plainly: teens on Wegovy “had greater incidences of cholelithiasis, cholecystitis, hypotension, rash, and urticaria compared to adults.”[1] That means more gallstones, gallbladder inflammation, low blood pressure, rash and hives. In STEP TEENS, gallstones occurred in 3.8% on Wegovy vs 0% on placebo, and gallbladder inflammation in 0.8% vs 0%. In adult trials the gallstone rates were 1.6% vs 0.7%.[1] Vomiting affected 36% of teens on Wegovy vs 10% on placebo, compared with 24% vs 6% in adults.[1]

On Saxenda, 34.4% of teens vomited vs 4% on placebo, and low blood sugar with symptoms was reported in 15.2% vs 4%, even though none of these teens had diabetes. One teen on Saxenda (0.8%) had pancreatitis and stopped treatment.[5] In the liraglutide trial, 10.4% stopped because of side effects vs none on placebo.[6] In youth with type 2 diabetes, the Mounjaro label lists more vomiting, belly pain and low blood sugar than in adults.[9] For context on managing nausea in adults, see our teardown of tradipitant for GLP-1 vomiting.

Mood and suicidal thoughts

This is a common worry. The evidence so far is reassuring but limited. The 2025 meta-analysis of randomized trials found no significant difference in suicidal thoughts or behaviors vs placebo, over short follow-up.[17] A 2026 meta-analysis of 11 studies (about 10,175 adolescents) found GLP-1 drugs “were not associated with higher rates of suicidal behaviour or depression,” and said a possible lower risk “should be interpreted cautiously.”[19] Two health-record studies found lower rates of suicidal thoughts or attempts in teens starting a GLP-1 than in matched teens on lifestyle programs (hazard ratios 0.67 and 0.74).[18][20] These are observational. Teens who get a prescription may differ in ways the matching misses, and in one study the result was not significant when compared with metformin instead.[20] In the liraglutide trial, one suicide occurred in the drug group; the investigator assessed it as “unlikely to be related to the trial treatment.”[6] For adults, see our review of GLP-1 drugs and mood.

What happens when teens stop

The liraglutide teen trial followed participants for 26 weeks after stopping. BMI scores rose more in the liraglutide group than in the placebo group after discontinuation.[6] Emily Noble of the University of Georgia told UGA Today that “weight loss often rebounds when you go off the medication.”[23] A Novo Nordisk trial testing how to keep weight off in 12- to 15-year-olds, STEP TEENS Weight Maintenance, has an estimated primary completion in November 2028.[16] In practice, a teen who starts may face years of treatment or a likely regain. See also what happens when you stop Ozempic, Wegovy or Zepbound.

The longevity question

A drug started at 13 could be taken for decades, so the healthy-aging question is bigger here than in adults. WHO reports that obesity among 5- to 19-year-olds rose from 2% in 1990 to 8%, about 170 million young people in 2024.[11] Lowering BMI and insulin resistance in the teen years could plausibly matter for adult heart and metabolic health, but no pediatric trial has measured those long-term outcomes.[17] A 2026 review from the University of Georgia lists “critical knowledge gaps” on “growth, pubertal development, cognition, neural reward processing, and lifelong energy balance regulation.”[22] Noble also asked whether children losing weight on these drugs are “also losing muscle mass.”[23] That is Jabbit’s analysis of the gap: the short-term benefit is well measured, and the long-term trade-off is not. For adults, see muscle loss on GLP-1s and bone density.

What a careful reader should notice

  1. The main trials were funded by the companies that sell the drugs: STEP TEENS and the liraglutide trial by Novo Nordisk, SURPASS-PEDS by Eli Lilly.[2][6][10]
  2. STEP Young numbers come from a company release, not a peer-reviewed paper, and the headline figure assumes every child kept taking the drug.[12]
  3. Trials lasted about a year or less; the pooled median was 0.51 years.[17]
  4. STEP TEENS added a 12-week lifestyle run-in, and WHO expects supervised lifestyle care first. Results without that support may differ.[1][11]
  5. The mood findings that look protective are observational and could reflect who gets prescribed.[20]
  6. The 594% rise in dispensing covers ages 12 to 25, and the letter had no data on why the drugs were prescribed.[21]

Questions parents ask

Can a 12-year-old take Wegovy?
In the US, the Wegovy injection is approved for obesity from age 12. WHO advises that drugs for ages 10 to 19 be considered only after a supervised lifestyle program has not worked. The decision belongs to the family and the child’s doctor.
Is Zepbound or Ozempic approved for teenagers?
No. Both labels say safety and effectiveness have not been established in pediatric patients. Mounjaro, which contains the same drug as Zepbound, is approved for type 2 diabetes from age 10. Tirzepatide trials in teens with obesity are underway.
How much weight do teens lose on Wegovy?
In the STEP TEENS trial, BMI fell 16.1% on Wegovy over 68 weeks and rose 0.6% on placebo. About 45% moved below the obesity cutoff, compared with 12% on placebo.
Are the side effects worse in teens than in adults?
Some are. The Wegovy label says teens had more gallstones, gallbladder inflammation, low blood pressure, rash and hives than adults. Vomiting affected 36% of teens on Wegovy, compared with 24% of adults.
Do GLP-1 drugs affect growth or puberty?
Nobody knows the long-term answer yet. Novo Nordisk says STEP Young found no safety concerns for growth or puberty in children aged 6 to under 12, but full results are not published. Researchers list growth, puberty and brain development as open questions.
Does the weight come back if a teen stops?
In the liraglutide teen trial, BMI rose more after stopping the drug than after stopping placebo. A trial on keeping weight off in teens is not expected to finish its main phase until 2028.

Bottom line

For teens with obesity, Wegovy and Saxenda are approved from age 12, and the trials show large, real drops in BMI and improvements in insulin resistance. Teens also had more vomiting and gallbladder problems than adults, weight tends to return after stopping, and the trials are too short to show effects on growth, puberty, the brain or adult health. Zepbound and Ozempic are not approved for anyone under 18, and WHO advises against weight-loss drugs before age 10. Watch for the full STEP Young results in mid-November and the first SURMOUNT-ADOLESCENTS data.
