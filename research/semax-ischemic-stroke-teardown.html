Semax stroke teardown: what the Russian trials show, and what they don't

Semax is a synthetic heptapeptide (Met-Glu-His-Phe-Pro-Gly-Pro), an ACTH(4-10) analog. In Russia it is a registered prescription nasal drop, and its 1% strength is labeled for acute moderate-to-severe ischemic stroke.[5][6] In the United States it is not an approved drug.[7][8] Most English-language claims about semax and stroke trace back to a small number of Russian papers. We opened the most-cited recent one in Russian, translated the key passages, and rebuilt its tables. Where we only had an abstract, we say so.

The short version

  • Main study: Gusev et al. 2018 enrolled 110 patients who were already in rehabilitation, roughly 3 or 7 months after an ischemic stroke. They compared semax plus rehabilitation with rehabilitation alone.[1]
  • Results: plasma BDNF roughly tripled by 1 month in the semax subgroups (for example 7.8 → 22.6 pg/mL) and stayed higher at 5 months. Barthel index scores rose faster in the semax subgroups. By 5 months the early-rehab groups ended up close together (85.8 with semax vs 84.1 without).[1]
  • Design limits: there was no placebo, the paper describes no blinding, the randomization method isn't given, groups were 20–30 patients, and there was no prespecified primary endpoint or correction for many comparisons.[1]
  • Safety: the authors report no side effects, and 95% of patients rated tolerability good or very good. That is a small, short, open-label sample. The Russian label lists mild nasal irritation with long-term use.[1][5]
  • Who did the research: the earlier acute-stroke papers include co-authors from the team that designed semax. The 2018 paper declares no conflicts and does not state its funding.[2][3][4][1]
  • Replication: we found no stroke trial of semax outside Russia, and ClinicalTrials.gov lists no semax trials at all.[9]

What the 2018 study tested

The study was run at the Pirogov Russian National Research Medical University and a Moscow municipal rehabilitation center.[1] It enrolled 110 patients aged 29–73 (mean 58.0 ± 9.7; 43 men, 67 women). They were split by when rehabilitation began: an “early” group of 60 patients (89 ± 9 days after the stroke) and a “late” group of 50 (214 ± 22 days). Each group was then divided into semax and no-semax subgroups: 1a (n=30) and 1b (n=30) early, and 2a (n=30) and 2b (n=20) late.[1]

«В свою очередь каждая группа была рандомизированно разделена на подгруппы больных, получавших семакс … и не получавших его»

Our translation: “Each group in turn was randomly divided into subgroups of patients who received semax … and who did not receive it”[1]

Everyone received the same rehabilitation program: medication, exercise therapy, mechanotherapy, biofeedback, massage, and psychotherapy. The semax subgroups also got the drug:[1]

«1% раствор семакса по 2 капли в каждый носовой ход 3 раза в день (6000 мкг/сут) в течение 10 сут (1-й курс). Через 20 дней после окончания 1-го курса лечение повторяли.»

Our translation: “1% semax solution, 2 drops into each nostril 3 times a day (6,000 mcg/day) for 10 days (1st course). 20 days after the end of the 1st course, treatment was repeated.”[1]

Patients were assessed at baseline and at 1, 2, and 5 months. Outcomes were plasma BDNF (by ELISA), limb strength on the British MRC scale, and the Barthel index, a 0–100 score of independence in daily activities.[1]

Note that this was a recovery-phase population. The Russian label for the 1% drops covers the acute period of moderate-to-severe ischemic stroke, as part of combination therapy.[5]

Gains and shares are our arithmetic from the table means. The paper's own text gives slightly different figures (for example total gains of 27.5 / 25.8 / 23.7 / 18.7 and a 1-month share of 78.5% for 1a), probably because it worked from unrounded values.[1] The pattern is the same either way. Semax subgroups improved sooner, while the 5-month gap between the early-rehab subgroups was under 2 points.

Strength (paresis). About 72–74% of patients had hemiparesis at entry. Limb strength improved in all subgroups. The paper attributes the between-group differences in strength mainly to when rehabilitation started, not to semax.[1]

BDNF and function. Barthel scores correlated with BDNF: r = 0.31 at 1 month and r = 0.24 at the end of the study.[1] Correlations of that size explain roughly 6–10% of the variation. BDNF is also a biomarker, not a clinical outcome.

A small inconsistency. The 5-month BDNF increase for subgroup 1b appears as 45.3% in one place and 41.9% in another. It doesn't change the conclusion, but it is the kind of slip that peer review usually catches.[1]

The authors' conclusion, in their words:

«Применение семакса оказывало основное влияние на уровень и динамику BDNF на протяжении всего наблюдения»

Our translation: “The use of semax had the main influence on the level and dynamics of BDNF throughout the entire observation period”[1]

What it got right

To be fair to the study, it has real strengths. It reported actual group means for every outcome at every visit, so the result can be rebuilt. Rehabilitation was standardized across subgroups, and baseline Barthel, BDNF, and paresis scores were similar between subgroups. The outcome scales are standard and widely used. It also did not overclaim on strength: it reported that semax didn't drive the motor results. The regimen, dose, and schedule are spelled out, and the full text is openly readable.[1]

Design: what it can't tell you

  • No placebo, and no blinding described. The comparison groups simply didn't get semax. Patients knew whether they were taking nose drops, and the paper doesn't say whoever scored the Barthel index was blinded. The Barthel index is partly based on observation and self-report, so this matters.[1]
  • Randomization method not reported. The paper says the groups were split “randomly” but gives no method, allocation concealment, or trial registration. In the late group the semax and no-semax subgroups differed significantly in time since stroke (231 ± 25 vs 188 ± 29 days), and their sizes were unequal (30 vs 20).[1]
  • Small groups, many tests. The subgroups had 20–30 patients. There were many t-tests across visits and outcomes, with no stated primary endpoint and no multiplicity adjustment. Results are reported as M ± m (mean ± standard error), which looks tighter than standard deviation.[1]
  • Short and surrogate-heavy. Follow-up lasted 5 months. Recurrence, mortality, and long-term disability weren't studied.[1]

The earlier acute-stroke work (abstracts only)

The study usually cited for acute stroke is Gusev, Skvortsova, Miasoedov et al., 1997. According to its PubMed abstract, 30 semax patients in the acute period of hemispheric ischemic stroke were compared with 80 conventionally treated patients with strokes “analogous in severity and location.” That describes a concurrent comparison group, not a randomized one. It reported “some influence on the rate of restoration of the damaged neurological functions,” with 12 mg/day (moderate strokes) and 18 mg/day (severe) for 5–10 days as the most effective doses.[2] A 1999 follow-up by Miasoedova, Skvortsova et al. was, according to its abstract, a “retrospective comparative” analysis of inflammatory markers.[3] We could not find open full texts of either paper, so we have not checked their tables.

Side effects and safety

The 2018 authors report:

«Прием препарата семакс не приводил к побочным эффектам и к ухудшению соматического состояния. … его переносимость как очень хорошую и хорошую отметили 95% пациентов.»

Our translation: “Taking semax did not lead to side effects or to worsening of physical condition. … 95% of patients rated its tolerability as very good or good.”[1]

The study didn't describe how adverse events were collected, and 60 people on the drug for two 10-day courses can't rule out uncommon harms. The current Russian prescribing information lists possible mild irritation of the nasal lining with long-term use. Contraindications include acute psychiatric disorders, anxiety disorders, a history of seizures, age under 18, and pregnancy or breastfeeding.[5] In the US, FDA lists semax among substances previously placed in compounding category 2 (nomination later withdrawn). It cites aggregation and impurity risks and states there is “no, or limited, safety-related information for proposed routes of administration.”[7]

Funding and conflicts

The 2018 paper states:

«Авторы заявляют об отсутствии конфликта интересов.»

Our translation: “The authors declare no conflict of interest.”[1]

The 2018 paper does not state its funding source.[1] The 1997 and 1999 acute-stroke papers include N.F. Miasoedov and V.N. Nezavibat'ko as co-authors.[2][3] Both also co-authored a 1997 review describing “15 years experience in [the] design and study” of semax.[4] In other words, the people who developed the drug were among the authors of its early clinical evaluation. That doesn't make the results wrong, but it is the kind of tie that independent replication is meant to rule out.

Replication outside Russia

We searched PubMed for semax with stroke or ischemia and found only Russian clinical studies. The rest were animal and gene-expression work, mostly from the same Moscow groups (for example rat BDNF data[10]). A ClinicalTrials.gov search for “semax” returned no registered studies.[9] No independent, placebo-controlled, blinded stroke trial of semax appears in the sources we checked.

Regulatory status

  • United States: not FDA approved. Drugs@FDA lists no approved product,[8] and FDA's compounding page flags safety concerns.[7]
  • Russia: Semax 1% nasal drops are registered in the state drug register (GRLS) to JSC “INPC Peptogen” as a prescription medicine. The registration number is ЛП-№(010596)-(РГ-RU), dated June 18, 2025, replacing Р N000812/01. The 0.1% drops have a separate registration, ЛП-№(009449)-(РГ-RU).[6][5]

Bottom line: the Russian data show semax is consistently associated with higher plasma BDNF and earlier gains in daily-living scores in small, open-label studies. Those studies have no placebo, and the early work involved the drug's developers. None of this has been tested in a blinded, independently run trial, and no regulator outside Russia has reviewed it.
