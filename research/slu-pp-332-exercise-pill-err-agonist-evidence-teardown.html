Home / Research / SLU-PP-332 teardown

Study teardown · SLU-PP-332 (an ERR agonist billed as an “exercise pill”) and its successor SLU-PP-915 · The developers’ mouse studies, an independent aging-kidney study, a human muscle-cell study, anti-doping lab papers, the WADA 2027 Prohibited List, FDA’s NDC, FAERS and compounding lists, registry and PubMed counts · Sources checked October 25, 2026

Published October 25, 2026 · Updated October 25, 2026

SLU-PP-332: does the “exercise in a pill” work in people, and is it safe? Mice given injections ran about 70% longer and obese mice gained less fat, but every result so far comes from mice or cells, we found no human trial, its developers report it is poorly absorbed by mouth, it is not FDA-approved, and anti-doping rules ban it in sport from January 2027.

SLU-PP-332 is a lab-made small molecule that activates estrogen-related receptors (ERRs), control switches inside muscle cells that help turn on many of the same genes endurance training does.[1][24] The “exercise pill” nickname echoes the researchers’ own press releases, titled “Exercise-mimicking drug sheds weight, boosts muscle activity in mice” and “Mimicking exercise with a pill.”[13][14] Does it work? In mice, yes for some measures: after a week of injections, 6 treated mice ran about 70% longer than 6 untreated mice, and obese mice gained far less fat.[1][2] In people, nobody knows. We found no human trial in ClinicalTrials.gov or PubMed, its developers write that it “lacks oral bioavailability,” its safety in humans has never been tested, it is not FDA-approved, and the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) adds it to the list of substances banned at all times from January 1, 2027.[22][23][7][19][15]

Why this matters now

On September 21, 2026, WADA published its 2027 Prohibited List, which names “SLU-PP-332, SLU-PP-915 and related substances” as banned metabolic modulators from January 1, 2027; USADA’s athlete advisory flags the same addition.[15][16][17] Anti-doping laboratories in Cologne and at UCLA published methods in 2026 to find its breakdown products, writing that this may help uncover “illicit use” and detect “misuse.”[9][10] FDA’s National Drug Code directory also shows bulk-powder listings under the name SLU-PP-332, two of them categorized for human prescription compounding and dated 2026.[18]

What SLU-PP-332 is

SLU-PP-332 is a small synthetic chemical (molecular weight about 290), not a peptide and not a hormone.[24] It was designed to switch on ERRα, ERRβ and ERRγ, nuclear receptors that control genes for mitochondria and fat burning in muscle.[1] Despite the name, ERRs do not respond to estrogen. Every animal study we read gave it by injection; the developers’ 2026 paper says it “lacks oral bioavailability,” meaning it is not shown to work when swallowed, and the university release said the next step was “ideally making it available as a pill instead of an injection.”[1][2][7][13] A later compound from the same program, SLU-PP-915, is described as orally bioavailable in mice; it has not been tested in people either.[7][22]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • Exercise endurance (mice). Sedentary mice injected for 7 days ran about 70% longer and 45% farther on a treadmill than untreated mice, in groups of 6.[1] In mice bred without muscle ERRα, the compound gave no extra endurance, which supports the idea that it works through that receptor.[1]
  • Weight and fat (mice). Obese mice weighed about 12% less after 28 days and gained under half a gram of fat, against about 5 grams in untreated obese mice; groups had 8 to 10 male mice.[2] In lean mice, body weight did not change, and glucose tolerance did not change after three weeks.[2]
  • Heart and kidneys (mice). In a mouse heart-failure model, it improved pumping function and survival.[3] In 21-month-old mice treated for 8 weeks, an independent team reported it “reversed the age-related increases in albuminuria, podocyte loss, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory cytokines.”[4]
  • Human muscle (cells only). One study took muscle samples from women having hip surgery and treated the muscle cells in a dish; no woman took the compound.[6]
  • Humans. ClinicalTrials.gov lists 0 studies of SLU-PP-332 or SLU-PP-915, and none of 12 PubMed records reports giving it to a person.[22][23] A 2026 systematic review covered only “experimental studies in animals and cell models.”[11]

The key studies, in one chart

Our analysis: the endurance result that drives the “exercise pill” headlines rests on a one-week injection study with 6 mice per group.[1] Small mouse studies are a normal first step, but many compounds that work in mice never work, or turn out unsafe, in people. The only human material tested so far is cells in a dish and liver fractions in a test tube.[6][9][10]

The developers’ case, in fairness

The developers report more than a single treadmill result. The compound turned on a gene program that overlapped with human acute-exercise data, its endurance effect disappeared in mice lacking muscle ERRα, and it showed benefits in separate mouse models of obesity and heart failure.[1][2][3] A separate team, whose paper declared no competing interests, reported kidney benefits in old mice.[4] The program has moved toward an oral successor, and the developers have been explicit about limits: a university release said human trials would come only after more animal safety testing, and the lead chemist said, “We cannot replace exercise; exercise is important on all levels.”[7][13][14]

Who funds and holds stakes

Authors of the developers’ papers disclose their interests. The 2023 paper states: “T.P.B., B.E., and J.K.W. are stockholders in Myonid Therapeutics, Inc., which focuses on ERR based therapeutics.”[1] The 2026 paper states that SLU-PP-915 “is covered under the intellectual property of Saint Louis University” and that an author is a stockholder in Myonid Therapeutics and Pelagos Pharmaceuticals, “which are both active in developing ERR agonists.”[7] An author of the heart-failure study discloses being “the co-founder and consultant for Pelagos Pharmaceuticals Inc.”[3] A chemistry paper discloses a patent licensed to Myonid, and a 2026 structure study, which calls SLU-PP-332 a “widely used chemical probe,” discloses that two authors “are cofounders and hold stock in Pelagos Pharmaceuticals, Inc.”[5][8] Our analysis: these disclosures are standard and do not mean the results are wrong; they are a reason to want independent replication, which so far exists only for the kidney study.[4]

Safety

  • In people: unknown. No human safety study exists that we could find.[22][23] The 2026 review says “Clinical trials are needed to confirm their efficacy and safety in humans.”[11]
  • In mice: no obvious problems in short studies. The developers reported “no overt toxicity” and normal blood counts and electrolytes in the short 2023 study; the university release said it “hasn’t generated any severe side effects” so far.[1][13] Short studies in a few dozen mice cannot rule out rare or long-term harms.
  • A theoretical question about cancer. Our analysis: SLU-PP-332 works by activating ERRα, and a 2026 review describes the body’s own ERRα as “a crucial driver of breast cancer progression.”[1][12] That review did not test SLU-PP-332, and we found no study of whether the compound changes cancer risk; this is an open question, not a finding.[12][23]
  • Reports to FDA. FDA’s adverse-event database returned no reports for SLU-PP-332. An empty search does not show safety; it may simply mean nobody has reported problems.[19]

Purity and what is in the vial

There is no FDA-approved SLU-PP-332 product and no official quality standard.[19][21] FDA’s NDC directory lists 5 bulk-powder registrations under the name, from 5 labelers. Only 2 list a substance name matching the published structure; 2 list a different, simpler chemical (4-hydroxybenzoic acid), and 1 lists an unrelated chemical name.[18][24] Our analysis: the mismatches may be data-entry errors, but they show that a label reading “SLU-PP-332” is not a guarantee of identity. A listing is a registration, not an FDA approval or quality finding, and says nothing about what any product contains.[18]

Legal status

SLU-PP-332 is not an FDA-approved drug, and Drugs@FDA lists no product containing it.[19] Under section 503A, a pharmacy may compound from a bulk substance that has a USP or NF monograph, is part of an FDA-approved drug, or is on FDA’s 503A bulks list.[21] SLU-PP-332 is not on FDA’s list of nominated bulk substances (categories 1 to 3, updated May 14, 2026).[20] Our analysis, which is not legal advice: based on these records it does not appear to fit any of those three pathways; FDA has not, to our knowledge, issued a statement specific to it. In sport, WADA’s 2027 list names it as banned at all times, in and out of competition, from January 1, 2027; USADA describes it as an example added to the existing metabolic-modulators category, so athletes should not assume it is permitted before then and should check with their anti-doping organization.[15][17]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. “Exercise pill” is a nickname that echoes press releases, not a description of a tested human medicine; the compound studied was injected, and its developers say it is not shown to work by mouth.[13][14][7]
  2. The headline 70% figure comes from 6 mice per group over 7 days.[1]
  3. Lean mice did not lose weight; the weight effect was in obese mice.[2]
  4. The “human” study used cells in a dish, and the human liver work was done by doping labs to detect the compound.[6][9][10]
  5. Anti-doping authorities moved before any human trial: the 2027 ban arrives with zero registered trials.[15][22]

Frequently asked questions

Does SLU-PP-332 work in humans?
Nobody knows. All results come from mice and cells; we found no human trial in ClinicalTrials.gov or PubMed.[22][23][11]
Is SLU-PP-332 safe?
Its safety in people has never been tested. Short mouse studies reported no obvious toxicity, which cannot rule out long-term or rare harms.[1][11]
Is SLU-PP-332 FDA approved?
No. Drugs@FDA lists no product containing it, and it is not on FDA’s list of substances nominated for 503A compounding.[19][20]
Does SLU-PP-332 work as a pill?
Not shown. The mouse studies used injections, and its developers write that it “lacks oral bioavailability.” An oral successor, SLU-PP-915, has only been tested in mice.[1][7]
Is SLU-PP-332 banned in sport?
WADA’s 2027 Prohibited List, in force from January 1, 2027, names SLU-PP-332, SLU-PP-915 and related substances as metabolic modulators banned at all times. USADA calls them examples added to an existing category, so athletes should check with their anti-doping organization before then.[15][16][17]
Does SLU-PP-332 cause weight loss?
In obese mice it cut fat gain and they weighed about 12% less after 28 days; lean mice did not lose weight. There are no human data.[2][22]
Is SLU-PP-332 a peptide?
No. It is a small synthetic chemical with a molecular weight of about 290, not a chain of amino acids.[24]

Bottom line

SLU-PP-332 is a promising research tool in mice: injected animals ran longer, obese mice gained less fat, and separate mouse models showed heart and kidney benefits.[1][2][3][4] It has never been tested in people, is not shown to work by mouth, is not FDA-approved, and products labeled with its name are not checked for identity or purity.[22][7][19][18] For athletes, it is banned from January 1, 2027.[15]
