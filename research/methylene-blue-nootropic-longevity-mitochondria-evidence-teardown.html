Study teardown · Methylene blue (methylthioninium chloride; ProvayBlue; TauRx’s derivative LMTM/HMTM) · The FDA label, Drugs@FDA, FAERS, Australia’s TGA alert, the Alzheimer’s phase 3 trials, small memory and mood trials, lab work on mitochondria, registry and PubMed counts · Sources checked October 25, 2026

Published October 25, 2026 · Updated October 25, 2026

Methylene blue: does the viral brain and longevity supplement work, and is it safe? It is an FDA-approved IV hospital drug for one blood disorder, the related Alzheimer’s drug showed no clear benefit in randomized comparisons in three large trials, the memory evidence in healthy people is one 26-person study, we found no human anti-aging trial, and its label carries a boxed warning for serotonin syndrome with common antidepressants.

Methylene blue is a blue dye that is also a real prescription drug: in the US it is approved as an intravenous hospital treatment for acquired methemoglobinemia, a blood disorder in which red blood cells cannot carry oxygen properly.[1][2] Online it is sold as an oral brain, mood and anti-aging supplement, which Australia’s drug regulator warned about in 2025.[4] Does it work for those uses? The evidence is thin. In three large Alzheimer’s trials, a related drug did not show a clear benefit in randomized comparisons, according to an independent review, the only memory study in healthy people we found gave 26 people a single dose, and we found no human trial of it for aging.[9][12][16][22][27] Is it safe? Its FDA label carries a boxed warning: it can cause serious or fatal serotonin syndrome when combined with common antidepressants and opioids.[1]

Why this matters now

In September 2025, Australia’s Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) reported “increasing importation and use of unregistered oral methylene blue” promoted for “cognitive enhancement, mood improvement, and anti-ageing effects,” and said it would ask border officers to seize non-compliant imports.[4] A 2026 nursing journal article is titled “Methylene blue and the social media phenomenon.”[7] The Alzheimer’s story is also moving: results of TauRx’s LUCIDITY trial were published in 2026, and the company says a UK decision on its drug is expected in 2026.[12][18]

What methylene blue is

Methylene blue is a synthetic dye and an old medicine. The US product is an injection made to a USP standard and given into a vein; it turns skin and body fluids blue and can make pulse oximeters read oxygen levels too low.[1] Drugs@FDA lists one brand, ProvayBlue, approved in 2016, and seven generics, all intravenous; we found no FDA-approved oral methylene blue product.[2] The “mitochondria” idea comes from lab and animal research: a review explains that methylene blue can pass electrons along the cell’s energy chain, and it reports protective effects in animal models of brain disease.[8] The Alzheimer’s drug LMTM (later called HMTM) is a different, related molecule from TauRx, and a review describes it as “derived from methylene blue.”[16]

The evidence, rung by rung

  • Approved use. The FDA label covers one use, acquired methemoglobinemia, treated in a medical setting by IV.[1]
  • Alzheimer’s disease. In the 891-person phase 3 trial, the authors reported that the main analyses “did not show any treatment benefit.”[9] In the 800-person trial, the analysis plan was revised before unblinding and the reported comparisons were not randomized; the authors wrote that “A further suitably randomized trial is required to test this hypothesis.”[10] In LUCIDITY, 598 people, the trial did not show significant differences on its two main goals at 52 weeks; the authors, who include TauRx staff, attribute this to activity in the control group, which got a small amount of the related compound to color urine.[12] Two independent reviews concluded that the randomized trials did not show a clear or consistent benefit.[16][17]
  • Memory in healthy people. One randomized brain-scan study gave 26 healthy adults a single dose and reported 7% more correct answers on a memory task about an hour later.[22] We found no trial testing whether taking it over weeks or months improves memory or focus in healthy people.[27]
  • Mood and anxiety. In bipolar disorder (37 people), depression and anxiety scores improved as an add-on, but thinking scores did not.[23] In PTSD (42 people), both exposure-therapy groups improved and neither differed from standard therapy at three months, though methylene blue improved evaluator-rated response and quality of life compared with placebo.[24] In claustrophobia (42 people), it seemed to help people whose fear had dropped during training and may have hurt those whose fear had not.[25]
  • Surgery. One open-label trial in China gave IV methylene blue during surgery to older adults and reported less delirium; this was a hospital IV use with no placebo and no blinding.[26]
  • Mitochondria and longevity. The case rests on lab and animal work.[8] A PubMed search for randomized trials of methylene blue with aging, longevity or lifespan terms found two records, neither about taking it to slow aging.[27] ClinicalTrials.gov lists 343 studies mentioning methylene blue across all uses; we did not classify them all.[28]

The key trials, in one chart

Our analysis: the three biggest trials, which together enrolled more than three times as many people as the other six combined, tested the TauRx derivative in Alzheimer’s disease, and none showed a clear benefit in its randomized comparisons; the claimed benefits come from revised or non-randomized analyses.[9][10][12][16] The positive signals in the chart come from studies of 26 to 248 people, one of them open-label and in hospital, and the only healthy-person study measured the effect of a single dose.[22][26]

The Alzheimer’s argument, in fairness

TauRx and its co-authors make a specific case. They report that the small amount of the compound used to color urine in the control group was itself active, so a normal placebo comparison was not possible.[12][14][15] A 2026 analysis compared LUCIDITY participants with outside control groups, matching 127 pairs.[14] The company says HMTM is investigational and “not approved or licensed by any medicines regulator,” that the UK’s MHRA asked for more information in October 2025, and that it expects a decision in 2026.[18][19] TauRx’s website also highlights biomarker results from LUCIDITY and the outside-group comparison.[18] Our analysis: comparisons with outside groups are weaker evidence than randomized ones, and the 2018 authors themselves wrote: “A further suitably randomized trial is required to test this hypothesis.”[10] A regulator’s decision would be the next real data point.

Safety

  • Serotonin syndrome. The label’s boxed warning says it “may cause serious or fatal serotonergic syndrome” with serotonergic drugs and opioids, and tells prescribers to avoid using it with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs and opioids.[1] Lab work shows it strongly blocks MAO-A, the same enzyme targeted by older MAOI antidepressants.[5] The TGA gives the same warning for oral products, naming SSRIs and some painkillers.[4] A 2024 toxicology case series describes serotonin toxicity linked to methylene blue.[6]
  • G6PD deficiency. The label rules it out in people with G6PD deficiency, a common inherited condition, because of the risk of hemolytic anemia (red blood cells breaking down).[1][4]
  • Pregnancy. The label says it may cause fetal harm; the TGA says it should not be used during pregnancy.[1][4]
  • Other effects. The label lists confusion, dizziness and vision changes, blue skin and body fluids, and falsely low pulse-oximeter readings.[1] The LUCIDITY paper lists headache and diarrhea as the most common side effects of the derivative.[12]
  • Reports. FAERS holds 1,605 reports naming methylene blue, and 518 include serotonin syndrome, the most common term; such reports are unverified and do not show cause.[3]

Purity and “supplement” products

The FDA-approved product is an injection made to a USP standard and given under medical care.[1][2] The TGA says imported oral products “have not been assessed by us for quality, safety or efficacy,” may contain “incorrect or undisclosed and harmful ingredients,” and mostly “do not include clear instructions or warnings.”[4] Our analysis: a product sold as a supplement, a “research chemical” or a dye has had no regulator check of what is in it, so its purity and strength cannot be assumed. We do not name or rate sellers.

Legal status

In the US, methylene blue injection is an FDA-approved prescription drug for acquired methemoglobinemia, and Drugs@FDA lists no approved oral product.[1][2] In Australia, the TGA says unregistered oral products are being imported for off-label uses and that it will ask the Australian Border Force to seize those that do not meet import conditions.[4] TauRx’s HMTM is investigational everywhere, according to the company, with a UK application under review.[18][19][21]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the sources cited.

  1. Two different molecules get blended together. The large Alzheimer’s trials tested TauRx’s derivative, not supplement methylene blue.[16][9][12]
  2. The trial design changed its description. The 2022 design paper described a “placebo-controlled phase”; the 2026 results paper describes the control as a urine colorant that its authors now say was active.[13][12]
  3. “Boosts mitochondria” is a lab finding, not a human outcome. We found no randomized trial of methylene blue for aging.[8][27]
  4. The interaction risk is not hypothetical. It is in a boxed warning, explained by lab work, and repeated by a second regulator.[1][5][4]
  5. The best-supported human uses are in hospitals, by IV, under monitoring.[1][26]

Frequently asked questions

Does methylene blue improve memory?
One small study of 26 healthy adults found 7% more correct answers on a memory task after a single dose. A bipolar trial found no effect on thinking. We found no long-term trial in healthy people.[22][23][27]
Is methylene blue safe?
Its FDA label has a boxed warning for serious or fatal serotonin syndrome with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs and opioids. It is ruled out in G6PD deficiency and may harm a fetus.[1][4]
Is methylene blue FDA approved?
Yes, but only as an injection (ProvayBlue and generics) for acquired methemoglobinemia. Drugs@FDA lists no approved oral product.[1][2]
Can you take methylene blue with antidepressants?
The FDA label says to avoid combining it with SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs and opioids because of serotonin syndrome. Australia’s TGA gives the same warning for oral products. Ask your prescriber.[1][4]
Is methylene blue approved for Alzheimer’s disease?
No. TauRx’s related drug HMTM is investigational; its phase 3 trials did not show a clear benefit in randomized comparisons, according to an independent review, and the company says a UK decision is expected in 2026.[9][12][16][18]
Does methylene blue help mitochondria or slow aging?
Lab and animal studies show effects on the cell’s energy chain, but we found no human trial showing it slows aging.[8][27]
Are methylene blue supplements pure?
There is no guarantee. Australia’s TGA says imported oral products were not assessed for quality and may contain wrong or undisclosed ingredients.[4]

Bottom line

Methylene blue is a useful hospital drug for one blood disorder.[1] As a brain or longevity supplement, the human evidence is a few small studies with mixed results, and the large Alzheimer’s trials of a related drug did not show a clear benefit in randomized comparisons.[22][23][9][12][16] Its risks are documented: a boxed warning for serotonin syndrome with common antidepressants and opioids, plus G6PD and pregnancy cautions, and regulators have not checked oral products sold online.[1][4]
