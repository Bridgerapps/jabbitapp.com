Study teardown · LL-37 (cathelicidin, developed as ropocamptide, discussed online as an “immune” or “antimicrobial” peptide) · FDA documents, the human trials and their sponsor disclosures, registry and FAERS counts, the core patent · Sources checked October 24, 2026

Published October 24, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

LL-37 (cathelicidin), the “immune peptide”: does it work, and is it safe? Your body makes it and small wound trials looked promising, but the largest trial missed its goal, nobody has tested injecting it, and FDA flags immune, reproductive and tumor-growth concerns.

LL-37 is a natural human peptide, the only cathelicidin our bodies make, released by skin, gut and airway cells and by white blood cells to kill microbes and signal the immune system.[4][5] As a drug, it has been tested in four small human trials, three of them on wounds and one by injection into melanoma tumors.[27][8][9][12][13] A 34-patient leg ulcer trial looked promising, but the 148-patient follow-up missed its main goal, and its developer later said it could not raise the money to continue.[8][9][11] We found no controlled trial of LL-37 injected under the skin or taken by mouth. FDA says it lacks the safety information to know whether it would harm people, and it cites immune, impurity, male-reproduction and tumor-growth concerns.[27][1]

Why this matters now

FDA says its Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee will discuss cathelicidin (LL-37) at a meeting before the end of February 2027, together with GHK-Cu, dihexa acetate, melanotan II and PEG-MGF. All five are being considered for the list of substances pharmacies may compound.[2] No date or briefing materials have been posted. FDA’s separate interim peptide compounding policy has been under White House review since October 14, 2026, and because its list of peptides is not public, it is unknown whether it covers LL-37.[3] Meanwhile, LL-37 is widely discussed online as a peptide for immunity, stubborn infections, biofilms and gut health. This page sets out what the human record shows before that meeting. For the wider policy, see our FDA peptide compounding explainer.

What LL-37 is

LL-37 is a 37-amino-acid fragment cut from a larger precursor protein, hCAP18. It is the only peptide of the cathelicidin family found in humans.[5] The body makes it in skin, the gut, the airways and other linings, and in white blood cells. In the lab it kills a broad range of bacteria and helps break up biofilms. It also attracts and activates immune cells and helps wound repair.[5][25] Vitamin D directly switches on its gene in immune cells, which is one way researchers think vitamin D supports defense against tuberculosis.[6][7] None of this lab biology shows that taking extra LL-37 helps healthy people. PubMed lists more than 4,700 papers on LL-37 or cathelicidin, mostly laboratory, animal or biomarker studies.[29]

Despite decades of interest, LL-37 has not become an approved antibiotic. A 2021 review lists the obstacles as “high cost, lower activity in physiological environments, susceptibility to proteolytic degradation, and high toxicity to human cells.”[25] The wound patent itself says LL-37’s “cytotoxic effect” may need to be reduced with a lipid in the formula.[26]

The human evidence: four small trials

  • Leg ulcers, 2014 (34 patients): after a placebo run-in, patients got one of three strengths of LL-37 or placebo on their ulcers twice a week for 4 weeks. The two lower strengths cut mean ulcer area by 68% and 50%, with healing rates about six and three times placebo, though only the first was statistically significant (p=0.003 and p=0.088). The highest strength did no better than placebo, and no safety concerns were reported.[8] The first author worked for Pergamum AB, an industry developer.[8]
  • Leg ulcers, 2021 (148 patients, phase IIb): this was the confirmatory test, run at 15 sites in Poland and Sweden for up to 13 weeks. It found no difference: 26.5% and 24.7% of patients on the two strengths had confirmed complete closure, compared with 25.3% on placebo.[9]
  • Diabetic foot ulcers, 2023 (25 patients): LL-37 cream improved a measure of healthy new tissue (granulation) at every weekly check over 4 weeks. It did not significantly lower inflammatory markers or bacterial counts, which matters given the peptide’s antimicrobial reputation.[12] Its registry record still lists the status as unknown, with no results posted.[27]
  • Melanoma, 2015–2020 (3 treated): this uncontrolled MD Anderson study injected LL-37 into tumors. Posted results cover 3 participants, one of whom stopped for lack of efficacy, which is too few to judge benefit.[13]

The largest trial: a miss, then a subgroup

When HEAL LL-37 missed its primary endpoint, its sponsor, Promore Pharma, pointed to patients with large ulcers (at least 10 cm²). In that group, 28.1% on the lower strength healed, compared with 8.1% on placebo (p=0.046).[9] Promore’s own clarification said that “this subgroup analysis regarding efficacy was not part of the original statistical analysis plan,” while noting that randomization had been stratified by wound size.[10] The higher strength did not show the same pattern (19.6%).[9] Our analysis: a single unplanned subgroup result in a trial that otherwise found no effect is a reason for a new trial, not proof of benefit. The paper discloses that Promore funded the study, that three authors were Promore employees and that one was a minority shareholder.[9]

That new trial has not happened. In a notice from Nasdaq Stockholm, Promore’s board is quoted as assessing in 2023 that “the opportunities to raise the capital needed to develop the Company’s program for ropocamptide were limited.” The company then merged into a medical device business under a new name.[11] Its core US wound patent has since expired, according to Google Patents.[26]

Safety: what is and isn’t known

  • FDA’s view: FDA says compounded LL-37 “may pose risk for immunogenicity for certain routes of administration” and may have problems with “peptide-related impurities.” It says it “lacks sufficient safety-related information” to know whether LL-37 would harm people, and that animal and lab research suggests “detrimental effects on male reproduction” and that it “can be protumorigenic in some tissues.”[1]
  • In the wound trials: LL-37 on the skin was generally well tolerated in studies lasting 4 to 13 weeks. In HEAL, 7.4% of patients had a serious adverse event, none judged related to the drug, and there were no deaths.[9][8][12] In the foot ulcer trial, one patient had mild skin irritation that the authors attributed to wound fluid.[12] These results are for local use on wounds and do not tell us about injected products.
  • Injected into tumors: a case report from the melanoma study describes wart-like skin growths with atypical cells, resembling keratoacanthoma, that appeared about 45 days after injections began and cleared within 2 months of stopping.[14] The trial registry lists a squamous cell carcinoma, actinic keratosis and other events in single participants, without saying whether LL-37 caused them.[13]
  • Autoimmune and inflammatory disease: LL-37 helps drive inflammation in psoriasis by turning the body’s own DNA into an immune trigger, and about two-thirds of patients with moderate-to-severe psoriasis have T cells that target it.[15][16] Excess cathelicidin promotes skin inflammation in rosacea, and LL-37–DNA complexes activate immune cells in lupus.[18][17][19] These studies concern the body’s own LL-37 in disease. Whether extra LL-37 could worsen these conditions has not been tested (our analysis).
  • Cancer, in both directions: LL-37 is overexpressed in some ovarian, breast and lung cancers, and blocking it slowed ovarian tumors in mice.[20][21] In colon and stomach cancer models it appears to suppress tumors instead.[22][23] That split is consistent with FDA’s wording that it can be protumorigenic “in some tissues” (our analysis).[1]
  • Male reproduction: in the lab, LL-37 stopped mouse and human sperm from swimming, and treated sperm failed to fertilize in mice. The researchers proposed it as a contraceptive.[24] FDA does not cite specific papers, so linking this study to FDA’s concern is our analysis.[1]
  • Adverse event reports: FAERS has no reports coded as LL-37 or cathelicidin and one non-serious report naming ropocamptide among 23 products. That is far too little to judge safety either way.[28]

Legal status

No LL-37 product is approved by FDA.[28] FDA lists cathelicidin LL-37 among bulk substances whose compounding nominations were withdrawn, with the safety summary quoted above, and its advisory committee is due to discuss it by February 2027.[1][2] Whether the pending interim peptide policy covers LL-37 is not public.[3]

What a careful reader should notice

These points are our analysis of the cited sources.

  1. “Your body makes it” is true, but that does not make extra LL-37 safe. The same peptide fuels inflammation in psoriasis, rosacea and lupus.[5][15][18][17]
  2. The encouraging leg ulcer result came from 34 patients. When the trial was repeated with 148, the effect disappeared.[8][9]
  3. In both leg ulcer trials, a stronger preparation did not do better, and in the first trial the highest strength matched placebo. A higher strength being no better is a warning sign, not an invitation to use more.[8][9]
  4. The antimicrobial reputation rests mainly on lab work. The one trial that measured bacteria in wounds found no significant drop.[25][12]
  5. All controlled human data are for LL-37 applied to wounds. We found no trial of injecting it under the skin or taking it by mouth.[27]
  6. FDA’s committee will be weighing a peptide that FDA itself says it lacks the safety information to assess.[1][2]

Frequently asked questions

Is LL-37 safe?
No one knows for use outside trials. Applied to wounds in trials lasting up to 13 weeks, it was generally well tolerated. FDA says it lacks the safety information to know whether LL-37 would cause harm in people, and it flags immunogenicity, impurity, male-reproduction and tumor-growth concerns. Injected use has not been tested in a controlled trial.[9][8][1][27]
Does LL-37 help wounds heal?
A 34-patient leg ulcer trial and a 25-patient diabetic foot ulcer trial reported benefits, but the largest trial, with 148 patients, found no difference from placebo in wound closure. Its large-wound subgroup signal came from an analysis that was not pre-planned.[8][12][9][10]
Does LL-37 boost immunity or clear infections and biofilms?
LL-37 is part of the body’s natural defenses and kills microbes and disrupts biofilms in lab studies. It has not been approved as an antibiotic, and the one wound trial that measured bacteria found no significant reduction. We found no human trial for immunity, Lyme disease or gut infections.[5][25][12][27]
Is LL-37 FDA approved or legal to compound?
No LL-37 product is FDA approved. FDA lists it among withdrawn compounding nominations with safety concerns, and its advisory committee is due to discuss it before the end of February 2027.[28][1][2]
Can LL-37 cause cancer?
There is no evidence that it causes cancer in people, but research points both ways. It is overexpressed in, and may help drive, some ovarian, breast and lung cancers, while it appears to suppress colon and stomach cancer in lab models. FDA says it can be protumorigenic in some tissues.[20][21][22][23][1]
Does LL-37 affect fertility?
In lab and mouse studies, LL-37 stopped sperm from moving and prevented fertilization, and researchers proposed it as a contraceptive. FDA cites nonclinical findings suggesting harm to male reproduction. Human effects are unknown.[24][1]
What is ropocamptide?
Ropocamptide is the name Promore Pharma used for LL-37 as a wound-healing drug. Promore’s board said in 2023 that its chances of raising money to develop it were limited, and the company has since become a device business.[9][11]

Bottom line

LL-37 is a genuine part of human immunity, and it showed early promise when applied to chronic wounds.[5][8] But the only large trial found no benefit, the developer stopped, nobody has tested injected use in a controlled trial, and FDA says it cannot judge whether LL-37 is safe while citing immune, reproductive and tumor-growth concerns.[9][11][27][1] Anyone using it outside a trial would be relying on lab and wound data that do not answer those questions.
