Menotropins (Menopur): what the US label says about side effects, storage and use

Menotropins, sold as Menopur, is a mix of gonadotropins with FSH and LH activity, extracted from the urine of postmenopausal women and further purified. Each vial contains 75 International Units of FSH activity and 75 International Units of LH activity, according to its US prescribing information (revised May 2018).[1] This page sets out what that label says about its approved use, how it’s supplied, storage, side effects and warnings.

What the label says it is

The label describes Menopur as a preparation of gonadotropins (FSH and LH activity) that produces ovarian follicular growth and maturation in women who do not have primary ovarian failure. It says hCG must be given to induce ovulation once follicular maturation is sufficient.[1]

What it’s approved for in the US

The US label lists one indication: development of multiple follicles and pregnancy in ovulatory women as part of an Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) cycle.[1]

How it’s supplied

Menopur comes as a vial of lyophilized (freeze-dried) powder for subcutaneous injection. Each vial is supplied with a 2 mL vial of 0.9% Sodium Chloride Injection, USP, as the diluent, and a Q•Cap vial adapter.[1] The powder is mixed before use; your clinic tells you how. This page doesn’t give mixing instructions.

Does Menopur need to be refrigerated?

Its US label doesn’t require refrigeration. The label says the freeze-dried powder may be stored refrigerated or at room temperature, 3–25°C (37–77°F), protected from light. Once it’s mixed, the label says to use it immediately and discard any unused material.[1] If your pharmacy or clinic gave you different storage instructions, ask them which applies to your supply.

Menotropins (Menopur) side effects on the label

These are the most frequent adverse reactions the label reports from its IVF trials. They are rates from those trials, not a forecast for any one person.[1]

Side effectRate the label reports
Abdominal pain6.7%
Headache6.2%
Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS)6.2%
Injection site pain and reaction3.9%

The label also lists these serious risks: ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome; abnormal ovarian enlargement; atelectasis, acute respiratory distress syndrome and exacerbation of asthma; thromboembolic events (thrombophlebitis in less than 1% of trial subjects); ovarian torsion; multi-fetal gestation and birth; ectopic pregnancy; spontaneous abortion; and ovarian neoplasms, for which a causal relationship has not been established.[1]

Warnings on the label

  • Menopur should only be used by physicians experienced in infertility treatment, and gonadotropin therapy requires appropriate monitoring facilities. The label says to use the lowest effective dose.[1]
  • OHSS may progress rapidly to a serious medical event. In the label’s IVF clinical trial it occurred in 7.2% of 373 women treated with Menopur (the side-effect table above uses a different trial group, n=434). The label says hCG must be withheld if OHSS may be developing.[1]
  • Serious pulmonary conditions and venous or arterial thromboembolic events have been reported with gonadotropins; in rare cases they have resulted in death.[1]
  • Ovarian torsion has been reported after treatment with gonadotropins.[1]
  • If the ovaries are abnormally enlarged on the last day of therapy, the label says hCG should not be given.[1]
  • Multi-fetal gestation: in the IVF clinical trial, multiple pregnancy occurred in 35.3% (n=30) of 85 total pregnancies.[1]
  • Congenital malformations after IVF or ICSI may be slightly higher than after spontaneous conception, and ectopic pregnancy may be increased. The risk of spontaneous abortion is increased with gonadotropin products; causality has not been established.[1]
  • Menopur is Pregnancy Category X and contraindicated in pregnancy. The label also notes that hCG is detected in Menopur.[1]
  • If a dose is missed, the label says not to double the next dose and to call the healthcare provider.[1]

Who the label says shouldn’t use it

The label’s contraindications are: prior hypersensitivity to Menopur or menotropins products or one of their excipients; high levels of FSH indicating primary ovarian failure; pregnancy; uncontrolled non-gonadal endocrinopathies (for example thyroid, adrenal or pituitary disorders); sex hormone dependent tumors of the reproductive tract and accessory organs; tumors of the pituitary gland or hypothalamus; abnormal uterine bleeding of undetermined origin; and ovarian cyst or enlargement of undetermined origin not due to polycystic ovary syndrome.[1]

What the label says about dosing

The label’s dosing statement for ART cycles, quoted:

“Beginning on cycle day 2 or 3, a starting dose of 225 International Units of MENOPUR is administered subcutaneously daily.”

Menopur prescribing information, Dosage and Administration[1]

The label gives that starting dose for women who have received a GnRH agonist for pituitary suppression, then adjusts it to each woman’s ovarian response.[1] Jabbit doesn’t suggest doses or timing. FAQ

What is Menopur?
Menopur is the brand name for menotropins, a purified preparation of gonadotropins with FSH and LH activity taken from the urine of postmenopausal women. Its US label approves it for developing multiple follicles and pregnancy in ovulatory women as part of an ART cycle.
Does Menopur need to be refrigerated?
Its US label says the unmixed powder may be stored refrigerated or at room temperature, 3–25°C (37–77°F), protected from light. After mixing, the label says to use it immediately and discard unused material.
What are the side effects of Menopur?
In the label’s IVF trials (n=434), the most frequent were abdominal pain (6.7%), headache (6.2%), ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (6.2%) and injection site pain and reaction (3.9%). The label’s serious risks include OHSS, ovarian torsion, thromboembolic events and multi-fetal pregnancy.
