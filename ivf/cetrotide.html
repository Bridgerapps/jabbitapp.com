Cetrorelix acetate (Cetrotide): what the US label says

Cetrorelix acetate, sold as Cetrotide, is a synthetic decapeptide with gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist activity. Its US label approves it for one use: inhibiting premature LH surges in women undergoing controlled ovarian stimulation. This page sets out what that label (revised July 2025) says about supply, storage, side effects and warnings.[1]

What the label says it is

The label says Cetrotide competes with natural GnRH for binding to membrane receptors on pituitary cells and so controls the release of LH and FSH in a dose-dependent manner.[1]

What it’s approved for in the US

Inhibition of premature LH surges in women undergoing controlled ovarian stimulation. That is the only indication on the US label.[1]

How it’s supplied

Cetrotide 0.25 mg comes as a vial that is mixed before use with the supplied diluent: 1 mL of Sterile Water for Injection in a pre-filled glass syringe, in a packaged tray. It’s given by subcutaneous injection, and the label says the solution should be used immediately after preparation.[1] Your clinic tells you how to prepare it; this page doesn’t give mixing instructions.

Does Cetrotide need to be refrigerated?

Yes, according to its US label: store it refrigerated, 2–8°C (36–46°F), and keep the packaged tray in the outer carton to protect it from light. Once mixed, use the solution immediately.[1]

Cetrorelix (Cetrotide) side effects on the label

These are rates the label reports from its clinical studies (N=949), not a forecast for any one person.[1]

Nausea1.3%
Headache1.1%
Local injection site reactions (redness, erythema, bruising, itching, swelling, pruritus)Reported

Serious risks on the label: ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, at 3.5% in clinical studies (moderate or severe, WHO grade II or III), with mild to moderate cases also reported after marketing; and hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactoid reactions, including with the first dose.[1]

Warnings on the label

  • Cetrotide should be prescribed by physicians who are experienced in fertility treatment.[1]
  • Pregnancy must be excluded before starting treatment.[1]
  • Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylactoid reactions with the first dose, have been reported. The label says special care should be taken in women with signs and symptoms of active allergic conditions, and treatment is not advised in women with severe allergic conditions.[1]
  • Enzyme elevations (ALT, AST, GGT, alkaline phosphatase) up to three times the upper limit of normal were found in 1–2% of patients during controlled ovarian stimulation; the clinical significance was not determined.[1]
  • It is not intended to be used in people aged 65 and over, and its pharmacokinetics have not been studied in renal or liver impairment, in the elderly or in children.[1]

Who the label says shouldn’t use it

The contraindications are: hypersensitivity to cetrorelix acetate, extrinsic peptide hormones or mannitol; known hypersensitivity to GnRH or any other GnRH analogs; known or suspected pregnancy, and lactation; and severe renal impairment.[1]

What the label says about dosing

“Cetrotide (cetrorelix acetate for injection) 0.25 mg may be administered subcutaneously once daily during the early- to mid-follicular phase.”

FAQ

What is Cetrotide?
Cetrotide is the brand name for cetrorelix acetate, a GnRH antagonist. Its US label approves it for inhibiting premature LH surges in women undergoing controlled ovarian stimulation.
Does Cetrotide need to be refrigerated?
Yes. Its US label says to store it refrigerated at 2–8°C (36–46°F), with the packaged tray in the outer carton to protect it from light, and to use the solution immediately after preparation.
What are Cetrotide’s side effects?
In the label’s clinical studies (N=949), nausea (1.3%) and headache (1.1%) were reported, along with local injection site reactions. The label’s serious risks are ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (3.5%, moderate or severe) and hypersensitivity reactions, including with the first dose.
Is Cetrotide the same as ganirelix?
No. Both are GnRH antagonists with nearly identical US indications, but they are different medicines with different labels: Cetrotide is mixed before use and stored refrigerated, while the ganirelix label we checked describes a ready-to-use prefilled syringe stored at room temperature.
