Trigger shot: what the US label says about choriogonadotropin alfa (Ovidrel)

The trigger shot is the one-off injection in an IVF cycle that your clinic schedules for a specific date and exact time. Choriogonadotropin alfa, sold as Ovidrel, has this use on its US label: induction of final follicular maturation and early luteinization in women in an ART program.[1] This page sets out what the Ovidrel label (revised June 2018) says, along with the US labels of two urinary hCG products, Novarel and Pregnyl. What the Ovidrel label says it is

Choriogonadotropin alfa is recombinant human chorionic gonadotropin (r-hCG) produced in genetically modified Chinese Hamster Ovary cells. The label says it “is an analogue of Luteinizing Hormone (LH) and binds to the LH/hCG receptor of the granulosa and theca cells of the ovary”, and that it “stimulates late follicular maturation and resumption of oocyte meiosis, and initiates rupture of the pre-ovulatory ovarian follicle” when there is no LH surge of the body’s own.[1]

What Ovidrel is approved for in the US

  • Induction of final follicular maturation and early luteinization in infertile women who have undergone pituitary desensitization and who have been appropriately pretreated with follicle stimulating hormones as part of an Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) program such as in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer.[1]
  • Induction of ovulation (OI) and pregnancy in anovulatory infertile patients in whom the cause of infertility is functional and not due to primary ovarian failure.[1]

How Ovidrel is supplied

Ovidrel PreFilled Syringe is a ready-to-use sterile liquid in a single-dose prefilled syringe; it isn’t mixed. The label says it “is intended for a single subcutaneous injection. Any unused material should be discarded”, and that it may be self-administered.[1]

Does Ovidrel need to be refrigerated?

Before it’s dispensed, the label says to refrigerate it at 2–8°C (36–46°F). After that, you can keep the syringe refrigerated until the expiry date, or at room temperature up to 25°C (77°F) for no more than 30 days; the label says to use it within those 30 days. Protect it from light, keep it in the original package, and discard unused material.[1]

The label’s serious risks are: ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS); ovarian enlargement; multiple births; arterial thromboembolism and thromboembolic events; allergic reactions, including anaphylactic reactions (reported after marketing); spontaneous abortion, ectopic pregnancy, premature labor and congenital abnormalities (reported after pregnancies resulting from hCG therapy in clinical studies); and adnexal torsion and hemoperitoneum (reported with menotropin therapy).[1]

Warnings on the Ovidrel label

  • It should only be used by physicians thoroughly familiar with infertility problems and their management, and requires monitoring of ovarian response with serum estradiol and transvaginal ultrasound.[1]
  • OHSS may progress rapidly to a serious medical event. In clinical trials it occurred in 1.7% of 236 patients (ART, 250 µg) and 3.0% of 99 patients (ovulation induction).[1]
  • If the ovaries are abnormally enlarged on the last day of FSH therapy, the label says choriogonadotropin alfa should not be given in that course of therapy.[1]
  • Multiple births occurred in 30.9% of 55 live deliveries in the ART studies and 13.3% of 15 live deliveries in the ovulation induction trial.[1]
  • A potential for arterial thromboembolism exists; the label says to consider the risks for women with risk factors for thromboembolic events.[1]
  • ALT elevations (up to 1.2 times the upper limit of normal) were found in 3% of patients receiving 250 µg; the clinical significance is not known.[1]
  • It may interfere with the interpretation of pregnancy tests; hCG can cross-react in radioimmunoassays of gonadotropins, especially LH.[1]

Who the Ovidrel label says shouldn’t use it

The contraindications are: prior hypersensitivity to hCG preparations or one of their excipients; primary ovarian failure; uncontrolled thyroid or adrenal dysfunction; an uncontrolled organic intracranial lesion such as a pituitary tumor; abnormal uterine bleeding of undetermined origin; ovarian cyst or enlargement of undetermined origin; sex hormone dependent tumors of the reproductive tract and accessory organs; and pregnancy.[1]

What the Ovidrel label says about dosing

“Ovidrel PreFilled Syringe 250 µg should be administered one day following the last dose of the follicle stimulating agent.”

Ovidrel prescribing information, Dosage and Administration (ART)[1]

The label adds: “Ovidrel PreFilled Syringe should not be administered until adequate follicular development is indicated by serum estradiol and vaginal ultrasonography.”[1] The date and exact time of your trigger shot come from your clinic. FAQ

When do I take my trigger shot?
At the date and exact time your clinic gives you. The Ovidrel label ties the injection to monitoring: it says the syringe should not be given until adequate follicular development is indicated by serum estradiol and vaginal ultrasonography. This page and Jabbit never work out a trigger time.
Does Ovidrel need to be refrigerated?
The label says it’s refrigerated (2–8°C) before dispensing. You can then keep it refrigerated until the expiry date, or at room temperature up to 25°C for no more than 30 days, using it within those 30 days. Protect it from light in the original package.
Is Ovidrel the same as Novarel or Pregnyl?
All three contain hCG, but their US labels differ. Ovidrel is recombinant hCG in a ready-to-use prefilled syringe given subcutaneously, and its label includes final follicular maturation in ART. Novarel and Pregnyl are vials of powder mixed with a supplied liquid, for intramuscular use only, and their US labels don’t list an ART or IVF indication.
What are the side effects of the Ovidrel trigger shot?
In the label’s ART studies (n=236), the most frequent were injection site pain (7.6%), injection site bruising (4.7%), post-operative pain (4.7%) and abdominal pain (4.2%). Its serious risks include OHSS, ovarian enlargement, multiple births and arterial thromboembolism.
