Ganirelix acetate injection: what the US label says

Ganirelix acetate is a synthetic decapeptide that blocks gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH). Its US label approves it for one use: inhibiting premature LH surges in women undergoing controlled ovarian hyperstimulation. This page sets out what Organon’s Ganirelix Acetate Injection label (revised September 2026) says about supply, storage, side effects and warnings.[1]

What the label says it is

The label describes ganirelix as having high antagonistic activity against naturally occurring GnRH. It acts by competitively blocking the GnRH receptors on the pituitary gonadotroph, causing a rapid, reversible suppression of gonadotropin secretion, with LH suppressed more than FSH.[1]

What it’s approved for in the US

Inhibition of premature LH surges in women undergoing controlled ovarian hyperstimulation. That is the only indication on the label.[1]

How it’s supplied

A ready-to-use aqueous solution, 250 mcg/0.5 mL, in a single-dose prefilled 1 mL glass syringe with a 27 gauge × 1/2 inch needle. It isn’t mixed, it’s for subcutaneous use only, and the label says to use each prefilled syringe only once.[1] The label we checked carries no separate brand name. If your ganirelix comes from a different labeler, follow the label that came with it.

Does ganirelix need to be refrigerated?

The label we checked doesn’t call for refrigeration. It says to store it at 25°C (77°F), with excursions permitted to 15–30°C (59–86°F), and to protect it from light.[1]

Ganirelix side effects on the label

These are rates the label reports from its controlled clinical studies (N=794), not a forecast for any one person.[1]

The label also lists ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (2.4% in controlled clinical studies); hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis (including anaphylactic shock), angioedema and urticaria, as early as the first dose (reported after marketing); and fetal death (3.7% in its adverse-event table from controlled clinical studies).[1]

Warnings on the label

  • Ganirelix should be prescribed by physicians who are experienced in infertility treatment.[1]
  • Pregnancy must be excluded before starting treatment.[1]
  • Hypersensitivity reactions, both generalized and local, including anaphylaxis, angioedema and urticaria, have been reported, as early as with the first dose. The label says that if a hypersensitivity reaction is suspected, ganirelix should be discontinued.[1]
  • Special care should be taken in women with signs and symptoms of active allergic conditions.[1]
  • Laboratory findings: neutrophil counts of 8.3 × 109/L or more in 11.9% of treated subjects, and downward shifts in hematocrit and total bilirubin; the clinical significance was not determined.[1]
  • It should not be used by lactating women.[1]

Who the label says shouldn’t use it

The contraindications are: known hypersensitivity to ganirelix acetate or any of its components; known hypersensitivity to GnRH or any other GnRH analog; and known or suspected pregnancy.[1]

What the label says about dosing

“After initiating FSH therapy on Day 2 or 3 of the cycle, Ganirelix Acetate Injection 250 mcg may be administered subcutaneously once daily during the mid to late portion of the follicular phase.”

FAQ

What is ganirelix?
Ganirelix acetate is a GnRH antagonist. Its US label approves it for inhibiting premature LH surges in women undergoing controlled ovarian hyperstimulation. The label we checked supplies it as a ready-to-use 250 mcg/0.5 mL prefilled syringe.
Does ganirelix need to be refrigerated?
The Organon label we checked says to store it at 25°C (77°F), with excursions permitted to 15–30°C (59–86°F), protected from light. It doesn’t call for refrigeration. Follow the label that came with your supply.
What are ganirelix’s side effects?
In the label’s controlled clinical studies (N=794), the most frequent were gynecological abdominal pain (4.8%), headache (3.0%) and vaginal bleeding (1.8%). The label also lists OHSS (2.4%) and hypersensitivity reactions as early as the first dose.
Is ganirelix the same as Cetrotide?
No. Both are GnRH antagonists with nearly identical US indications, but they are different medicines with different labels, supply and storage. See our side-by-side page.
