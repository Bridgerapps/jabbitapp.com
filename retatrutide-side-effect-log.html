What this page is (and isn’t)

This is a logging template. It doesn’t tell you what to take, when to change dose, or how to treat symptoms. It helps you capture a clean timeline so you can spot patterns (or confirm there aren’t any).

What to log each day

  • Symptom (nausea, reflux, constipation, fatigue, headache, mood, sleep)
  • Severity (0–10) and duration
  • Injection timestamp (and the day-of-week rhythm)
  • Food + hydration notes (big changes only)
  • Other changes (travel, illness, missed dose, new meds/supplements, alcohol)
  • What helped (if anything) — purely observational

How to spot “timeline” patterns (without overfitting)

Many people notice a weekly rhythm with once-weekly meds: a day (or two) that feels different than the rest. Logging helps you answer:

  • Do symptoms cluster 0–48h after injection?
  • Do symptoms show up on day 3–5 instead?
  • Are symptoms worse after dose changes or missed doses?
  • Do they line up with travel, stress, or sleep disruption?

Build a week-cycle view around each injection

A calendar date alone can hide a repeating pattern. Group observations by their distance from the injection so each week can be compared on the same frame. This is a record-keeping workflow, not a prediction of when symptoms should occur.

  • Injection event: record the date and exact time in your retatrutide injection tracker.
  • 0–24 hours: note new symptoms, severity, duration, hydration, meals, sleep, and anything unusual.
  • 24–72 hours: keep the same symptom labels and 0–10 scale, including a zero when nothing happened.
  • Days 4–7: continue short daily check-ins so late-week changes are visible instead of being remembered only at the next injection.
  • Next injection: start a new cycle without overwriting the prior week.

A useful post-injection symptom entry

Keep each entry factual enough that two weeks can be compared. A compact entry can read: “36 hours after injection — nausea 4/10 — lasted 90 minutes — slept 6 hours — no other routine change.” If fatigue, anxiety, or wearable changes matter to you, log them with the same timestamp rather than assuming they came from the injection.

Compare patterns, not isolated days: after several cycles, review the same post-injection window side by side. Look for repetition, exceptions, and competing context such as illness, travel, stress, alcohol, or a schedule change.

What to bring to a clinician conversation

  • The injection timestamp and the first symptom timestamp.
  • Severity, duration, and whether the symptom fully resolved.
  • Several complete week cycles, including symptom-free days.
  • Missed or shifted routine events recorded in the retatrutide missed-dose tracker.
  • Relevant context such as illness, hydration changes, sleep disruption, or other medication changes.

A log cannot establish cause or replace medical evaluation. It can make the sequence clearer and reduce reliance on memory.

Quick checklist

  • Log daily (even “0” days).
  • Write down big changes (travel, illness, missed dose).
  • Keep notes short and factual.
  • Bring the log to appointments.

When to treat it as urgent

If you have severe symptoms, signs of dehydration, fainting, chest pain, trouble breathing, or anything that feels dangerous, treat that as urgent and seek appropriate care.
