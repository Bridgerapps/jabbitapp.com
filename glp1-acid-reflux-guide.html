Managing heartburn and gastroesophageal reflux on Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and Zepbound

Acid reflux and GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease) are among the most commonly reported side effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro, Zepbound). If you're experiencing heartburn, regurgitation, or stomach acid traveling upward after your injections, you're not alone—and there are proven strategies to find relief.

Quick Answer: GLP-1 medications relax the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), slowing digestion and increasing stomach pressure. This makes acid reflux more likely. Management includes dietary adjustments, timing strategies, over-the-counter medications, and lifestyle changes.

Why GLP-1 Medications Cause Acid Reflux

GLP-1 receptor agonists work by:

  • Delaying gastric emptying — food stays in your stomach longer, increasing pressure
  • Reducing lower esophageal sphincter tone — the muscle that keeps stomach acid from rising becomes more relaxed
  • Increasing stomach acid production — some users report more acid overall

These mechanisms are intentional—they contribute to feelings of fullness and reduced appetite—but they can cause uncomfortable reflux symptoms, especially:

  • In the first few weeks of treatment (as your body adjusts)
  • When increasing to a higher dose
  • When eating large meals or lying down after eating
  • With certain trigger foods

Immediate Relief Strategies

1. Timing and Posture Adjustments

  • Wait 2-3 hours after eating before lying down
  • Elevate your head while sleeping — use an extra pillow or bed wedge
  • Don't eat within 3 hours of your injection
  • Stay upright for at least 30 minutes after meals

2. Dietary Triggers to Avoid

  • Citrus fruits and juices
  • Tomato-based foods (sauce, ketchup)
  • Spicy or fatty foods
  • Chocolate and mint
  • Caffeine and carbonated beverages
  • Alcohol (especially wine)
  • Large meals — stick to smaller portions

3. Over-the-Counter Solutions

  • Antacids (Tums, Rolaids, Maalox) — neutralize stomach acid quickly for immediate relief
  • H2 blockers (Pepcid, Zantac) — reduce acid production; take before meals for prevention
  • PPIs (Prilosec, Nexium) — stronger acid suppression; consult your provider for long-term use
  • Alginates (Gaviscon) — create a protective foam barrier against reflux
Pro tip: Many users find that taking an H2 blocker (like Pepcid) 30 minutes before their GLP-1 injection prevents reflux throughout the day.

When to Talk to Your Provider

Seek medical advice if you experience:
  • Reflux symptoms more than 2x per week
  • Symptoms not relieved by OTC medications
  • Difficulty swallowing or pain when swallowing
  • Persistent nausea or vomiting
  • Unexplained weight loss
  • Symptoms waking you up at night
  • Chest pain (to rule out cardiac issues)

Your provider may recommend:

  • Temporarily lowering your dose
  • Switching to a different GLP-1 medication
  • Prescription-strength acid suppressants
  • Further evaluation for hiatal hernia or other conditions

Long-Term Management

Lifestyle Changes That Help

  • Weight management — even modest weight loss can reduce reflux
  • Smoking cessation — nicotine relaxes the LES
  • Loose-fitting clothing — tight waistbands increase stomach pressure
  • Stress management — stress can worsen reflux symptoms
  • Chewing gum — stimulates saliva production, which neutralizes acid

Supplements That May Help

  • Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) — soothes the esophageal lining
  • Melatonin — some studies show it can help with nighttime reflux
  • Probiotics — may improve gut motility and reduce symptoms
  • Magnesium — helps relax the LES in some users
Important: Always consult your healthcare provider before starting any new supplement, especially if you're on other medications.

What About Continuing Your GLP-1 Medication?

Mild to moderate reflux is often manageable with the strategies above. However, if acid reflux significantly impacts your quality of life or doesn't respond to treatment, discuss these options with your provider:

  • Temporary dose reduction while your body adjusts
  • Switching from weekly to more frequent, lower-dose injections
  • Exploring alternative medications
  • Adding a maintenance proton pump inhibitor (PPI)

For many users, reflux symptoms improve significantly after the first 4-8 weeks as their body adapts to the medication. Track your symptoms to identify patterns and share this data with your provider.
