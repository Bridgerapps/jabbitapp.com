GLP-1 bloodwork: what the labels actually say to monitor

There is no single label-defined “GLP-1 blood panel” for everyone. Current Wegovy and Zepbound labels call for blood-glucose monitoring in people with diabetes and targeted evaluation when specific risks or symptoms arise. The right lab plan depends on why the medicine is used, diabetes status, other medicines, symptoms, and the prescriber’s plan. [1] [2]

Labels set triggers, not one universal panel

The labels separate ongoing diabetes monitoring from evaluation prompted by a risk, adverse reaction, or clinical suspicion. Other labs may matter because of a person’s underlying conditions or other medicines, but these sources do not establish a one-size-fits-all GLP-1 panel.

Why A1C timing is diabetes-specific

A1C reflects average glycemia over about two to three months. ADA 2026 diabetes-care guidance recommends assessing glycemic status at least twice yearly, and more often—for example, every three months—for people not at goals or with factors such as recent treatment changes, frequent or severe hypo- or hyperglycemia, or changes in health status. [3]

That is diabetes-care guidance, not a universal schedule for every person using a GLP-1 medicine. The individual plan still depends on the reason for treatment and the prescriber’s goals.

The clean distinction

Routine follow-up follows the person’s diabetes plan. Kidney or gallbladder evaluation is tied to the label’s specific risks, reactions, or suspicions.

Tracking supplies context; it does not interpret a result. A clear record can keep the following information together for a prescriber:

  • Indication and exact medication or product name
  • Clinician-directed schedule and documented changes
  • Injection dates and symptom timing
  • Weight or progress trend
  • Concurrent glucose-lowering medicines
  • Lab dates and results copied exactly
  • Questions to bring to the prescriber

Five questions to bring to a prescriber

  1. What is the goal of each lab in my plan?
  2. Is there a baseline result we are comparing against?
  3. What symptom or change would trigger an earlier check?
  4. How do my other glucose-lowering medicines change the monitoring plan?
  5. What result should prompt me to contact you?

Frequently asked questions

Is there a standard GLP-1 blood panel?

No single panel is defined for everyone in the current Wegovy and Zepbound labels. Monitoring depends on diabetes status, other medicines, symptoms, risks, and the prescriber’s plan. [1] [2]

Do Wegovy and Zepbound require kidney tests for everyone?

The labels do not establish routine kidney testing for everyone. They call for renal-function monitoring when adverse reactions could lead to volume depletion, especially during initiation and escalation. [1] [2]

Should calcitonin be checked routinely?

Both labels state that routine serum calcitonin or thyroid-ultrasound monitoring is of uncertain value for early detection of medullary thyroid carcinoma. [1] [2]

What do higher amylase or lipase levels mean?

Both labels report increases and say their clinical significance is unknown without other signs or symptoms of pancreatitis. An isolated result is not self-interpreting. [1] [2]

How often is A1C checked?

ADA diabetes-care guidance recommends assessing glycemic status at least twice yearly, and more often—for example, every three months—for people not at goals or with factors such as recent treatment changes. This is diabetes guidance, not a universal GLP-1 schedule. [3]
