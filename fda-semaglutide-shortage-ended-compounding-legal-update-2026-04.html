Is the semaglutide shortage over? What FDA and the courts have said

Yes. FDA determined on February 21, 2025 that the shortage of semaglutide injection products (the drug in Ozempic and Wegovy) was resolved.[1] On August 27, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit affirmed a lower-court judgment that upheld that decision, in a case brought by a compounders’ trade group and a compounding pharmacy.[2] As of October 29, 2026, FDA’s shortage data list no semaglutide injection or tirzepatide product in shortage.[3][5]

Timeline, from FDA’s own updates and the court record

DateWhat happened
Oct 2, 2024FDA determined the tirzepatide injection (Mounjaro, Zepbound) shortage was resolved. On Oct 22, 2024 FDA said that decision had been remanded to it for re-evaluation as part of litigation.[1]
Dec 19, 2024After re-evaluating, FDA again determined the tirzepatide shortage was resolved.[1]
Feb 21, 2025FDA determined the semaglutide injection shortage, in place since 2022, was resolved, after confirming with the manufacturer that supply “can meet the present and projected national demand.” FDA added that patients “may still see intermittent and limited localized supply disruptions.”[1] The Outsourcing Facilities Association, a compounders’ trade group, and North American Custom Laboratories sued; Pharmacy Times reported that the group called the decision “reckless and arbitrary.”[7]
Apr 24, 2025The district court in Texas denied the trade group’s request for a preliminary injunction (OFA v. FDA, No. 4:25-cv-00174). FDA said its grace period for state-licensed pharmacies and physicians (section 503A) had ended, and that outsourcing facilities (503B) had until May 22, 2025.[1]
Jun 13, 2025The district court granted summary judgment to FDA and to Novo Nordisk, which had joined the case on FDA’s side, and entered final judgment; the trade group appealed.[2]
Apr 1, 2026FDA reminded compounders of the conditions for the 503A and 503B exemptions and said: “Tirzepatide and semaglutide do not currently appear on the 503B bulks list or on FDA’s drug shortage list.”[1]
Aug 27, 2026The Fifth Circuit affirmed, in an unpublished opinion (revised Sept 1, 2026). It said any error by FDA in not using notice-and-comment procedures “was harmless, and the FDA’s ultimate action was not arbitrary or capricious.” The same opinion says the court affirmed the parallel tirzepatide case (Eli Lilly) the same day.[2] We have not tracked any further appeal.

What FDA’s shortage data show now (October 29, 2026)

  • Semaglutide: the only records are three Rybelsus tablet strengths (3, 7 and 14 mg), marked “To Be Discontinued” and first posted June 4, 2026. The record says: “These products will be replaced by Ozempic (semaglutide) tablets.” That is a discontinuation notice, not a shortage of Ozempic or Wegovy injections.[3]
  • Tirzepatide and dulaglutide: no records in the same data.[3]
  • Liraglutide (Victoza, Saxenda and generics): 9 of 11 records still carry the status “Current,” and 2 are marked “To Be Discontinued.”[4]

These counts come from openFDA, which says its results are unvalidated; FDA’s Drug Shortages Database is the official list and can change any day.[5]

What “shortage over” means for compounded semaglutide

The shortage list matters because of two compounding rules FDA restated on April 1, 2026:[1]

  • Outsourcing facilities (503B) may compound from bulk ingredient only if the ingredient is on FDA’s 503B bulks list or the drug is on the shortage list at the time. FDA says semaglutide and tirzepatide are on neither.[1]
  • State-licensed pharmacies and physicians (503A) may not compound “essentially a copy” of a commercially available drug “regularly or in inordinate amounts,” unless a prescriber documents a change that makes a significant difference for an identified patient. FDA’s example: semaglutide plus vitamin B12 in an injection can still count as a copy when the strengths are within 10% of the approved products. FDA says it does not intend to take action against a compounder that fills four or fewer prescriptions of such a product in a calendar month.[1]

FDA also states that compounded drugs are not FDA approved and do not go through its premarket review for safety, effectiveness and quality.[1] For FDA’s 2026 actions on how compounded GLP-1s are marketed, see our explainers on FDA’s February and March 2026 statements, the 30 telehealth warning letters and the compounded “Wegovy pill”. For where bulk semaglutide powder comes from, see our check of FDA’s own registration files.

Not the same as the peptide policy: HHS’s draft enforcement-discretion policy filed October 28, 2026 names six peptides (BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c, semax and TB-500). Semaglutide and tirzepatide are not among them.[6] Details: our policy explainer.

Our reading: for most people the court cases change little day to day. The shortage has been over since February 2025 and both courts have now sided with FDA. FDA itself said local pharmacies may still see short gaps, so a shortage listing doesn’t tell you whether your pharmacy has your dose this week. Ask your pharmacist and prescriber about that, and don’t switch products based on a web page.

The practical checklist: what to track when access changes

Think of this like a small “refill incident report.” You’re not trying to build a case or prove anything, you’re trying to reduce confusion and speed up the next step.

1) Your medication timeline (keep it boring and factual)

  • Medication name and form: injection, tablets (if applicable), etc.
  • Current dose and dosing day: plus the date you last took it (or last had it available).
  • Start date / stop date: if anything changed recently, record the exact date.
  • Lot/packaging details (optional): photos of label/box if you already have them.

2) Pharmacy attempt log (this is the big one)

  • Date/time of each attempt or call
  • Pharmacy name + location (or mail order name)
  • Outcome: filled, partial fill, backorder, “can order”, “cannot order”, transfer required
  • What they said they need: new Rx, diagnosis code, prior auth, insurance info, stock check date
  • Next action + who owns it: you, prescriber, pharmacy, insurer

3) Paperwork folder (save once, reuse repeatedly)

  • Prescription details: Rx date, prescriber name, pharmacy it was sent to, refills remaining
  • Insurance artifacts: prior authorization letters, denial reasons, appeal submissions (if any)
  • Receipts and out-of-pocket quotes: screenshots are fine, just include date/time
  • Portal messages: screenshots/PDFs of instructions from the care team

4) “What changed” summary (two sentences)

Write a tiny summary you can paste into a portal message:

  • Before: “I was filling X at Y pharmacy without issues through DATE.”
  • After: “On DATE, they said Z, and I was told I need A/B (or that they can’t fill).”

Questions to ask (copy/paste)

1) What’s the next step if my usual pharmacy can’t fill?
Should the prescription be transferred, resent, or changed? Who should I contact first?
2) What paperwork will your team need to move faster?
Prior auth status, diagnosis code needs, or specific form requirements.
3) If there’s a gap, what should I track day-to-day?
Symptoms, side effects, weight or other markers, and what would be a “call sooner” threshold.
4) Can we agree on a simple plan and a date to reassess?
So you’re not stuck in a loop of calls without a clear checkpoint.
