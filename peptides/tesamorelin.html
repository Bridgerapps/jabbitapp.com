FDA label, pivotal trials and FDA letters, checked October 24, 2026

Published February 14, 2026 · Updated October 24, 2026

Is tesamorelin a weight-loss drug?

No. The current label says tesamorelin is for reducing excess abdominal fat in adults with HIV-associated lipodystrophy—and specifically says it is not indicated for weight-loss management because it is weight neutral. [1]

The short answer

“Reduces abdominal fat” and “is a weight-loss drug” are not interchangeable claims. Tesamorelin’s labeled indication is narrow: excess abdominal fat in adults with HIV-associated lipodystrophy. The label explicitly excludes weight-loss management, and its limitation statement says long-term cardiovascular safety has not been established. [1]

What approval does—and does not—mean

FDA approval supports the labeled use in the labeled population. It does not establish tesamorelin for general obesity, bodybuilding, anti-aging, or cosmetic body recomposition. [1]

Visceral fat was the endpoint—not scale weight

The Phase 3 program used 26-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled studies, with visceral adipose tissue measured by CT as the primary outcome. The completed trial record for NCT00123253—also identified by the alias NCT00435136—likewise lists visceral adipose tissue as its primary outcome. [1] [3]

That distinction matters. A CT scan can quantify a specific internal fat compartment; a scale reports total body weight. Evidence for change in one endpoint should not be relabeled as evidence for another. The label’s own weight-neutral limitation reinforces that boundary. [1]

What the pivotal randomized trial actually showed

412: participants with HIV and abdominal fat accumulation

−15.2%: change in CT-measured visceral adipose tissue in the tesamorelin arm

+5.0%: change in the placebo arm

In the 26-week trial reported by Falutz and colleagues, participants were randomized to tesamorelin or placebo and the primary endpoint was CT-measured visceral adipose tissue. More participants receiving tesamorelin withdrew because of an adverse event. The abstract reported no significant between-group difference in glycemic measures during this trial. [2]

The defensible reading is specific: the trial showed a visceral-fat result in adults with HIV and abdominal fat accumulation. It did not establish tesamorelin as a general weight-loss treatment. [2] [1]

Mechanism is not proof of every marketed use

Tesamorelin is a growth hormone–releasing factor/hormone analog. It stimulates endogenous growth hormone and increases IGF-1. That mechanism helps explain why the label calls for attention to IGF-1, but a biological mechanism does not extend an approval beyond the population and outcome actually studied. [1]

Safety and evidence boundaries

The current label’s warnings and precautions address malignancy, elevated IGF-1, fluid retention, glucose intolerance or diabetes, and hypersensitivity. Its contraindications include active malignancy, pregnancy, disruption of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, and hypersensitivity. These are reasons to treat the current label and prescriber-led monitoring as central—not as fine print. [1]

This page is educational and does not provide dosing, timing, or treatment instructions. Questions about whether tesamorelin fits a person’s situation, and every schedule decision, belong with the prescriber and current product labeling.

What the label says about stopping, IGF-1, blood sugar and antibodies

The benefit lasts only while the drug is taken. In the trials’ extension phases, visceral fat rose 22% and 16% over 26 weeks in patients switched from tesamorelin to placebo, and the second trial’s authors wrote that the gains were “rapidly lost.” [1] [4]

Tesamorelin raises IGF-1, and often above the normal range: at 26 weeks, 47% of patients had IGF-1 more than 2 standard deviations above normal and 36% more than 3. The label says “the effects of prolonged elevations in IGF-1 levels are unknown” and calls for monitoring. New diabetes-range blood sugar (HbA1c of 6.5% or more) occurred in 5% of patients versus 1% on placebo. [1]

Half of patients developed antibodies to tesamorelin by 26 weeks. In about 60% of those, the antibodies also reacted with the body’s own growth hormone–releasing hormone, and at week 52, 10% had antibodies that neutralized tesamorelin and 5% had antibodies that neutralized natural GHRH in lab tests. [1] That matters for unapproved GHRH analogs such as CJC-1295, which have no comparable data; see our CJC-1295 and ipamorelin teardown.

Can you get tesamorelin from a compounding pharmacy or a “research” seller?

Not through the routes FDA recognizes, by FDA’s own documents. FDA approved tesamorelin as Egrifta on November 10, 2010 (application 022505). On March 23, 2020, it was one of the approved products that federal law moved to biologic status: FDA’s list of applications “deemed to be BLAs” (biologics licenses) that day includes tesamorelin acetate, Egrifta. [5]

That change matters for compounding. FDA’s guidance on biological products says the compounding exemptions in sections 503A and 503B of the drug law cover the drug-approval requirement, but “they do not provide an exemption from the requirement to obtain premarket approval under section 351 of the PHS Act,” which is the biologics license. [6] Our reading of those two documents together, which is analysis and not legal advice, is that the usual compounding-pharmacy route does not cover tesamorelin.

Online sellers that label tesamorelin “for research use only” are a separate case. In at least five warning letters dated August 24, 2026, FDA said products sold online as tesamorelin, including one sold as a tesamorelin and ipamorelin blend, are unapproved new drugs. FDA wrote that despite the “research use only” or “not for human” labels, evidence from the sellers’ websites, including selling bacteriostatic water for mixing the products, showed they were intended for human use. [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] Those are FDA’s findings in letters; a warning letter is not a court ruling. Egrifta’s approval covers Egrifta, not these products.

Tesamorelin is not approved in the European Union. The company withdrew its application on June 21, 2012, and the European Medicines Agency says that at the time its committee “was of the provisional opinion that Egrifta could not have been approved.” [12]

What is still being studied: a randomized trial called TRIUMPH (not related to Lilly’s retatrutide trials of the same name) is testing tesamorelin or placebo, both added to home exercise, in 100 older adults with HIV, looking at physical function. ClinicalTrials.gov lists it as recruiting, with main results expected around June 2028, so there are no results yet. [13] [14]

Frequently asked questions

Is tesamorelin FDA-approved for weight loss?

No. The current label says it is not indicated for weight-loss management and describes it as weight neutral. [1]

What is tesamorelin approved for?

It is indicated to reduce excess abdominal fat in adults with HIV-associated lipodystrophy. [1]

Did the pivotal trial show weight loss?

The trial was designed around CT-measured visceral adipose tissue, not general weight loss. It does not establish tesamorelin as a weight-loss treatment. [2] [1]

Does FDA approval validate anti-aging or bodybuilding use?

No. Approval for the labeled population and indication does not establish general obesity, bodybuilding, anti-aging, or cosmetic-recomposition use. [1]

Can a compounding pharmacy make tesamorelin?

Since March 23, 2020, FDA has treated Egrifta (tesamorelin) as a biologic, and FDA’s guidance says the compounding exemptions in sections 503A and 503B do not exempt products from the biologics license requirement. Our reading, which is not legal advice, is that the usual compounding route does not cover tesamorelin. [5] [6]

Is “research use only” tesamorelin the same as Egrifta?

No. In warning letters dated August 24, 2026, FDA called products sold online as tesamorelin unapproved new drugs, despite their “research use only” labels. Egrifta’s approval does not extend to them. [7] [8] [9] [10] [11]
