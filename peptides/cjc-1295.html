CJC-1295: what the research shows

CJC-1295 is a long-acting lab-made analog of growth hormone–releasing hormone (GHRH), invented by ConjuChem. It reached early human trials, but it is not an FDA-approved drug, and FDA’s compounding advisers voted against it in 2024.[1][9][4]

Two versions under one name

The version tested in people, CJC-1295 with DAC, carries a chemical hook that binds to albumin in the blood, which gives it a half-life of 5.8 to 8.1 days.[1] Most products sold for stacking are CJC-1295 without DAC (“Mod GRF 1-29”), the same 29 amino acids without the hook. FDA found no study establishing whether that version is pharmacologically active.[3] In ConjuChem’s patent it was used as the comparison peptide, and in rats it was undetectable in blood within 60 minutes.[6]

Human trial data

Two randomized, placebo-controlled trials (28 and 49 days) in healthy adults aged 21–61 gave CJC-1295 with DAC under the skin. A single injection raised mean growth hormone 2- to 10-fold for 6 days or more and IGF-1 1.5- to 3-fold for 9–11 days.[1] A follow-up study found that normal growth hormone pulses persisted on top of the raised baseline.[2] FDA counts 63 healthy adults in the published studies, 73% of whom got one injection. Side effects were common: in the first trial, 33 of 35 people on the drug reported one, including injection-site reactions in about 70%, headache in 63% and flushing, warmth and brief drops in blood pressure in 30%.[3]

The stopped trial

ConjuChem’s only trial in patients, in 192 people with HIV-related fat changes, was stopped in July 2006 after a participant had a fatal heart attack about two hours after his 11th weekly dose. The trial doctor thought it was likely unrelated to the drug. No results were posted or published.[7][3][8]

Regulatory status

No FDA-approved drug contains CJC-1295.[9] In December 2024 FDA’s compounding advisers voted 0 to 13 against four forms and 1 to 12 against the fifth, for growth hormone deficiency.[4] FDA lists it among substances previously in category 2 whose nominations were withdrawn and says it “has identified serious adverse events associated with CJC-1295 including increased heart rate and systemic vasodilatory reaction.”[5] It is not on the agenda for FDA’s 2026–2027 peptide advisory meetings.[11] An FDA peptide compounding policy went to the White House for review on October 14, 2026; its text and the peptides it covers aren’t public.[10]

What is not known

There are no trials in people with any disease, no long-term safety data and no carcinogenicity studies, and the no-DAC version has never been studied in a person.[3] No study has tested it combined with ipamorelin. For the full paper trail and how it compares with tesamorelin, see our teardown.
