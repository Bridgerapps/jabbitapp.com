MOTS-c: what the research shows

MOTS-c is a 16-amino-acid peptide encoded in the mitochondrial 12S rRNA gene. It is not an FDA-approved drug, and FDA reports no human exposure data for it by any route.[1][3][4]

What MOTS-c is

MOTS-c was described in 2015 as a mitochondrial-derived peptide that acts mainly in skeletal muscle, inhibiting the folate cycle and activating AMPK. In male mice on a high-fat diet, 0.5 mg/kg a day by injection for eight weeks prevented obesity without changing food intake.[1] In 2021 the same group reported that exercise raised MOTS-c in human muscle and blood, and that 15 mg/kg improved running in mice; a late-life treatment showed only a non-significant lifespan trend (P = 0.23).[2]

Regulatory status

FDA lists MOTS-c among substances previously placed in category 2 of its compounding interim policies whose nominations were withdrawn, stating it “has not identified any human exposure data” for products containing it.[4] FDA’s 2026 review found no in-vivo pharmacokinetic or toxicity studies and recommended against adding it to the compounding list.[3] In July 2026 the advisory committee voted 7 to 5, with 2 abstentions, in favor.[8] MOTS-c is on the World Anti-Doping Agency’s 2026 prohibited list.[9]

Human data

Published human studies measure the body’s own MOTS-c; none we found gave MOTS-c to people.[7] The gene variant m.1382A>C (K14Q) was linked to more type 2 diabetes in less active Japanese men and not to exceptional longevity.[5] CohBar’s modified analog CB4211 lowered liver enzymes over four weeks in 20 people with fatty liver, but liver fat fell similarly on placebo and injection-site reactions were common (79% vs 33%).[6]

What is not known

There are no published human trials of MOTS-c itself, no human pharmacokinetic data, and no established human dose. In human blood in a test tube it is quickly cut into shorter fragments.[3] For the full paper trail, see our teardown below.
