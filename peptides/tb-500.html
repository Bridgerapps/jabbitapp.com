TB-500: what the research shows

TB-500 is sold as a synthetic version of a short region of thymosin beta-4 (N-acetylated LKKTETQ). It is not an FDA-approved drug, and FDA reports no human exposure data for it.[1][2][3]

What TB-500 is

Doping-control laboratories identified the N-acetylated 17–23 fragment of human thymosin beta-4 (Ac-LKKTETQ) as the key ingredient in TB-500 products.[1] The LKKTETQ segment is the region of thymosin beta-4 associated with actin binding and cell migration; TB-500 was described as a veterinary preparation, and racing laboratories have developed assays to detect it in horses.[4]

Regulatory status

No FDA-approved drug product contains TB-500 or thymosin beta-4; a Drugs@FDA search returns no approved application.[2] FDA lists “Thymosin beta-4, fragment (LKKTETQ), also known as TB-500” among substances previously placed in category 2 of its compounding interim policies whose nominations were withdrawn. FDA states it “has not identified any human exposure data” for drug products containing the fragment and flags aggregation, impurities, and immunogenicity risk.[3]

Update, October 28, 2026: HHS made public a draft enforcement-discretion policy that lists TB-500 among six peptides (the others are BPC-157, epitalon, KPV, MOTS-c and semax). HHS says it does not intend to take enforcement action against state-licensed pharmacies, federal facilities or licensed physicians that compound these peptides under 10 conditions.[8] For TB-500, Appendix B names TB-500 acetate, for subcutaneous or intramuscular injection only.[8] The conditions include bulk powder from an FDA-registered facility with a certificate of analysis, one listed active ingredient per product, reporting of serious adverse events and a patient-specific prescription.[8] HHS calls the document a draft, open to comment on regulations.gov (Docket No. HHS-OASH-2026-0496) until 2 weeks after its October 30, 2026 Federal Register publication (November 13, by our count), and says it is not binding and may be revisited if a safety signal or quality problem arises.[8] This is not an FDA approval: compounded drugs are not reviewed by FDA for safety or effectiveness, and the policy records that FDA’s drug center recommended against listing TB-500.[8] For what it does and doesn’t change, see our FDA peptide policy explainer. Full-length thymosin beta-4 is a different molecule

Human trials have studied the full 43-amino-acid thymosin beta-4, not TB-500. In a phase 1 study of 40 healthy volunteers, intravenous thymosin beta-4 at 42–1,260 mg daily for 14 days had infrequent, mild-to-moderate adverse events and no dose-limiting toxicity.[5] A phase 2 study of a topical thymosin beta-4 gel in 73 patients with venous ulcers found safety comparable to placebo and suggested a possible healing effect at one dose.[6] These results cannot be transferred to a 7-amino-acid fragment.

What are the side effects of TB-500?

Short answer: they are not established. We found no published human study of TB-500 itself. FDA says it “has not identified any human exposure data” for drug products containing the fragment and “lacks important information regarding any safety issues raised by this drug, including whether it would cause harm if administered to humans.” The risk FDA names is immunogenicity (an immune reaction) for certain routes of administration, because of possible aggregation and peptide-related impurities.[3]

The “thymosin beta-4 side effects” often quoted online come from studies of the full-length 43-amino-acid molecule, not TB-500: adverse events were “infrequent, and mild or moderate in intensity” in a 40-person intravenous study in healthy volunteers,[5] and a topical gel’s safety was “comparable to placebo” in 73 people with venous ulcers.[6] Both were small, short studies of a different molecule, so in our view they say little about an injected 7-amino-acid fragment. A 2026 Sports Medicine review of approved and unapproved peptides, which covers TB-500, says “rigorous human safety data are scarce.”[7]

What is not known

There are no published controlled human trials of TB-500 itself, no human pharmacokinetic data, and no established human dose. A 2026 review of gray-market sports peptides lists TB-500 among unapproved compounds with scarce human safety data.[7]
