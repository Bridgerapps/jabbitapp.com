Retatrutide: what the trials show, side effects, and the “food noise” question

Retatrutide is Eli Lilly’s investigational once-weekly shot that acts on three hormone receptors (GIP, GLP-1 and glucagon). It is not FDA approved.[1] Five phase 3 trials have now reported. In the four longer obesity trials (68 to 80 weeks), average weight loss at the top dose ran from about 19% in people with type 2 diabetes to about 29%, depending on the trial and how it is counted, and Lilly plans to file for approval in the first quarter of 2027.[10] The side effects to know about are stomach problems, skin-sensation changes (dysesthesia) and, in some trials, low blood pressure.

Where retatrutide stands (October 2026)

  • Not approved anywhere. Drugs@FDA lists no application.[1] Lilly says it is finishing the manufacturing (CMC) data package and plans to submit a Biologics License Application in Q1 2027, and that retatrutide “cannot be legally sold or marketed for human use.”[10]
  • A regulatory fight is still open. FDA said again on October 2, 2026 that it considers retatrutide a drug rather than a biologic, part of an ongoing dispute with Lilly over which kind of application applies. It is not a rejection of the drug.[18]
  • There is a legal access route, but it is narrow. Since June 5, 2026, Lilly has listed a single-patient expanded-access program for adults with a BMI of 35 or more, at least two serious obesity-related complications, no success on the highest available dose of an approved weight drug, and no way to join a trial.[16]

Does retatrutide get rid of “food noise”?

This is the question going around X this week, where users describe food as “just fuel,” no cravings, and sharper focus within weeks.[20] The plain answer: the drug clearly cuts hunger and the urge to overeat, but “food noise” itself has never been measured, and the focus claims are untested.

What the trials did measure. In the 338-person phase 2 obesity trial, people filled out the 51-item Eating Inventory at weeks 24 and 48. Hunger and “disinhibition” (losing control of eating when stressed or tempted) fell more on every retatrutide dose than on placebo. Deliberate restraint went up in every group, placebo included, with no difference between them. People who lost the most weight also reported the biggest drops in hunger and disinhibition.[11] A phase 2 trial in 275 adults with type 2 diabetes found the same thing on appetite rating scales at doses of 4 mg and up.[3][12] In exit interviews with 40 phase 2 participants, 31 of the 36 who got retatrutide said their eating changed within the first 8 weeks, and 25 described feeling happy.[13]

What is missing. These are exploratory, Lilly-run analyses of a few hundred people, and the p-values were not adjusted for multiple comparisons.[11] There is no standard scale for “food noise.” Lilly built a new eating questionnaire (the EBAQ) partly from those same interviews and validated it in a 120-person online survey, but we found no retatrutide trial results that use it yet.[14] We checked all 33 retatrutide studies registered on ClinicalTrials.gov: none lists focus, cognition, mood or depression as an outcome, and only one, an 85-person phase 1 study that finished in August 2025 with no results posted, lists appetite and calorie intake.[15] The only published retatrutide memory study we found was in diabetic rats.[19]

Appetite loss can also be a side effect. In TRIUMPH-2, “decreased appetite” was logged as an adverse event in 17.1% of people on 12 mg versus 4.5% on placebo.[10] If you want the broader question of whether these drugs dull pleasure beyond food, see our GLP-1 “joy” teardown.

About “within weeks.” Trial participants started at 2 mg and stepped up every four weeks.[10] In phase 2, the 12 mg group had lost 17.5% at 24 weeks and 24.2% at 48 weeks.[2] The big headline numbers took months.

Side effects

  • Stomach problems, by far the most common. In TRIUMPH-1 at 12 mg: nausea 42.4% vs 14.8% on placebo, diarrhea 32.0% vs 13.5%, vomiting 25.3% vs 4.8%.[9]
  • Dysesthesia (tingling, burning or odd skin sensations). 12.5% on 12 mg vs 0.9% on placebo in TRIUMPH-1; 7.3% vs 0.7% in TRIUMPH-2; 6.4% vs 1.3% in TRIUMPH-3. Lilly says most cases were mild to moderate and resolved during treatment.[9][10]
  • Low blood pressure. 6% on 12 mg vs under 1% on placebo in TRIUMPH-2, worth knowing if you already take blood-pressure medicine.[6]
  • Heart rate. In phase 2, heart rate rose with dose, peaked at 24 weeks and declined after that.[2]
  • Quitting for side effects. 11.3% on 12 mg vs 4.9% on placebo in TRIUMPH-1, and 13.5% vs 4.8% in TRIUMPH-3.[9][10]
  • Heart attacks and strokes: not settled. In TRIUMPH-3, which enrolled people with heart disease, there were 27 major cardiovascular events (heart death, heart attack or stroke) on retatrutide (9 mg and 12 mg pooled) and 23 on placebo (hazard ratio 1.12, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.96). That is too few events to show benefit or harm. A dedicated outcomes trial of about 10,000 people is still running.[10][15]

Gray-market retatrutide

Retatrutide is widely sold online as a “research peptide.” The first published lab test of those products, from Australia, analyzed three vials labelled 10 mg. All three contained retatrutide, but the amounts were 5.13 mg, 16.5 mg and 19.0 mg, or 51% to 190% of the label. Heavy metals were far below safety limits, but sterility and bacterial toxins were not tested.[17] That means a dose meant to be 2 mg could really be anywhere from about 1 mg to nearly 4 mg. None of the trial numbers above apply to these products. Our powder supply-chain teardown covers who makes the raw powder.

What is not known yet

We don’t yet know what happens over years of use or after stopping (a weight-maintenance trial is underway), whether retatrutide prevents heart attacks or kidney disease, or how it compares head-to-head with tirzepatide (Zepbound) and semaglutide; those trials are listed as active but not yet reported.[15] Every phase 3 result so far comes from the same sponsor.

Frequently asked questions

Is retatrutide FDA approved?
No. Retatrutide is investigational, and Drugs@FDA lists no approved application. Lilly says it plans to submit it to the FDA in the first quarter of 2027, after finishing its manufacturing data package, and that it cannot legally be sold or marketed for human use.
When could retatrutide be available?
Lilly plans to file in Q1 2027, and approval can only come after FDA reviews that filing, so it will not be available as an approved drug in 2026. A separate dispute over whether FDA reviews it as a drug or a biologic is still unresolved.
How much weight do people lose on retatrutide?
In Lilly's 80-week TRIUMPH-1 trial in people without diabetes, the 12 mg dose produced 25.0% average weight loss counting everyone randomized (28.3% in Lilly's on-drug estimate), versus 3.9% on placebo. People with type 2 diabetes lost less: 18.8% versus 5.1% in TRIUMPH-2.
Does retatrutide stop food noise?
Lilly's phase 2 questionnaires found less hunger and less tendency to overeat on every retatrutide dose than on placebo, and most exit-interview participants said their eating changed within 8 weeks. But no trial has measured food noise itself, and none of the 33 registered retatrutide studies lists focus, cognition or mood as an outcome, so claims of sharper focus are untested.
What is dysesthesia on retatrutide?
Dysesthesia means abnormal or unpleasant skin sensations such as tingling, burning or sensitivity. In TRIUMPH-1 it was reported by 12.5% of people on 12 mg versus 0.9% on placebo. Lilly says most cases were mild to moderate and resolved during treatment.
Is gray-market retatrutide the same as the trial drug?
No. In the first published lab test of vials sold as retatrutide, three vials labelled 10 mg contained 5.1 mg, 16.5 mg and 19.0 mg. They did contain retatrutide, but the dose ranged from about half to nearly double the label, and sterility was not tested.
