GHK-Cu: what the research shows

GHK (glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine) is a tripeptide found in human plasma, saliva, and urine that binds copper. GHK-Cu is used in topical cosmetic products; there is no FDA-approved injectable drug containing it.[1][2]

What the research covers

Reviews by L. Pickart and colleagues describe effects on collagen and glycosaminoglycan synthesis, metalloproteinase activity, and wound healing in animal models, and report cosmetic study results such as improved skin firmness and reduced fine lines.[1][3] These reviews come from authors at a skin-care company’s research department, and broad claims such as regulating at least 4,000 human genes come from those reviews rather than from clinical trials.[1]

Topical versus injectable

The published human data are mostly topical and cosmetic. FDA’s compounding review is specifically about GHK-Cu for injectable routes, which it lists among substances previously placed in category 2 whose nominations were withdrawn, citing aggregation and impurity risk and stating “there are limited data in humans to inform safety-related considerations.”[4]

Human trials

As a wound drug (Iamin gel), GHK-Cu did no better than placebo in a 1992 trial in 86 people with venous leg ulcers, and its developer ProCyte reported in October 1994 that a 511-patient Phase III trial in diabetic foot ulcers showed no significant benefit. ProCyte then obtained FDA clearance for Iamin gel as an over-the-counter wound dressing in 1996, a device route based mainly on safety.[6][7] A 2006 randomized study of GHK-Cu skin products after laser resurfacing (13 completers) found no objective difference.[8] A 2016 split-face trial in 40 women, funded by the serum’s maker, found wrinkle volume fell more with a GHK-Cu nano-carrier serum than with the plain serum base (24.1% versus 15.0%).[9] For the full paper trail, see our GHK-Cu teardown.

Regulatory status

A Drugs@FDA search returns no approved drug application containing GHK-Cu.[2] A 2026 review lists GHK-Cu among peptides marketed directly to patients without approval.[5]

What is not known

There are no controlled trials of injected GHK-Cu with clinical outcomes and no established injectable dose.[4][5]
