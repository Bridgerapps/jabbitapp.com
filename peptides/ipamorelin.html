Ipamorelin: what the research shows

Ipamorelin is a five-amino-acid peptide that releases growth hormone through the ghrelin receptor. Novo Nordisk designed it in the 1990s. It is not an FDA-approved drug, its one published patient trial failed, and FDA’s compounding advisers voted against it in 2024.[1][3][8][5]

Preclinical profile

Novo Nordisk researchers described ipamorelin in 1998 as the first selective growth hormone secretagogue. In rats and pigs it released growth hormone with potency similar to GHRP-6, and in pigs it didn’t raise ACTH or cortisol beyond the levels seen with GHRH, even at doses more than 200-fold above the effective dose.[1] These are animal findings.

Human studies

Every human study gave ipamorelin by vein. In healthy men, a 15-minute infusion produced a single growth hormone peak at about 40 minutes, with a half-life of about two hours; FDA notes the lowest dose (about 3 mcg/kg) produced negligible growth hormone.[2][4] In a phase 2 trial after bowel surgery (NCT00672074), 0.03 mg/kg twice daily didn’t significantly beat placebo among 114 patients (median time to first tolerated meal 25.3 vs 32.6 hours, p = 0.15). FDA’s review notes more low potassium and high blood sugar on ipamorelin and two deaths whose relation to the drug is unclear.[3][4] A larger 320-patient dose-finding trial (NCT01280344) finished in May 2014 with no results posted and no publication we could find; FDA says development was discontinued.[7][4] FDA found no human data for injection under the skin, the way it is sold.[4]

Regulatory status

No FDA-approved drug contains ipamorelin.[8] In October 2024 FDA’s compounding advisers voted 0 to 12, with 1 abstention, against adding either form to the 503A list, citing “a lack of information supporting safety and efficacy.”[5] Ipamorelin acetate is still in category 2 under FDA’s 503B (outsourcing facility) policy, added September 29, 2023, and also appears among withdrawn nominations.[6] It is not on the agenda for FDA’s 2026–2027 peptide advisory meetings.[10] An FDA peptide compounding policy cleared White House review on October 27, 2026; the version HHS made public on October 28 lists six peptides, and ipamorelin is not among them.[9]

Update, October 29, 2026: A coroner in Victoria, Australia, found that the March 2025 death of actor Richard Norton, 75, was “triggered by his injection of the peptide CJC1295/Ipamorelin,” which “caused an anaphylactic reaction which in turn led to cardiac arrest.” The finding says he had taken more than the prescribed dose, lists asthma and a heart condition as contributing, and notes the peptides could not be detected in post-mortem testing. The coroner recommended that Australia’s TGA consider prohibiting such peptides outside clinical trials.[11] One case can’t show how often this happens; our teardown walks through the finding.

What is not known

There are no positive efficacy trials in people, no data for the under-the-skin route, no long-term safety data, and no trial of ipamorelin combined with CJC-1295.[4] For the dose comparison and the full paper trail, see our teardown.
